Why India farms by the monsoon calendar

Almost three-quarters of India's rain falls between June and September. A farmer in Latur, a rice grower in Konkan and a wheat grower in Punjab all plan their year around that one event. The result is three distinct cropping seasons – kharif, rabi and zaid – each with its own weather, water source and crops. Knowing the season tells you half the story of any crop.

13.1 Crop seasons in India

Crop season

  1. In simple wordsThe part of the year in which a crop is sown and grown.
  2. Scientific definitionA distinct period of the year, defined by temperature, rainfall and day length, during which particular crops are sown, grown and harvested.
  3. Standard definition · key factIndia has three crop seasons: kharif, rabi and summer (zaid).
  4. One-line (exam)Crop season = growing period defined by weather.

India's crop calendar follows the south-west monsoon (June–September), which brings about 75 % of the annual rainfall. The year is therefore divided into three main cropping seasons.

Maharashtra note

Maharashtra is India's leading state for rabi jowar (grown on stored moisture in the black soils of Solapur, Ahilyanagar and Marathwada) and a leading producer of soybean, cotton, tur and sugarcane. Sugarcane does not fit any one season: it is planted as adsali (July–August), pre-seasonal (October–November) or suru (January–February).

When exactly should kharif sowing start?

Not on the first shower! Agro-advisories recommend sowing only after 75–100 mm of cumulative rainfall when the soil is moist to about 15–20 cm depth. Sowing on a single early shower risks a ‘false start’ – seedlings die in the dry spell that often follows, and the farmer must re-sow.

13.2 Kharif season (खरीप हंगाम)

Kharif season

  1. In simple wordsThe rainy-season crop season – sown with the monsoon (June–July) and harvested in autumn.
  2. Scientific definitionThe crop season coinciding with the south-west monsoon, from June–July to September–October, for crops needing warm, humid weather and short days at flowering (Arabic kharif = autumn).
  3. Standard definition · key factKharif crops: rice, jowar, bajra, maize, soybean, cotton, groundnut, tur.
  4. One-line (exam)Kharif = monsoon season, June–October.

The kharif season is the rainy or monsoon season. Crops are sown with the onset of the south-west monsoon in June–July and harvested in September–October, i.e. in autumn – the Arabic word kharif means autumn. It is the largest cropping season of India: the normal kharif area is about 1,097 lakh ha (2019–20 to 2023–24 average), and in 2025–26 kharif crops covered about 1,143 lakh ha, with rice alone on about 440 lakh ha. In Maharashtra the average kharif area is about 144 lakh ha (excluding sugarcane), roughly 70 % of the state’s gross cropped area.

Kharif in Maharashtra

Soybean (about 50 lakh ha) and cotton (about 40 lakh ha) are the two largest kharif crops of the state, followed by tur, maize, rice, bajra and moong/urad. Rice and nachni dominate the high-rainfall Konkan and the eastern Vidarbha districts; soybean–cotton–tur dominate Vidarbha and Marathwada; bajra and tur dominate the scarcity zone.

Good practices for the kharif season

  1. Sow only after 75–100 mm of cumulative rain and 15–20 cm moist soil.
  2. Use broad bed furrow (BBF) or ridges and furrows to drain excess water and conserve moisture in dry spells.
  3. Choose varieties whose duration fits the rainfall period; keep a contingency crop plan ready for delayed monsoon.
  4. Control weeds in the first 30–45 days – the critical period of crop–weed competition.
  5. Follow agromet advisories (IMD, Meghdoot app) for sowing, spraying and fertiliser application.

13.3 Rabi season (रब्बी हंगाम)

Rabi season

  1. In simple wordsThe winter crop season – sown after the rains (October–November) and harvested in spring.
  2. Scientific definitionThe crop season from October–November to February–March, for crops grown on residual soil moisture or irrigation that need cool weather for growth and warm weather at maturity (Arabic rabi = spring).
  3. Standard definition · key factRabi crops: wheat, rabi jowar, gram, safflower, mustard.
  4. One-line (exam)Rabi = winter season, October–March.

The rabi season is the winter or post-monsoon season. Crops are sown in October–November, after the monsoon withdraws, and harvested in February–April, i.e. in spring – the Arabic word rabi means spring. India’s normal rabi area is about 638 lakh ha; in 2025–26 rabi crops covered about 652 lakh ha, with a record 334 lakh ha under wheat. In Maharashtra the normal rabi area is about 58 lakh ha, led by rabi jowar, gram and wheat.

Rabi in Maharashtra

Maharashtra is India’s leading rabi jowar state – about two-thirds of the state’s jowar area is rabi, grown on moisture stored in the deep black soils of Solapur, Ahilyanagar, Pune, Beed and Dharashiv. Famous varieties include Maldandi M 35-1 (released 1935) and newer ones such as Phule Revati and Phule Suchitra. Gram (chickpea), wheat, safflower and onion are the other major rabi crops.

13.4 Summer season (उन्हाळी हंगाम / zaid)

Summer (zaid) season

  1. In simple wordsThe short hot-weather crop season between rabi harvest and monsoon.
  2. Scientific definitionThe crop season from February–March to May–June, under irrigation, for short-duration, heat-tolerant crops.
  3. Standard definition · key factSummer crops: summer groundnut, moong, watermelon, cucumber, fodder crops.
  4. One-line (exam)Zaid = summer season, March–June, irrigated.

The summer or zaid season (Persian–Arabic zaid = extra, additional) is the short season between the rabi harvest and the kharif sowing. Crops are sown in February–March and harvested in May–June. They are grown only under assured irrigation. The normal summer area of India is about 75 lakh ha (2022–23 to 2024–25 average); in 2025 it reached a record 84 lakh ha, led by summer rice (about 33–36 lakh ha), pulses such as moong and urad, groundnut, sesame and maize.

Importance of the summer season

  • Raises cropping intensity – a third crop on the same land after kharif and rabi (e.g. rice–wheat–moong in the Indo-Gangetic plains).
  • Short-duration legumes (moong, urad) add nitrogen and improve soil health between two cereals.
  • Supplies green fodder in the lean summer months and vegetables and melons for the urban market.
  • Summer groundnut gives higher yield and better quality than kharif groundnut because of bright sunshine and fewer diseases.
  • Uses irrigation water and labour that would otherwise stay idle.

Watch out

Summer crops need the most water per hectare of all seasons, because evapotranspiration is highest. In water-scarce areas, choose low-water crops (moong, sesame, fodder) and use drip or sprinkler irrigation and mulching. Summer rice is discouraged in many regions because it lowers the groundwater table.

Crops that do not fit one season

Some crops occupy the field across two or three seasons. Sugarcane in Maharashtra is planted in three seasons: adsali (July–August, 16–18 months), pre-seasonal (October–November, 14–15 months) and suru (January–February, 12 months). Tur (pigeon pea), cotton (long-duration), castor, banana, turmeric and fodder crops such as lucerne also span more than one season.

13.5 Factors deciding the time of sowing

FactorHow it decides the sowing date
Onset and amount of rainKharif sowing after 75–100 mm cumulative rain and 15–20 cm moist soil
TemperatureGermination needs a minimum soil temperature; rabi wheat is sown when day temperature falls to about 25 °C (early November)
PhotoperiodShort-day crops (soybean, rice) flower early and yield less when sown late
Crop duration and next cropTimely kharif harvest allows timely rabi sowing on residual moisture
Pests and diseasesEarly-sown jowar escapes shoot fly; late-sown chickpea suffers more pod borer
Market and labourHarvest timed for better prices and labour availability

Watch out

A “false start” of the monsoon – one heavy shower followed by a 2–3 week dry spell – kills seedlings of crops sown too early. Waiting for adequate soil moisture is safer than racing to sow first.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Explain the concept of a ‘false start’ of the monsoon and how agromet advisories help farmers avoid it. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Heavy shower then dry spell; seedling death; 75–100 mm rule; IMD forecasts, Meghdoot app.

  2. Describe the kharif season with respect to period, climatic requirements and important crops grouped by type, with reference to Maharashtra. (7 marks · BTL-2 · CO2)

    Answer points: June–July to Sep–Oct; warm 25–35 °C, monsoon rain, high RH, short days, C₄ crops; cereals, millets, pulses, oilseeds, fibre, vegetables, fodder; soybean and cotton lead in Maharashtra; BBF, 75–100 mm rule.

  3. Explain the climatic requirements of rabi crops and give the recommended sowing windows of important rabi crops of Maharashtra. (7 marks · BTL-2 · CO2)

    Answer points: Oct–Nov to Feb–Apr; cool growth, warm ripening; long-day C₃ crops; stored moisture/irrigation; frost and terminal heat; rabi jowar, gram, safflower, wheat, onion sowing times.

  4. Compare kharif, rabi and zaid seasons with respect to weather, water source, crops and risks. Explain why Maharashtra leads India in rabi jowar. (8 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Comparison table; monsoon, residual moisture, irrigation; risks; deep Vertisols, stored moisture, scarcity zone.

  5. What is the summer (zaid) season? Discuss its crops, sub-seasons and importance in raising cropping intensity and farm income. (8 marks · BTL-4 · CO2)

    Answer points: Feb–Mar to May–June; hot, dry, irrigation only; moong, urad, groundnut, sesame, melons, fodder; zaid kharif and zaid rabi; third crop, legumes, fodder, market, water-saving practices.

  6. Analyse the factors that decide the time of sowing of kharif and rabi crops, and explain the consequences of early and late sowing with examples. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Rain, temperature, photoperiod, duration, pests, market; false start; late soybean, wheat terminal heat, shoot fly in jowar.

  7. Using the agro-climatic zones of Maharashtra, design a season-wise crop plan for (a) the scarcity zone and (b) eastern Vidarbha, justifying each crop. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Rainfall, soils; bajra + tur – rabi jowar/safflower; rice – linseed/lathyrus; risk management, contingency.

  8. Critically evaluate the impact of changing monsoon patterns on the traditional cropping seasons of India and suggest adaptations. (12 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO4)

    Answer points: Delayed onset, longer dry spells, intense events; shifted sowing windows, short-duration varieties, contingency plans, water harvesting.
