Q1 a) Define soil pH, electrical conductivity and cation exchange capacity, stating the unit of each.

5 marks · BL1 Remember · CO1

1. Soil pH

Soil pH is the negative logarithm (to base 10) of the hydrogen-ion activity of the soil solution. It measures soil reaction – how acidic or alkaline the soil is.

pH = − log₁₀ [H⁺] = log₁₀ (1/[H⁺])
  • Unit: pH has no unit (it is a logarithmic number on a 0–14 scale).
  • Measured with a glass-electrode pH meter in a 1 : 2.5 soil : water suspension.
  • pH 7 = neutral; < 7 acidic; > 7 alkaline. Each one-unit fall in pH means a 10-fold rise in H⁺ activity.
  • Most crops grow best at pH 6.5 – 7.5, where nutrient availability is highest.

2. Electrical conductivity (EC)

Electrical conductivity is the ability of the soil solution (or a soil–water extract) to conduct an electric current. Because current is carried by dissolved ions, EC is a direct measure of the total soluble salts in the soil.

  • Unit: deci-Siemens per metre (dS m⁻¹); 1 dS m⁻¹ = 1 mmhos cm⁻¹ = 1 mS cm⁻¹.
  • Measured with a conductivity bridge in a 1 : 2 soil : water extract, or in the saturation paste extract (ECe).
  • Approximate salt content: TDS (mg L⁻¹) ≈ 640 × EC (dS m⁻¹).
EC (1 : 2), dS m⁻¹RatingEffect on crops
< 1.0NormalSafe for all crops
1.0 – 2.0Critical for germinationSensitive seeds may fail
2.0 – 3.0Critical for salt-sensitive cropsBeans, gram, citrus affected
> 3.0InjuriousMost crops suffer; ECe > 4 = saline soil

3. Cation exchange capacity (CEC)

Cation exchange capacity is the total quantity of exchangeable cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, H⁺, Al³⁺ …) that a soil can adsorb and hold on its negatively charged colloids, per unit mass of soil, at a given pH (normally pH 7.0).

  • Unit: cmol(p⁺) kg⁻¹ (centimoles of positive charge per kg of soil) – numerically equal to the older unit meq/100 g.
  • Determined by saturating the soil with 1 N ammonium acetate (pH 7) and measuring the NH₄⁺ held.
  • CEC shows the soil’s capacity to store nutrients and its buffering power.

Q1 b) List the soil orders recognized in Soil Taxonomy and describe the characteristics of any one.

5 marks · BL2 Understand · CO1

Soil Taxonomy is the comprehensive soil classification system of the USDA (Soil Survey Staff, first published 1975; Keys to Soil Taxonomy, 13th ed., 2022). Its highest category, the order, is based on diagnostic horizons and properties that reflect the dominant soil-forming processes. Characteristics of Vertisols (black cotton soils)

Vertisols are clayey soils that shrink and crack when dry and swell when wet, so the soil “turns over” on itself (verto = to turn). They are the dominant soils of the Deccan Trap basalt in Maharashtra and cover about 8.5 % of India.

  1. High clay: ≥ 30 % clay in all horizons to at least 50 cm depth, dominated by the 2 : 1 swelling clay montmorillonite (smectite).
  2. Cracks: wide, deep cracks (≥ 0.5–1 cm wide, reaching 50 cm or more) open in the dry season; surface material falls in – self-mulching / “self-swallowing” soil.
  3. Slickensides: polished, grooved shear faces formed by swelling pressure; wedge-shaped (lenticular) peds in the subsoil.
  4. Gilgai micro-relief (small mounds and depressions) on the surface.
  5. Chemistry: high CEC (40–60 cmol kg⁻¹), high base saturation (Ca, Mg), pH 7.5–8.5, often with CaCO₃ nodules; low in N, P, Zn and organic carbon.
  6. Water: very high water-holding capacity but slow infiltration and poor drainage; sticky when wet and very hard when dry – a narrow working moisture range.
  7. Colour: dark grey to black, owing to Ti-magnetite and clay–humus complexes, not high organic matter.
  8. Crops: cotton, soybean, sorghum (rabi jowar), tur, gram, wheat, sugarcane – well-suited to rainfed rabi crops on stored moisture.

Q1 c) Classify rocks and give one example of each group.

5 marks · BL2 Understand · CO1

A rock is a naturally formed, solid aggregate of one or more minerals. Rocks are the parent material of most soils. On the basis of origin (mode of formation) rocks are classified into three groups.

1. Igneous rocks (primary rocks)

Formed by cooling and solidification of molten magma (below the surface) or lava (on the surface). They are massive, crystalline and contain no fossils. About 95 % of the Earth’s crust (to 16 km) is igneous.

2. Sedimentary rocks (secondary rocks)

Formed from sediments (weathered fragments, chemical precipitates or organic remains) deposited in layers, compacted and cemented. They are stratified (layered), may contain fossils, and cover about 75 % of the land surface though only 5 % of crust volume.

3. Metamorphic rocks

Formed when existing igneous or sedimentary rocks are changed in form, texture and mineral make-up by heat, pressure and chemically active fluids, without melting (Gk. meta = change, morph = form).

Q2 a) Furnish the volumetric composition of an ideal mineral soil in the form of a labelled diagram.

5 marks · BL2 Understand · CO1

Soil is a three-phase system – solid, liquid and gas. In an ideal (representative) surface mineral soil, such as a silt loam in good condition for plant growth, half the volume is solid and half is pore space. At optimum moisture the pore space is shared about equally between water and air.

ComponentVolume (%)PhaseRole
Mineral matter (sand, silt, clay)45SolidSkeleton of the soil; source of K, Ca, Mg, micronutrients
Organic matter (humus)5SolidImproves structure, CEC, water holding; source of N, P, S
Soil water (soil solution)25LiquidDissolves nutrients; supplies water to plants
Soil air25GasSupplies O₂ to roots and microbes; removes CO₂
Total100Solids 50 % : Pores 50 %

Key points

  • The proportions of water and air are inversely related: after rain or irrigation water fills the pores and air falls; as the soil dries, air replaces water.
  • Organic matter is only about 5 % by volume (1–6 % by weight) yet has a very large effect on soil properties.
  • Soil air contains more CO₂ (0.2–1 %) and less O₂ than the atmosphere (0.04 % CO₂, 21 % O₂).
  • Compacted soils have less than 50 % pore space; well-aggregated clay soils may have 55–60 %.

Q2 b) Illustrate (with neat sketches) any four types of soil structure.

5 marks · BL2 Understand · CO1

Soil structure is the arrangement of primary soil particles (sand, silt and clay) into secondary units called aggregates or peds, and the pore spaces between them. It is described by its type (shape), class (size) and grade (distinctness). Based on the shape and arrangement of peds, the main types are shown below (any four are enough in the exam).

Also remember

Soils without peds are structureless: single-grain (loose, e.g. sand dunes) or massive (one coherent mass, e.g. puddled paddy soil or C horizon). Organic matter, Ca²⁺, clay, roots, earthworms and microbial gums promote good aggregation; Na⁺ and excessive tillage destroy it.

Q2 c) Furnish two characteristics and one reclamation method each for saline soil and sodic soil.

5 marks · BL2 Understand · CO1

Salt-affected soils are classified on the basis of ECe (electrical conductivity of the saturation extract), ESP (exchangeable sodium percentage) and pH (US Salinity Laboratory, Richards 1954). India has about 6.73 million ha of salt-affected soils – 2.96 Mha saline and 3.77 Mha sodic (ICAR-CSSRI); Maharashtra has about 0.61 Mha, mainly in the black soils of irrigated command areas.

Dominant saltsNeutral soluble salts – chlorides and sulphates of Na, Ca, MgNa₂CO₃ and NaHCO₃; exchangeable Na⁺ on clay

Soil structureFlocculated; good permeabilityDispersed, hard, columnar subsoil; very poor permeability

Two characteristics (exam answer)

  • Saline soil: (1) ECe > 4 dS m⁻¹ with ESP < 15 and pH < 8.5; (2) white salt crust of soluble chlorides and sulphates, with flocculated, permeable soil.
  • Sodic soil: (1) ESP > 15 and pH > 8.5 with ECe < 4 dS m⁻¹; (2) dispersed clay and humus give a black, hard, impermeable soil with columnar subsoil.

Reclamation

Saline soil – leaching with drainage: the soluble salts are washed below the root zone by applying good-quality (low-EC) irrigation water in excess of crop need (leaching requirement), and removed through subsurface (tile) drainage or open drains. Supporting measures: scraping of surface salt crust, deep ploughing, organic manures, and growing salt-tolerant crops (barley, sugar beet, cotton, dhaincha).

Sodic soil – chemical amendment with gypsum: exchangeable Na⁺ must first be replaced by Ca²⁺. Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) is applied according to the gypsum requirement (typically 5–15 t ha⁻¹), mixed in the top 10–15 cm, and the soil is then ponded and leached to remove the sodium sulphate formed. Other amendments: pressmud, phosphogypsum, elemental sulphur, pyrite, FYM, and salt-tolerant crops (rice, dhaincha, karnal grass).

Na₂-clay + CaSO₄ → Ca-clay + Na₂SO₄ (leached out in drainage water)

Q3 a) Define soil colloids and list any four of their properties.

5 marks · BL1 Remember · CO1

Soil colloids are the most chemically active fraction of soil, made up of extremely fine particles of size about 1 nm to 1 µm (0.001–1 µm), which have a very large specific surface area and carry an electrical (mostly negative) charge. They remain suspended in water and cannot be seen with an ordinary microscope. They are of two kinds:

  • Inorganic (mineral) colloids – clay minerals (kaolinite, montmorillonite, illite, vermiculite) and hydrous oxides of Fe and Al.
  • Organic colloids – humus.

Properties of soil colloids (any four)

  1. Very small size: 1 nm – 1 µm; they pass through ordinary filter paper and show Brownian movement and the Tyndall effect (scatter light).
  2. Large specific surface area: montmorillonite 600–800 m² g⁻¹, humus 800–900 m² g⁻¹, kaolinite only 5–30 m² g⁻¹, compared with < 0.1 m² g⁻¹ for sand.
  3. Electrical charge: mostly negative, from isomorphous substitution (permanent charge, e.g. Mg²⁺ for Al³⁺) and from broken edges and dissociation of –OH and –COOH groups (pH-dependent charge). The negative particle with its swarm of cations is called a micelle.
  4. Adsorption and cation exchange: they adsorb cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, NH₄⁺) and exchange them with the soil solution – the basis of CEC and nutrient storage.
  5. Flocculation and dispersion: Ca²⁺ and H⁺ cause colloids to coagulate into flocs (good structure); Na⁺ causes dispersion (poor structure).
  6. Plasticity, cohesion and adhesion: wet colloids can be moulded and stick to each other and to tools.
  7. Swelling and shrinkage: 2 : 1 clays (montmorillonite) swell on wetting and shrink and crack on drying.
  8. Water adsorption: hold large amounts of water on their surfaces (hygroscopic water).

Q3 b) Classify the essential plant nutrients.

5 marks · BL2 Understand · CO1

An element is essential when it satisfies the criteria of Arnon and Stout (1939): (1) the plant cannot complete its life cycle without it; (2) its deficiency is specific and can be corrected only by supplying that element; and (3) it is directly involved in plant nutrition or metabolism. Eighteen elements are recognised as essential, classified as follows.

Elements that stimulate growth of some plants but are not essential for all: Na (C₄ plants, sugar beet), Si (rice, sugarcane), Se, V, Al (tea), I.
