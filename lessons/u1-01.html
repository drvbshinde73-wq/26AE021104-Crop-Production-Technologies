A handful of life

Pick up one handful of moist garden soil. In your palm you are holding more living organisms than there are people on Earth – bacteria, fungi, tiny insects and worms – plus crushed rock that may be thousands of years old, water, air and the remains of last season's leaves. Almost everything we eat – rice, jowar, dal, vegetables, milk, even cotton for our clothes – starts in this thin layer. In this lecture we ask a simple question with a deep answer: what exactly is soil?

1.1 What is soil?

Think of the Earth as a big ball with a hard rocky skin. On the very top of that skin, there is a thin, loose, crumbly layer in which plants grow. That loose layer is soil. It is the part of the Earth you walk on, dig in, and grow crops in.

Different books define soil in slightly different ways. All of the following are correct and you can use any of them in an exam:

  • Geological view: Soil is the outermost solid layer of the Earth, made by the breakdown (weathering) of rock.

  • Pedological view: Soil is a natural body formed from weathered rock by the action of climate and living organisms over a long time. It has layers (horizons) and keeps changing.

  • Edaphological (agricultural) view: Soil is a collection of natural bodies on the Earth’s surface made of mineral and organic matter, which is able to support plant growth.

In very simple words

Soil = broken rock + dead and decaying plant/animal material + air + water + living organisms, all mixed together in a natural way. It is “the foundation for life on land”, because almost all our food comes from it.

Explained simply

Think of soil as a kitchen where rock is slowly cooked into food for plants. Rock is the raw grain; sun, rain, heat, cold and living things are the cooks; and time is the slow fire. What comes out is a loose, layered, living material that can hold water, air and nutrients – and roots can grow through it.

A geologist, an engineer and a farmer look at the same soil differently: the geologist sees broken rock, the engineer sees a base for buildings and roads, and the farmer sees a home for crop roots. As agricultural engineers you need all three views.

Common mistake

Soil is not the same as "dirt". Dirt is soil out of place – on your shoes or clothes. Soil in a field is a natural, organised, living body with layers (horizons) and it took hundreds of years to form.

1.2 Components of soil (what is soil made of?)

A good soil is a mixture of four things. By volume, an ideal loam topsoil is about:

Component Approx. share by volume What it does
Mineral matter (sand, silt, clay, rock pieces) 45 % Skeleton of the soil; holds nutrients; gives texture
Water (soil solution) 25 % Dissolves nutrients; plants drink it from here
Air (soil air) 25 % Gives oxygen to roots and microbes; removes CO₂
Organic matter (OM) 5 % Food for microbes; builds structure; stores nutrients and water

Inside the 5 % organic matter, roughly 80 % is humus (the dark, stable, well-decomposed part), about 10 % is living roots and 10 % is soil organisms (earthworms, insects, bacteria, fungi).

Water and air share the pore space (the empty gaps). When it rains, water fills the gaps and pushes air out; when the soil dries, air returns. Water + air together are always about 50 % of the volume.

By weight, a dry soil is mostly mineral matter (about 95 %+) and organic matter is only a few percent – but that small amount matters enormously.

Explained simply – the 45-5-25-25 rule

Imagine a one-litre box filled with ideal topsoil. About 450 ml is mineral matter (sand, silt, clay), 50 ml is organic matter, and the remaining 500 ml is empty space (pores) shared by about 250 ml water and 250 ml air.

Half the soil is "nothing" – and that nothing is very important! Roots breathe the air in the pores and drink the water in the pores. A soil without pores is like a room without doors and windows.

In the field

After heavy rain the pores fill with water and air is pushed out – that is why crops like soybean turn yellow in a waterlogged field: their roots cannot breathe. When the field drains, air comes back into the pores and the crop recovers.

1.3 Chemical composition of soil

Soil is made of elements locked inside minerals and organic matter. Oxygen and silicon alone make up about three-quarters of the mineral part, because most soil minerals are silicates (silicon + oxygen compounds).

Element Typical % Where it lives in soil
Oxygen (O) 44 – 50 Oxides, silicates, water, organic matter
Silicon (Si) 20 – 33 Quartz, clay minerals (silicates)
Aluminium (Al) 7 – 15 Clay minerals, Al oxides
Iron (Fe) 3 – 6 Iron oxides and hydroxides (gives red/yellow colours)
Calcium (Ca) 1 – 5 Carbonates, silicates, exchangeable Ca²⁺
Potassium (K) 1 – 3 Feldspars, micas, exchangeable K⁺
Magnesium (Mg) 0.5 – 2 Silicates, carbonates, exchangeable Mg²⁺
Sodium (Na) 0.2 – 2 Feldspars, exchangeable Na⁺
Others (Ti, Mn, P …) < 1 each Various minerals

Composition of soil organic matter (the carbon-based part): Carbon 50–58 %, Oxygen 30–37 %, Hydrogen 5–6 %, Nitrogen 3–5 %, and small amounts of S, P and others (< 1 %). This is why we multiply organic carbon by about 1.72 to estimate organic matter (OC ÷ 0.58 = OM).

Soil is a natural mixture of four members: mineral matter, organic matter, water and air.

Mineral part = mostly oxygen + silicon + aluminium + iron. Organic part = mostly carbon + oxygen + hydrogen + nitrogen.

Explained simply

Most of the soil is made of just a few elements. Oxygen and silicon alone make up about three-quarters of the Earth's crust, because the commonest minerals (quartz and silicates) are made of them. Plant nutrients such as nitrogen, phosphorus and potassium are present in much smaller amounts – which is exactly why farmers need to add fertilizers and manures.

Remember the mineral part as O-Si-Al-Fe and the organic part as C-H-O-N.

1.4 Soil science and its branches

Soil science is the study of soil as a natural resource on the Earth’s surface, including how it forms, how it is classified and mapped, and its physical, chemical and biological properties in relation to use and management.

Branch What it studies
Soil geology (mineralogy) The rocks and minerals from which soil comes and the process of formation
Soil physics Physical laws acting in soil – texture, structure, water movement, temperature
Soil chemistry Chemical components of soil and their reactions – pH, CEC, nutrients
Soil biology / microbiology Role of plants, animals and microbes in soil
Soil fertility and nutrient management How soil supplies nutrients and how fertilizers are used

The two main divisions of soil science:

  • Pedology (Greek pedon = soil): study of soil as a natural body – its formation (genesis), classification, morphology and description. The “origin” viewpoint.

  • Edaphology (Greek edaphos = ground): study of soil in relation to plant growth, nutrition and crop yield. The “plants” viewpoint.

Pedology = soil as a natural body; Edaphology = soil for plants. This pair is asked very often.

Two ways of looking at soil

Pedology (Greek pedon = ground) studies soil as a natural body: how it formed, its layers, and how to name and map it. Edaphology (Greek edaphos = soil, floor) studies soil as a home for plants: fertility, nutrients, water and productivity.

A simple memory trick: Pedology = Pedigree (the family history of soil); Edaphology = Eating (what the plant gets from the soil).

Why engineers need soil science

Designing drip and sprinkler systems needs infiltration rate and water-holding capacity; choosing tillage tools needs soil strength and stickiness; planning drainage needs permeability. Every one of these is a soil property you will study in Unit I.

The black rock under Maharashtra

About 66 million years ago, huge cracks opened in the Earth and lava poured out again and again, layer upon layer, over a vast area of western India. That lava cooled into the dark rock called basalt – the Deccan Trap. Today most of Maharashtra stands on it, and when basalt weathers it gives us the famous black cotton soil. So the story of our soils begins with rocks and minerals.

1.5 Introduction

All soil starts as rock. Rock is made of minerals; minerals are made of elements. Water, wind, temperature and living things slowly break rock into smaller and smaller pieces (weathering). That broken material is the parent material of soil. Then plants, animals and microbes add organic matter, and over time the pile of broken rock becomes soil with layers. So the chain is:

Elements➜Minerals➜Rocks➜Weathering➜Parent material➜Soil

Explained simply – the building chain

Think of a building: elements are bricks' raw clay, minerals are the bricks, and rocks are the walls. When the wall slowly breaks down (weathering), the broken pieces become the parent material, and living things then turn that material into soil.

1.6 What is a rock?

A rock is a hard mass of mineral matter made of one or more rock-forming minerals. Rocks form the solid crust of the Earth. The study of rocks is called petrology (Greek petra = rock, logos = science), and the study of how rocks originate is petrogenesis.

Inside the Earth the temperature is very high. In some places rocks of the upper mantle and lower crust partially melt to form a hot liquid called magma. Melting happens when (i) temperature rises, (ii) pressure falls (rising rock decompresses), or (iii) water/gases are added, which lowers the melting point. When magma reaches the surface it is called lava.

Rock vs mineral – the chocolate test

A mineral is like plain chocolate: one substance with a fixed recipe (chemical formula) and a regular crystal shape. A rock is like a fruit-and-nut bar: a mixture of several minerals stuck together. Granite, for example, is a "bar" of quartz, feldspar and mica.

1.7 The three families of rocks

Feature Igneous (primary) Sedimentary (secondary) Metamorphic
How formed Cooling and hardening of magma / lava Deposition, compaction and cementation of sediments Existing rocks changed by heat, pressure and chemically active fluids
Texture Crystalline, no layers Layered (stratified), visible grains Crystalline, often banded or foliated
Share of crust About 95 % of crust is igneous + metamorphosed igneous Covers most of the surface as a thin layer Formed deep, shows up after uplift
Examples Granite, basalt, gabbro, obsidian Sandstone, shale, limestone, conglomerate Gneiss, schist, slate, marble, quartzite

How to remember the three families

Igneous = born of fire (Latin ignis): melted rock (magma or lava) cooled and hardened. Sedimentary = settled layers: bits of older rock carried by water or wind, settled, pressed and cemented. Metamorphic = changed form (Greek meta = change, morph = shape): existing rock changed by great heat and pressure without melting.

Cooling speed decides crystal size: lava that cools fast on the surface (basalt) has tiny crystals; magma that cools slowly deep underground (granite) has large, visible crystals.

Rocks of Maharashtra

Basalt (igneous) covers most of the state and gives black soils. In the Konkan, basalt weathered under heavy rain gives red-brown laterite, cut into blocks for houses. Parts of eastern Vidarbha have older granite, gneiss and sandstone, which give lighter red and yellow soils.

Common mistake

Students often say "sedimentary rocks form from lava" – no! Lava forms igneous rock. Sedimentary rocks form from sediments (sand, mud, shells) deposited mainly under water.

A. Igneous rocks (primary or massive rocks)

They are the first rocks formed on Earth when molten magma solidified. Based on where the magma cools, they are of two main kinds:

Extrusive (volcanic) Intrusive (plutonic)
Where cooled On or near the surface (lava) Deep inside the crust
Cooling speed Fast Very slow
Crystal size Fine-grained / glassy / bubbly (vesicular) Coarse-grained, large visible crystals
Examples Basalt, rhyolite, andesite, obsidian, pumice, scoria Granite, diorite, gabbro, pegmatite, syenite

  • Why large crystals in granite? Because magma cooled slowly underground, so atoms had time to arrange into big crystals. Lava cools quickly, so crystals stay tiny.

  • Vesicular rocks (pumice, scoria) have holes because gas/steam bubbles were trapped as lava cooled on the surface.

  • Dykes are intrusive rocks formed in vertical cracks; sills form in horizontal cracks. Plutonic = solidified at great depth; hypabyssal = at shallow depth.

  • Basalt is the most important rock for India’s Deccan plateau, and the parent of Maharashtra’s black soil.

B. Sedimentary rocks (secondary rocks)

When rocks weather, the broken pieces are carried by water, wind or ice and dropped (deposited) somewhere, often in layers. Over millions of years these layers are pressed and cemented together by materials such as silica (SiO₂), iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) or lime, forming sedimentary rocks. They are also called clastic rocks (made of fragments).

Classified by Types Examples
Origin – residual Left in place after weathering Laterite
Origin – transported (suspension) Carried as solids and settled Sandstone, shale
Origin – chemical precipitation Dissolved minerals crystallise when water evaporates Limestone, ironstone, rock salt, gypsum
Origin – organic From plant/animal remains Peat, coal, phosphatic deposits
Grain size – rudaceous Boulder/pebble size Conglomerate
Grain size – arenaceous Sand size Sandstone
Grain size – silty Silt size Siltstone
Grain size – argillaceous Clay size Shale

C. Metamorphic rocks

Meta = change, morph = form. When igneous or sedimentary rocks are buried deep or squeezed by mountain-building, the heat, pressure and hot fluids change their minerals or texture without fully melting them. Change by water/fluids is hydro-metamorphism; change by pressure is dynamo-metamorphism.

Original rock Becomes
Sandstone Quartzite

1.8 Minerals

A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and an orderly crystal structure. Rocks are collections of minerals (like a cake made of different ingredients). Minerals are broadly classified as metallic (ferrous, non-ferrous, precious), non-metallic (mica, salt, potash, sulphur, limestone) and energy minerals (coal, petroleum).

Primary minerals are those that came directly from cooled magma and have not been chemically changed (quartz, feldspar, mica, hornblende, augite, olivine). Secondary minerals are new minerals formed from the breakdown of primary ones (clay minerals like kaolinite, iron oxides like hematite, carbonates like calcite). Most nutrients are released from primary minerals when they weather.

Relative abundance of minerals in the crust (approx.): plagioclase feldspars 39 %, K-feldspars 12 %, quartz 12 %, pyroxenes 11 %, micas 5 %, amphiboles 5 %, clays 5 %, other silicates 3 %, non-silicates 8 %. This is why feldspars dominate and why silicon is so common in soil.

Mineral group Meaning Examples with formula Soil importance
Silicates Contain Si and O with other elements. Most abundant group. Quartz SiO₂; Feldspar KAlSi₃O₈; Muscovite mica KAl₂(AlSi₃O₁₀)(OH)₂; Hornblende; Augite (pyroxene); Olivine (Mg,Fe)₂SiO₄; Kaolinite Al₂Si₂O₅(OH)₄ Parent of clays; source of K, Ca, Mg, Na, Fe
Carbonates Contain the carbonate ion CO₃²⁻ Calcite CaCO₃; Dolomite CaMg(CO₃)₂; Magnesite MgCO₃; Siderite FeCO₃ Source of Ca and Mg; cause of alkaline/calcareous soils; limestone
Oxides Oxygen + metals Hematite Fe₂O₃; Magnetite Fe₃O₄; Corundum Al₂O₃; Rutile TiO₂ Give red / black colour; anion exchange; P fixation
Sulphides Sulphur + metals Pyrite FeS₂; Galena PbS; Sphalerite ZnS; Chalcopyrite CuFeS₂ Weathering of pyrite gives sulphuric acid (acid sulphate soils)

Silicate groups by structure: quartz and feldspar = tectosilicates; micas, kaolinite = phyllosilicates (sheet-like); amphibole = double-chain; pyroxene = single-chain; olivine = isolated tetrahedra.

Ferromagnesian minerals (olivine, pyroxene, hornblende, biotite) contain Fe and Mg; they weather faster than quartz.

Why some minerals survive and others vanish

Minerals that crystallised first from very hot magma (olivine, pyroxene) are the least comfortable at the Earth's cool, wet surface, so they weather fastest. Quartz crystallised last at the lowest temperature and is the most stable – that is why river sand and beach sand are mostly quartz: everything else has already weathered away.

Primary minerals come straight from the cooling magma; secondary minerals (clays, oxides) are "new" minerals made from the broken pieces during weathering. Clay is therefore the child of feldspar and mica.

Try it yourself

Jar test at home: take a glass jar, fill one-third with garden soil and two-thirds with water, add a pinch of salt or detergent, shake hard for 2 minutes and leave it undisturbed for a day. You will see layers settle: sand at the bottom (within a minute), silt above it (within an hour) and clay on top (after many hours), with bits of organic matter floating. You have just separated the mineral and organic parts of soil!

Rock walk: collect three stones near your college or home. Look with a hand lens: can you see separate crystals (likely igneous like granite), layers or sand grains (sedimentary), or bands and shiny flakes (metamorphic)? Scratch with an iron nail – quartz will not scratch, calcite will. Put a drop of dilute acid (lemon juice or vinegar) – fizzing means calcium carbonate (limestone).

    Answer points: Three definitions with authorities; comparison; edaphological view links soil to plant growth; engineering use; conclusion.

  2. Explain the volume composition of an ideal loam soil with a diagram and analyse how a change in water content alters soil aeration and plant growth. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Mineral 45 %, OM 5 %, water 25 %, air 25 %; pore space 50 %; water–air inverse relation; waterlogging and drought effects; diagram.

  3. Discuss the elemental composition of the Earth's crust and soil organic matter, and justify the use of the factor 1.724 to convert organic carbon to organic matter. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO1)

    Answer points: O, Si, Al, Fe, Ca, Na, K, Mg percentages; OM composition C 58 %; 100/58 = 1.724; implications.

  4. Differentiate pedology and edaphology and describe the branches of soil science with their applications in crop production. (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO1)

    Answer points: Definitions; branches – soil physics, chemistry, biology, fertility, genesis, survey, conservation; applications.

  5. Explain why soil is described as the meeting place of the lithosphere, hydrosphere, atmosphere and biosphere. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO1)

    Answer points: Pedosphere concept; contributions of each sphere; interactions; diagram.

  6. Describe the internal structure of the Earth and analyse why only the crust is directly relevant to soil formation. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Crust, mantle, outer and inner core; discontinuities; depths and states; crust as parent material; thin soil skin.

  7. Explain the rock cycle with a neat diagram and evaluate its significance for the formation and renewal of soils. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO1)

    Answer points: Igneous, sedimentary, metamorphic transformations; processes; diagram; soil as part of the cycle.

  8. Compare igneous, sedimentary and metamorphic rocks with respect to origin, texture, composition and the soils they produce in Maharashtra. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Table; Deccan basalt → black soil; sandstone, limestone, gneiss soils; examples.

  9. Classify soil-forming minerals into primary and secondary minerals and explain their role as sources of plant nutrients. (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO1)

    Answer points: Definitions; quartz, feldspar, mica, ferromagnesian; clay minerals, oxides, carbonates; nutrients released; weathering sequence.

  10. Explain why soils developed on basalt differ from those developed on granite in texture, fertility and water behaviour. (5 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Mineral composition; weathering products; clay type; base content; examples.
