10.1 What is soil reaction?

Soil reaction is the degree of acidity or alkalinity of a soil. It is measured as the pH of a soil–water suspension. It is considered the single most important chemical property of soil because it controls nutrient supply, toxicity and microbial activity.

Acidic Neutral Alkaline
pH Below 7 About 7 Above 7
H⁺ vs OH⁻ H⁺ > OH⁻ H⁺ = OH⁻ OH⁻ > H⁺
Where Humid, high-rainfall regions — Arid and semi-arid regions
Why Rain leaches Ca, Mg, K, Na; H⁺ takes over exchange sites Balanced Bases (Ca, Mg, Na) accumulate; Na₂CO₃ hydrolyses giving OH⁻, pH may reach 9–10

10.2 pH is a logarithmic scale

pH = −log₁₀ [H⁺] (Sørensen), where [H⁺] is in mol/L (or g/L). At neutrality [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷, so pH = 7. Water: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴, so pH + pOH = 14.

  • Each pH unit = 10-fold change in H⁺. pH 6 has 10 × more H⁺ than pH 7; pH 5 has 100 ×; pH 4 has 1000 ×.

  • Example: [H⁺] = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ → pH = 6 − log 4.2 = 6 − 0.62 = 5.38.

10.3 Sources of soil acidity

  • Natural processes: decomposition of organic matter forms organic acids; root and microbial respiration produce CO₂ → carbonic acid; nitrification of ammonium releases H⁺: NH₄⁺ + 2O₂ → NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + H₂O.

  • Acidic parent material: granite, sandstone, shale – low in bases; highly weathered tropical soils.

  • Acid rain/atmospheric deposition: SO₂ and NOₓ form sulphuric and nitric acids.

  • Leaching of basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na) by heavy rain – leaves H⁺ and Al³⁺ behind.

  • Acid-forming fertilizers: urea, ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate: (NH₂)₂CO + 4O₂ → 2NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + CO₂ + H₂O (ultimately).

  • Crop removal of bases; continuous cropping without returning OM.

10.4 Sources of soil alkalinity

  • Parent material: limestone, dolomite, calcite, Ca–Mg-rich minerals release bases and carbonates.

  • Accumulation of carbonates/bicarbonates: CaCO₃, MgCO₃, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃ build up when leaching is limited (arid and semi-arid).

  • Poor-quality irrigation water high in Na⁺, CO₃²⁻, HCO₃⁻.

  • Evaporation and capillary rise bring alkaline groundwater and salts to the surface.

  • Excess sodium inputs: sodium nitrate, industrial effluents, fly ash.

  • Inadequate leaching/poor drainage and waterlogging.

10.5 Three kinds of acidity

Kind What it is How big
Active H⁺ ions in the soil solution – what roots and microbes feel; measured as soil pH Tiny – at pH 4 only about 2 kg CaCO₃/ha needed to neutralise (furrow slice)
Exchangeable H⁺ and Al³⁺ adsorbed on colloids; released by an unbuffered salt such as KCl About 100 × the active acidity
Residual H and Al tightly bound to organic matter and clay (can be released only slowly or by lime) Largest: 1,000 × (sandy) to 100,000 × (clayey) the active acidity; lime need 4–8 t/ha

The three are in equilibrium: when lime neutralises active acidity, exchangeable and residual acidity release more H⁺ to replace it. That is why large amounts of lime are needed.

Active acidity is the tip of an iceberg; most of the acidity is hidden below the “water” (on and inside colloids).

10.6 Buffering capacity

Buffering capacity is the soil’s ability to resist a change in pH when acid or base is added. Add the same amount of HCl to pure water (pH 7 → ~4) and to a soil suspension (pH 7 → ~6.5–6.8): the soil changes much less because colloids absorb H⁺ by exchange. Buffering is higher with more clay, more clay colloids/organic matter and higher CEC. Sandy, low-clay soils are weakly buffered and change pH quickly. Importance: stabilises pH, moderates sudden changes in nutrient availability and protects roots from pH stress.

10.7 Factors controlling soil reaction

Factor Effect
Rainfall and climate High rain leaches bases → acid; low rain → alkaline
Parent material Basic rocks (basalt, limestone) give higher pH; acid rocks (granite) give lower pH
Soil colloids H⁺ as main exchangeable cation makes soil acid; Na/Ca as main cations make it alkaline
Soil and root processes Root respiration, CO₂ in soil air, organic acids lower pH
Crops and cultivation Crop removal of bases and legume H⁺ release acidify
Nitrogen fertilizer Ammonium-type N fertilizer speeds acidification
Moisture and CO₂ Drier soil – higher pH; high CO₂ in soil air lowers pH
Flooding Pushes most soils to pH 6.5–7.2 within a month

10.8 Significance of soil pH

  • Overall: most nutrients are most available at pH 6.0–7.5 (the “sweet spot”).

  • Nitrogen: maximum availability at pH 6–8, because that is the best range for microbes that mineralise N and fix it symbiotically.

  • Phosphorus: maximum at pH 6.5–7.5. Below 6.5, Fe and Al in solution form insoluble Fe/Al phosphates; above ~7.5–8, Ca phosphates precipitate. High P availability again at pH > 8.5 as soluble sodium phosphates.

  • Iron and manganese: availability increases with acidity (more soluble) – can become toxic below pH 5.5; become deficient at high pH.

  • Zn, Cu, B: most available in slightly acidic to neutral soils; deficiency in alkaline/calcareous soils. Molybdenum: opposite – more available at higher pH.

  • Ca, Mg, K: low in strongly acid soils (leached); adequate above pH 6.

  • Aluminium: toxic below pH 5.0–5.5; roots are damaged.

  • Microorganisms: most work best at pH 6.0–7.5; nitrifying bacteria are inhibited below ~5.5 and above ~9; fungi tolerate acid soils.

  • Earthworms are inhibited by high acidity.

Acidic soils: apply lime (CaCO₃, Ca(OH)₂, dolomite). Alkaline soils: apply gypsum, elemental sulphur, organic matter; grow tolerant crops.

Ideal pH for most crops: 6.3–6.8 (range 6.0–7.5).
