When good land turns white

Drive through some canal-irrigated villages of western Maharashtra and you may see patches of land with a white crust shining in the sun, or black, hard, barren patches where nothing grows. Many of these fields once gave good sugarcane. Too much irrigation and poor drainage brought salts to the surface. Such problematic soils cover millions of hectares in India – but the good news is that most of them can be reclaimed with the right science.

10.1 What are problematic soils?

Problematic soils are soils whose physical, chemical or biological constraints limit crop growth and productivity. They need special management or reclamation before good crops can be grown. Main types in India: saline, sodic (alkali), saline-sodic, acid, acid sulphate (kari), calcareous, submerged/waterlogged and degraded alkali soils.

Salt-affected soils occur in four main tracts: the semi-arid Indo-Gangetic alluvial plains (Punjab, Haryana, western UP), arid Rajasthan and Gujarat, arid and semi-arid central and southern states (Maharashtra, Madhya Pradesh, Karnataka, Telangana), and the coastal alluvial areas. In Maharashtra about 0.6 million ha is salt-affected; most is sodic/alkali (about 70 %), then saline (~29 %); districts like Jalna, Parbhani, Buldhana, Nanded and Akola are most affected.

Explained simply

A problematic soil is a soil that cannot give normal crop yields because of some chemical or physical defect – too much salt, too much sodium, too much acidity, too much water, too much lime – unless we correct it. Think of a patient: we must first diagnose (soil test: pH, EC, ESP) and then give the right treatment (gypsum, lime, drainage, leaching).

Saline = too much salt (plants cannot drink: osmotic problem).

Sodic = too much sodium on clay (soil becomes like cement when dry and like paste when wet).

Saline-sodic = both.

Saline vs sodic – white alkali vs black alkali

Saline soil has too much dissolved salt. The salt makes it hard for roots to suck up water (osmotic stress) – plants wilt even in wet soil, like a thirsty person who cannot drink sea water. The surface shows a white crust; the soil structure is still good.

Sodic soil has too much sodium stuck on the clay (ESP > 15). Sodium disperses the clay, so the soil becomes like paste when wet and like cement when dry; water cannot enter. Dissolved organic matter gives a dark colour – "black alkali".

In Maharashtra

Salt-affected soils are found in canal command areas such as the Nira, Pravara and Krishna valleys, in parts of Sangli, Satara, Pune, Solapur and Ahilyanagar districts where sugarcane is heavily irrigated, and along the coastal Konkan (khar lands) where sea water enters.

Formulas to remember

  • ESP = (Exchangeable Na ÷ CEC) × 100.

  • SAR = Na ÷ √[(Ca + Mg)/2] (concentrations in meq/L) – sodium hazard of irrigation water.

  • RSC = (CO₃ + HCO₃) − (Ca + Mg) in meq/L – residual sodium carbonate; > 2.5 meq/L unsafe.

Reclamation of saline soils

Principle: remove the excess salts from the root zone by leaching with good-quality water and provide drainage.

  • Cultural and field practices: scrape off surface salt crust; land levelling for uniform water distribution; improve infiltration and drainage; do not reuse saline drainage water.

  • Leaching: apply extra good-quality water so salts move below the root zone; works only if drainage (surface or subsurface/tile) is good.

  • Crop selection: salt-tolerant crops – barley, wheat, sorghum, cotton, sugar beet; plant at favourable times.

  • Biological: green manures (dhaincha, sesbania, sunhemp), FYM, compost, crop residues, mulching to reduce evaporation.

  • Irrigation: good-quality water, appropriate frequency, drip/sprinkler or basin, avoid secondary salinisation.

The logic of reclamation

Salt dissolves in water, so the cure for salinity is to wash it down below the root zone and remove it through drains. Without drainage, the salt simply comes back up when water evaporates. In short: leach + drain + manage irrigation.

Reclamation of sodic soils

Principle: replace exchangeable Na⁺ with Ca²⁺ and leach the displaced sodium out of the root zone.

Gypsum reaction

2Na–Soil + CaSO₄ → Ca–Soil + Na₂SO₄ (soluble, leached out)

Gypsum (CaSO₄·2H₂O) is cheap, slightly soluble, and the most common amendment. Amount (gypsum requirement) depends on ESP, CEC, texture, depth and pH – decided by soil testing.

  • Chemical amendments: gypsum (most common), pyrite, elemental sulphur or sulphuric acid (for calcareous soils containing free CaCO₃, which produce Ca after acidification), calcium chloride, press mud.

  • Physical: land levelling, deep ploughing/subsoiling to break hard layers, surface scraping and flushing, improving infiltration.

  • Biological: FYM, compost, green manure (Sesbania aculeata, dhaincha), afforestation – organic matter releases CO₂ and organic acids that dissolve insoluble Ca compounds.

  • Crop selection: tolerant – rice, sorghum, barley; sensitive – onion, carrot, citrus, beans.

  • Irrigation/drainage: good quality (low-SAR, low-EC) water, adequate drainage.

Why leaching alone fails on sodic soil

In sodic soil the sodium is held on the clay, not floating freely, and the soil will not even let water pass. So we must first replace sodium with calcium using gypsum (CaSO₄). Calcium takes the sodium's place on the clay, the clay flocculates, pores open, and the released sodium sulphate is then leached away. Gypsum first, then water.

Saline-sodic soils

Replace Na⁺ with Ca²⁺ (gypsum) first or together with leaching. Leaching without a Ca source would remove salts but leave sodium-dominated, dispersed soil. If the soil is calcareous, sulphur or sulphuric acid can be used. Always use good-quality water and ensure drainage.

10.3 Acid soils

Soils with pH < 7 (practically problem when pH < 5.5–6). In India roughly 92–99 million ha (28–31 % of the area) have pH ≤ 6.5, in high-rainfall regions of north-east India, Kerala, Odisha, Jharkhand and the Western Ghats.

  • Characteristics: high soluble Al, Fe, Mn (toxicity); deficiency of Ca, Mg, P, Mo, B; low base saturation; low microbial activity; strong P fixation by Fe/Al.

  • Cause: leaching of bases by heavy rain, acid parent material, organic acids, acid-forming fertilizers, crop removal.

  • Main limitation: aluminium toxicity and nutrient deficiency.

Explained simply

In very acid soils, aluminium dissolves and becomes toxic – it stunts roots so they look short and stubby. Phosphorus gets locked by iron and aluminium, and calcium and molybdenum become deficient, so legumes nodulate poorly. Lime (CaCO₃) neutralises the acidity and supplies calcium. A little lime in the furrow every year is often more economical than a big dose once.

In Maharashtra

Acid lateritic soils of the Konkan (Ratnagiri, Sindhudurg, parts of Raigad) respond well to liming for groundnut and pulses; rice and cashew tolerate acidity.

Reclamation of acid soils

  • Liming – the main method. Materials: burnt lime (CaO), hydrated lime (Ca(OH)₂), calcite/limestone (CaCO₃), dolomite (CaCO₃·MgCO₃), basic slag (CaSiO₃). Lime supplies Ca²⁺ (and Mg²⁺), neutralises H⁺ and precipitates Al³⁺. Reaction: Soil–H₂ + CaCO₃ → Soil–Ca + H₂O + CO₂. Rate depends on soil pH, texture, buffering and crop (lime requirement), commonly applied once in 3–5 years; mix well into the soil.

  • Cultural practices: deep ploughing, subsoiling, land levelling, organic manures, split N application.

  • Tolerant crops: rice, millets, oats, groundnut, tea, potato, pineapple, in initial years.

  • Fertility management: use non-acid-forming fertilizers (SSP, TSP, DAP, calcium ammonium nitrate); prefer K₂SO₄/KCl as per need; add FYM, compost, green manure to buffer and complex Al.

  • Irrigation: good-quality water, frequent light irrigation to leach Al/Fe, drainage.

10.4 Acid sulphate soils (kari soils)

  • Occur in waterlogged humid coastal and estuarine areas with sulphide-rich (pyrite, FeS₂) materials: Kuttanad (Kerala – “kari” soils), coastal Odisha, Sundarbans (West Bengal).

  • When drained or excavated, pyrite oxidises to sulphuric acid: FeS₂ + O₂ + H₂O → H₂SO₄ (+ Fe compounds); pH falls to < 3.5 (extreme 2–3), causing Fe and Al toxicity and nutrient imbalance.

  • Management: keep the sulphidic layer saturated with water; avoid deep drainage and prolonged drying; control water table; use tolerant rice varieties (Pokkali in Kerala); apply lime (only per soil test); flush acidity and soluble Fe/Al with good-quality water where drainage permits.

A soil that becomes acid when it dries

These soils form in coastal swamps rich in pyrite (iron sulphide). While waterlogged they are harmless, but when drained the pyrite oxidises to sulphuric acid and pH can fall below 3.5. So the best management is often to keep them flooded and grow rice. The famous example is the Kari soils of Kuttanad, Kerala.

10.5 Calcareous soils

  • Found in arid and semi-arid areas with abundant CaCO₃ in the parent material and a calcic horizon.

  • pH alkaline: 7.0–8.5 (CaCO₃ precipitates near pH 8.5); free CaCO₃ shows effervescence with dilute HCl.

  • Low in organic matter and available N; deficient in P, Fe, Zn, Mn, Cu, B and Mo (fixation at high pH).

  • Management: organic manures, green manuring, gypsum/sulphur for pH, foliar micronutrients, P placement with FYM, acid-forming fertilizers (ammonium sulphate), irrigation scheduling.

Too much lime is also a problem

Calcareous soils contain free calcium carbonate – they fizz when a drop of dilute acid is added. Their pH is high (7.5–8.5), so phosphorus combines with calcium and iron, zinc and manganese become unavailable. Crops such as groundnut, sugarcane and citrus show yellow young leaves (iron chlorosis). Use organic manures, acid-forming fertilizers, sulphur and foliar sprays of micronutrients.

10.6 Submerged soils and waterlogged soils

  • Develop under prolonged or periodic water saturation – rice paddies, floodplains, wetlands, depressions.

  • Water fills pores, oxygen diffusion is blocked, soil becomes reduced: low redox potential (Eh), grey/bluish-grey colour (gleying), Fe³⁺ → Fe²⁺ and Mn⁴⁺ → Mn²⁺ (can reach toxic level).

  • Soil pH moves towards neutral (6.5–7.2) – acid soils rise, alkaline soils fall.

  • Organic matter breaks down anaerobically, giving methane (CH₄) and H₂S; nitrate is lost by denitrification.

  • Uses/management: rice and wetland crops; drainage and raised beds for upland crops; use ammoniacal N placed in the reduced zone; avoid nitrate fertilizers.

A soil that cannot breathe

When soil is flooded, oxygen is used up within a day or two and microbes start using other substances (nitrate, manganese, iron, sulphate) to breathe. Iron turns grey-green, toxic gases such as H₂S may form, and most crops suffer. Rice is the exception – it has air channels (aerenchyma) that carry oxygen to its roots. Drainage is the cure for unwanted waterlogging.

10.7 Degraded alkali soils

  • Occur in irrigated arid/semi-arid regions with poor drainage and high evaporation, in fine-textured soils; long-term irrigation with sodium-rich water.

  • ESP > 15, pH > 8.5 in the sodic subsoil; surface horizon may be neutral/acidic due to leaching of bases; clay dispersion, surface crusting, low infiltration, hard-setting.

  • Process: Clay–Na + H₂O ⇌ Clay–H + Na⁺ + OH⁻ and 2NaOH + CO₂ → Na₂CO₃ + H₂O.

  • Reclaimed like sodic soils: gypsum, organic matter, deep tillage, good-quality water, drainage.

Explained simply

When a sodic soil is leached without enough calcium, the surface loses its sodium and becomes slightly acidic, but the subsoil remains sodic and very hard (columnar structure). It has the problems of both – poor infiltration and crusting – and needs gypsum, organic matter and deep tillage to break the hard layer.

Play with it

Try it yourself

Salt-crust experiment: fill a small tray with soil, wet it with salty water (one spoon salt in a glass) and keep it in the sun. As water evaporates you will see a white crust form on the surface – that is how saline soils develop in the field. Now pour plenty of fresh water and let it drain through holes in the tray: the crust disappears – leaching and drainage!

Facts & amazing information

1

Salt-affected soils cover several million hectares in India, mostly in canal-irrigated and coastal areas.

2

The Central Soil Salinity Research Institute (CSSRI) at Karnal, Haryana, works on reclaiming saline and sodic soils.

3

The white crust of saline soil is called 'white alkali'; the dark, dispersed sodic soil is 'black alkali'.

4

Gypsum reclaims sodic soil because its calcium replaces sodium on clay – sodium sulphate then washes away.

5

Acid sulphate (kari) soils become strongly acidic when drained because pyrite oxidises to sulphuric acid.

6

Over-irrigation of sugarcane without drainage is a leading cause of salinity in parts of western Maharashtra.

    Answer points: Areas; regions; productivity loss; programmes.
