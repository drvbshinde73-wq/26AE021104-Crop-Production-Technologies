One number that controls everything

A farmer applies the same fertilizer on two fields. One field gives a bumper crop; the other shows yellow leaves and poor growth. The soil test reveals the reason: pH 6.8 in the first field and 8.9 in the second. Soil reaction (pH) is like the master switch of the soil – it decides which nutrients are available, which are locked up, which microbes are active and even which crops can grow at all.

10.1 What is soil reaction?

Soil reaction is the degree of acidity or alkalinity of a soil. It is measured as the pH of a soil–water suspension. It is considered the single most important chemical property of soil because it controls nutrient supply, toxicity and microbial activity.

Acidic Neutral Alkaline
pH Below 7 About 7 Above 7
H⁺ vs OH⁻ H⁺ > OH⁻ H⁺ = OH⁻ OH⁻ > H⁺
Where Humid, high-rainfall regions — Arid and semi-arid regions
Why Rain leaches Ca, Mg, K, Na; H⁺ takes over exchange sites Balanced Bases (Ca, Mg, Na) accumulate; Na₂CO₃ hydrolyses giving OH⁻, pH may reach 9–10

Explained simply

Soil reaction simply means how acidic or alkaline the soil is. It depends on the balance between hydrogen ions (H⁺, which make soil acidic) and hydroxyl ions (OH⁻, which make it alkaline). When they are equal, the soil is neutral (pH 7).

10.2 pH is a logarithmic scale

pH = −log₁₀ [H⁺] (Sørensen), where [H⁺] is in mol/L (or g/L). At neutrality [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷, so pH = 7. Water: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴, so pH + pOH = 14.

  • Each pH unit = 10-fold change in H⁺. pH 6 has 10 × more H⁺ than pH 7; pH 5 has 100 ×; pH 4 has 1000 ×.

  • Example: [H⁺] = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ → pH = 6 − log 4.2 = 6 − 0.62 = 5.38.

One step = ten times

The pH scale is logarithmic: each one-unit drop means 10 times more acidity. So a soil of pH 5 is 10 times more acidic than pH 6, and 100 times more acidic than pH 7. This is why changing pH by even one unit needs a lot of lime and is a big deal for crops.

Like the Richter scale for earthquakes, a small change in the number means a big change in the real strength.

Common mistake

pH 4 is not "twice as acidic" as pH 8, and pH 6 is not "slightly" more acidic than pH 7 – it is ten times more. Always think in powers of ten.

10.3 Sources of soil acidity

  • Natural processes: decomposition of organic matter forms organic acids; root and microbial respiration produce CO₂ → carbonic acid; nitrification of ammonium releases H⁺: NH₄⁺ + 2O₂ → NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + H₂O.

  • Acidic parent material: granite, sandstone, shale – low in bases; highly weathered tropical soils.

  • Acid rain/atmospheric deposition: SO₂ and NOₓ form sulphuric and nitric acids.

  • Leaching of basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na) by heavy rain – leaves H⁺ and Al³⁺ behind.

  • Acid-forming fertilizers: urea, ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate: (NH₂)₂CO + 4O₂ → 2NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + CO₂ + H₂O (ultimately).

  • Crop removal of bases; continuous cropping without returning OM.

How soils become acidic

Heavy rain is the main culprit: it washes out basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na) year after year, and H⁺ and Al³⁺ take their place. Other sources: CO₂ from roots and microbes forming carbonic acid, organic acids from decaying matter, acid-forming fertilizers (ammonium sulphate, urea through nitrification) and acid rain. That is why the high-rainfall Konkan has acid soils while the dry Deccan does not.

10.4 Sources of soil alkalinity

  • Parent material: limestone, dolomite, calcite, Ca–Mg-rich minerals release bases and carbonates.

  • Accumulation of carbonates/bicarbonates: CaCO₃, MgCO₃, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃ build up when leaching is limited (arid and semi-arid).

  • Poor-quality irrigation water high in Na⁺, CO₃²⁻, HCO₃⁻.

  • Evaporation and capillary rise bring alkaline groundwater and salts to the surface.

  • Excess sodium inputs: sodium nitrate, industrial effluents, fly ash.

  • Inadequate leaching/poor drainage and waterlogging.

How soils become alkaline

In low-rainfall areas the bases are not washed away; instead, water evaporates and leaves calcium carbonate, sodium carbonate and other salts behind. Irrigation with water rich in bicarbonate or sodium, and poor drainage, make the problem worse – common in canal-irrigated parts of western Maharashtra.

10.5 Three kinds of acidity

Kind What it is How big
Active H⁺ ions in the soil solution – what roots and microbes feel; measured as soil pH Tiny – at pH 4 only about 2 kg CaCO₃/ha needed to neutralise (furrow slice)
Exchangeable H⁺ and Al³⁺ adsorbed on colloids; released by an unbuffered salt such as KCl About 100 × the active acidity
Residual H and Al tightly bound to organic matter and clay (can be released only slowly or by lime) Largest: 1,000 × (sandy) to 100,000 × (clayey) the active acidity; lime need 4–8 t/ha

The three are in equilibrium: when lime neutralises active acidity, exchangeable and residual acidity release more H⁺ to replace it. That is why large amounts of lime are needed.

Active acidity is the tip of an iceberg; most of the acidity is hidden below the “water” (on and inside colloids).

10.6 Buffering capacity

Buffering capacity is the soil’s ability to resist a change in pH when acid or base is added. Add the same amount of HCl to pure water (pH 7 → ~4) and to a soil suspension (pH 7 → ~6.5–6.8): the soil changes much less because colloids absorb H⁺ by exchange. Buffering is higher with more clay, more clay colloids/organic matter and higher CEC. Sandy, low-clay soils are weakly buffered and change pH quickly. Importance: stabilises pH, moderates sudden changes in nutrient availability and protects roots from pH stress.

The shock absorber of soil

Buffering capacity is the soil's resistance to change in pH – like a shock absorber on a bike that smooths out bumps. When you add acid or lime, the exchange sites on clay and humus release or absorb H⁺ and keep the pH from swinging wildly. Clayey, humus-rich soils are highly buffered (need more lime to change pH); sandy soils are poorly buffered (pH changes quickly). This protects plants and microbes from sudden changes.

10.7 Factors controlling soil reaction

Factor Effect
Rainfall and climate High rain leaches bases → acid; low rain → alkaline
Parent material Basic rocks (basalt, limestone) give higher pH; acid rocks (granite) give lower pH
Soil colloids H⁺ as main exchangeable cation makes soil acid; Na/Ca as main cations make it alkaline
Soil and root processes Root respiration, CO₂ in soil air, organic acids lower pH
Crops and cultivation Crop removal of bases and legume H⁺ release acidify
Nitrogen fertilizer Ammonium-type N fertilizer speeds acidification
Moisture and CO₂ Drier soil – higher pH; high CO₂ in soil air lowers pH
Flooding Pushes most soils to pH 6.5–7.2 within a month

10.8 Significance of soil pH

  • Overall: most nutrients are most available at pH 6.0–7.5 (the “sweet spot”).

  • Nitrogen: maximum availability at pH 6–8, because that is the best range for microbes that mineralise N and fix it symbiotically.

  • Phosphorus: maximum at pH 6.5–7.5. Below 6.5, Fe and Al in solution form insoluble Fe/Al phosphates; above ~7.5–8, Ca phosphates precipitate. High P availability again at pH > 8.5 as soluble sodium phosphates.

  • Iron and manganese: availability increases with acidity (more soluble) – can become toxic below pH 5.5; become deficient at high pH.

  • Zn, Cu, B: most available in slightly acidic to neutral soils; deficiency in alkaline/calcareous soils. Molybdenum: opposite – more available at higher pH.

  • Ca, Mg, K: low in strongly acid soils (leached); adequate above pH 6.

  • Aluminium: toxic below pH 5.0–5.5; roots are damaged.

  • Microorganisms: most work best at pH 6.0–7.5; nitrifying bacteria are inhibited below ~5.5 and above ~9; fungi tolerate acid soils.

  • Earthworms are inhibited by high acidity.

Acidic soils: apply lime (CaCO₃, Ca(OH)₂, dolomite). Alkaline soils: apply gypsum, elemental sulphur, organic matter; grow tolerant crops.

Ideal pH for most crops: 6.3–6.8 (range 6.0–7.5).

Using pH to make decisions

  • pH below 5.5: aluminium and manganese toxicity, P fixed by Fe and Al – apply lime; grow tolerant crops such as rice, tea, potato, pineapple.
  • pH 6.5–7.5: the sweet spot – most nutrients available, most crops happy.
  • pH above 8.5: sodium problems, poor structure, deficiency of Zn, Fe, Mn, B and P – apply gypsum and organic manures; grow tolerant crops such as barley, cotton, sugar beet.

Play with it

Try it yourself

Buffering experiment: put 50 g sand in one cup and 50 g clayey garden soil in another, add 50 ml water each and the same few drops of vinegar. Test pH (or use the red cabbage indicator) before and after. The sand's pH drops more – it is poorly buffered.

Facts & amazing information

1

The term pH was introduced by Danish chemist S.P.L. Sørensen in 1909.

2

Konkan lateritic soils commonly have pH 5–6, while black soils of the Deccan are often 7.5–8.5.

3

At very low pH (below about 5), aluminium becomes soluble and toxic, giving short, stubby roots.

4

Some crops prefer acidity: tea, pineapple and potato grow well at pH 5–5.5.

5

Clayey, humus-rich soils may need several times more lime than sandy soils to raise pH by one unit, because of high buffering.

6

Kari soils of Kerala can have pH below 3.5 when they dry out – among the most acidic agricultural soils in India.

Possible ESE questions

Short-answer questions

  1. Define soil reaction. (2 marks)

    Answer points: The degree of acidity or alkalinity of soil, expressed as pH.

  2. What is buffering capacity of soil? (2 marks)

    Answer points: The ability of soil to resist change in pH on addition of acid or alkali.

  3. Name the three kinds of soil acidity. (2 marks)

    Answer points: Active (solution), exchangeable (salt-replaceable) and residual (reserve) acidity.

Long-answer questions

  1. Explain the sources of soil acidity. (5 marks)

    Answer points: Leaching of bases by heavy rain, acid parent material, organic acids from decomposition, CO₂/carbonic acid, acid-forming fertilizers (ammonium sulphate, urea), acid rain, Al hydrolysis.

  2. Discuss the significance of soil pH in crop production. (5 marks)

    Answer points: Nutrient availability (Truog chart), toxicity of Al/Mn at low pH, deficiency of Fe/Zn/P at high pH, microbial activity, crop suitability, fertilizer choice, reclamation needs.

  3. Define soil reaction. Discuss sources of acidity and alkalinity, buffering capacity and the significance of pH in agriculture. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Definition and log scale; acidity and alkalinity sources; three kinds of acidity; buffering – mechanism and factors; significance and management (liming, gypsum, crop selection).

  4. Explain the concept of pH as a logarithmic scale and the three kinds of soil acidity. Why does a soil need much more lime than predicted from its active acidity? (10 marks)

    Answer points: pH = −log aH⁺; active, exchangeable, residual acidity; reserve acidity far larger than solution acidity; buffering; lime requirement methods.

  5. Discuss the sources of soil acidity in humid tropical regions and of alkalinity in arid regions of India. How do these processes affect nutrient availability? (10 marks)

    Answer points: Acidity – leaching, OM acids, Al hydrolysis, fertilizers, acid rain; alkalinity – carbonates, bicarbonates, Na, evaporation, poor drainage, irrigation water; Truog chart effects.

  6. Explain the buffering capacity of soils, its mechanisms and the factors affecting it. Discuss its significance in liming and fertilizer use. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Exchange reactions, carbonate dissolution, Al hydrolysis, OM; factors – clay, OM, CEC; practical – lime requirement, stability of pH, fertilizer acidification.

  7. Analyse the effect of soil pH on the availability of macro- and micronutrients with the help of Truog’s diagram. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Diagram; N, K, S broad; P max 6–7; Ca, Mg higher at high pH; Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu higher at low pH; Mo higher at high pH; B; optimum 6.5–7.5.

  8. Explain the methods of determining lime requirement of acid soils. Describe the reactions of liming materials in soil. (10 marks)

    Answer points: SMP buffer, Woodruff, exchangeable Al methods; CaCO₃ + 2H⁺ → Ca²⁺ + H₂O + CO₂; Al neutralisation; materials, fineness, placement.

  9. Discuss the significance of soil reaction for crop selection, microbial activity and toxicity problems, with examples of crops suited to acid and alkaline soils. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Al/Mn toxicity, Na/B toxicity; bacteria vs fungi; nitrification; acid-tolerant (tea, rice, pineapple, potato) and alkali-tolerant (barley, cotton, sugar beet, dhaincha).

  10. Two soils have pH 5.0 and 7.0. How many times more H⁺ activity does the acid soil have? Explain. (5 marks)

    Answer points: 10² = 100 times; log scale.

  11. Critically examine the long-term effect of continuous use of ammonium-based fertilizers on soil pH and suggest corrective measures. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Nitrification releases H⁺; acidification over years; Al toxicity, base loss; corrective – liming, balanced fertilization, OM, nitrate fertilizers, monitoring pH.
