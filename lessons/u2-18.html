Legumes and cotton – the black-soil trio

Soybean in kharif, chickpea in rabi and cotton across both seasons dominate the black soils of Vidarbha and Marathwada. Two of them fix their own nitrogen; the third is India's most important fibre. Each has its own critical operations – BBF and seed inoculation for soybean, nipping and wilt control for chickpea, refuge and pink-bollworm management for cotton.

Black-soil trio

Soybean, chickpea and cotton occupy most of the black (Vertisol) soils of Vidarbha and Marathwada. Soybean (kharif) followed by chickpea (rabi) on residual moisture is the most common double-cropping system of the region, and cotton + tur is the classic intercropping system.

Important terms and operations explained

Legume and nodulation

What it is: Legumes (family Fabaceae) form root nodules when Rhizobium/Bradyrhizobium bacteria infect root hairs; the bacteria fix atmospheric N₂ into ammonia using the enzyme nitrogenase.

Why it is done / why it matters: A good legume crop fixes 40–80 kg N/ha, meets most of its own N need and leaves N for the next crop – a key reason for growing soybean and chickpea in rotations.

How and when: Effective nodules are pink-red inside (leghaemoglobin) and appear 15–20 days after sowing; ensure them by seed inoculation, adequate P, Mo and S, and good aeration.

Seed inoculation

What it is: Coating seed with a culture of Rhizobium/Bradyrhizobium (and PSB) just before sowing.

Why it is done / why it matters: Introduces efficient N-fixing bacteria where the soil lacks them; increases nodulation and yield by 10–15 % at very low cost.

How and when: Treat seed first with fungicide/Trichoderma, then with culture at 25 g/kg (using jaggery solution as sticker); dry in shade and sow within a few hours. Never mix culture with fungicide.

Broad bed and furrow (BBF)

What it is: A land configuration of raised beds about 1.2–1.5 m wide separated by 30–45 cm furrows, made with a BBF planter.

Why it is done / why it matters: In heavy black soils it drains excess rain through furrows, conserves moisture during dry spells, improves aeration of roots and raises soybean/chickpea yield by 15–25 %.

How and when: Prepared at sowing with a tractor BBF planter that also sows 3–4 rows on each bed; furrows guide irrigation water in rabi.

Nipping

What it is: Plucking off 3–5 cm of the growing tip of chickpea plants.

Why it is done / why it matters: Removes apical dominance, so more branches, flowers and pods form; also checks excess vegetative growth under irrigation. The tender tops are eaten as leafy vegetable.

How and when: At 30–40 DAS, by hand or by grazing sheep lightly; not done in late-sown or poor crops.

Pod shattering

What it is: Splitting of dry pods in the field so that seeds fall out.

Why it is done / why it matters: Causes direct loss of yield in soybean and other legumes when harvest is delayed.

How and when: Harvest at 95 % brown pods in the morning when pods are less brittle; choose shattering-tolerant varieties.

Square, flower and boll

What it is: A square is the cotton flower bud enclosed in three leafy bracts; it opens into a flower (cream on the first day, pink the next); the fertilised ovary develops into a boll (capsule) containing seeds covered with lint.

Why it is done / why it matters: Square and boll counts decide cotton yield; shedding of squares/young bolls is a major cause of low yield.

How and when: First square at 35–45 DAS, flowering at 55–65 DAS, boll opens 45–60 days after flowering.

Monopodia and sympodia

What it is: Monopodial branches are vegetative branches that grow from the lower main-stem nodes; sympodial branches are fruiting branches that bear squares and bolls in a zig-zag manner.

Why it is done / why it matters: More sympodia close to the main stem means more bolls; too many monopodia means excessive vegetative growth.

How and when: Encourage sympodia by optimum spacing, balanced N, growth regulators (mepiquat chloride) and detopping.

Detopping

What it is: Removing the terminal bud (top 5–10 cm) of the main stem of cotton.

Why it is done / why it matters: Stops vertical growth, diverts food to developing bolls, improves boll weight and reduces pest hiding places in the canopy.

How and when: At 80–90 DAS or when 15–20 sympodia have formed.

Kapas, lint, ginning out-turn and bale

What it is: Kapas = seed cotton as picked; lint = fibre separated from seed by ginning; ginning out-turn (GOT) = lint weight as % of kapas; a bale = 170 kg of lint.

Why it is done / why it matters: Farmers sell kapas; mills buy lint graded by staple length, strength and fineness.

How and when: GOT of Indian hybrids is about 33–36 %; 100 kg kapas gives about 35 kg lint and 63 kg seed.

Refuge crop

What it is: Non-Bt cotton (or pigeon pea) planted in rows around or within Bt cotton fields.

Why it is done / why it matters: Bollworms feeding on the refuge do not face Bt toxin, so susceptible moths survive and mate with any resistant moths from Bt fields – this delays development of resistance (pink bollworm has already become resistant).

How and when: Plant the refuge seed supplied with the Bt packet (border rows / 5–20 % as advised) at the same time as Bt cotton.

Economic threshold level (ETL)

What it is: The pest population or damage level at which control measures should be taken to prevent the pest reaching the economic injury level.

Why it is done / why it matters: Avoids unnecessary sprays, saves money, protects natural enemies and the environment – the basis of IPM.

How and when: Pink bollworm: 8 moths/trap/night for 3 nights or 10 % damaged bolls; chickpea pod borer: 1 larva per metre row.

Parawilt (sudden wilt)

What it is: Sudden drooping of cotton leaves within 24–48 hours, without any pathogen.

Why it is done / why it matters: Occurs when heavy rain or irrigation follows a long dry spell – roots suffocate and hormone balance is upset; plants recover if treated quickly.

How and when: Spray cobalt chloride 10 ppm within 24–48 hours; ensure drainage.

High-density planting system (HDPS)

What it is: Growing compact cotton varieties at close spacing (90 × 10–15 cm or 60 × 10 cm) – 1.1 to 1.6 lakh plants/ha instead of 20,000–35,000.

Why it is done / why it matters: Short-duration compact crops escape late pink bollworm attack, suit mechanised picking and give higher yields on shallow soils.

How and when: Use compact varieties, mepiquat chloride sprays to control height, and timely termination.

Bird perches

What it is: T-shaped wooden or bamboo stands placed in fields.

Why it is done / why it matters: Insectivorous birds (drongo, myna) sit on them and eat caterpillars such as Helicoverpa and Spodoptera – a cheap biological control.

How and when: 25–50 perches/ha, slightly above crop height, from flowering onward; remove at harvest.

Cleistogamy

What it is: Pollination and fertilisation taking place inside a closed flower bud.

Why it is done / why it matters: Makes crops like chickpea (and often wheat) strictly self-pollinated, so varieties remain pure.

How and when: Crossing needs emasculation of buds before they open.

Note on recommendations

Doses, varieties and dates below are general recommendations of the state agricultural universities of Maharashtra (MPKV Rahuri, Dr PDKV Akola, VNMKV Parbhani, Dr BSKKV Dapoli) and ICAR institutes. Always follow the latest package of practices and the pesticide label for your district.

Irrigation

  • Rainfed crop; give a protective irrigation during long dry spells.
  • Critical stages: flowering and pod filling (most critical).

Weed management

  • Critical period: 15–45 DAS; weeds can reduce yield by 30–50 %.

Flowering and pollination

Intercultural operations

  • Hoeing with bullock/ tractor hoe twice; open conservation furrows after 30 days.
  • Rogue off-types and diseased plants in seed plots.
  • Intercropping: soybean + tur 4:2 (or 6:2) – a standard Maharashtra system.

Flowering and pollination

Intercultural operations

  • Nipping (removing the top 3–5 cm of shoots) at 30–40 DAS in irrigated/vigorous crop – more branches and pods; leaves can be eaten as vegetable.
  • Hoeing to create soil mulch and conserve moisture.
  • Bird perches (T-shaped) 25–50/ha for natural control of pod borer.

Irrigation

  • Critical stages: flowering and boll development (most critical); sowing and square formation.
  • Avoid water stagnation; drip irrigation with fertigation raises yield by 30–40 % and saves 40–50 % water.
  • Stop irrigation when 50 % bolls open.

Weed management

  • Critical period: 15–60 DAS (crop grows slowly at first).
  • Pre-emergence pendimethalin 1.0 kg a.i./ha; post-emergence pyrithiobac-sodium 62.5 g + quizalofop-ethyl 50 g/ha at 20–25 DAS.
  • 2–3 hoeings with bullock hoe (dawra) and hand weeding.

Flowering and pollination

First square appears 35–45 days after sowing; flowers open 20–25 days later (55–65 DAS). The flower is cream-white on the first day and turns pink-red the next day. Cotton is often cross-pollinated (5–30 %) by bees, although self-pollination is common. Boll matures 45–60 days after flowering. Heavy shedding of squares and young bolls (up to 60 %) is natural but increases with stress.

Intercultural operations

  • Thinning and gap filling 10–15 DAS.
  • Hoeing and earthing up at 30–35 DAS.
  • Detopping at 80–90 DAS (or at 15–20 sympodia) to check vertical growth and improve boll setting.
  • Spraying of growth regulator mepiquat chloride in HDPS and excessive-growth crop; NAA 20 ppm to reduce square shedding.
  • Intercropping: cotton + tur, cotton + soybean, cotton + moong/urad.

Maturity indices, harvesting and yield

ItemDetails
Maturity indicesBolls burst open and lint is fluffy and dry; 150–180 days (Bt hybrids)
PickingHand picking of fully opened bolls at 7–10-day intervals in 3–5 pickings after dew dries (10 a.m.); avoid trash and keep first and last pickings separate
YieldRainfed Bt hybrids 15–20 q kapas/ha; irrigated 25–35 q/ha; ginning out-turn 33–36 %

Post-harvest management

  • Dry kapas in shade to 8–10 % moisture; grade by colour and trash; store in clean, dry place (not in plastic bags).
  • Ginning: lint + seed; pressing into bales of 170 kg.
  • After the last picking, uproot and destroy stalks or shred them – breaks the life cycle of pink bollworm; do not leave cattle to graze on residual bolls for long.

Facts & amazing information

1

Soybean has about 40 % protein – roughly twice that of other pulses and three times that of cereals.

2

India grows about 70 % of the world’s chickpea, but still imports pulses because domestic demand is so high.

3

Cotton was spun and woven at Mohenjo-daro about 5,000 years ago – India is the original home of cotton textiles.

4

Bt cotton was approved in India in 2002; today more than 90 % of the cotton area is under Bt hybrids, but pink bollworm has developed resistance.

5

Maharashtra has the largest cotton area of India – about 41 lakh hectares in 2024–25.

6

A good chickpea or soybean crop can leave 40–80 kg N/ha in the soil for the next crop.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Give the Marathi name, botanical name, family, origin and chromosome number of soybean, chickpea and cotton. (5 marks · BTL-1 Remember · CO2)

    Answer points: सोयाबीन, Glycine max, Fabaceae, NE China, 2n = 40; हरभरा, Cicer arietinum, Fabaceae, SE Turkey, 2n = 16; कापूस, G. hirsutum, Malvaceae, Central America, 2n = 52.

  2. Explain wilt and pod borer of chickpea and their integrated management. Add the purpose of nipping. (7 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Fusarium wilt – symptoms, vascular browning, resistant varieties, Trichoderma; Helicoverpa – ETL, pheromone traps, bird perches, HaNPV, emamectin; nipping at 30–40 DAS for branching.

  3. Describe the package of practices of soybean with special reference to BBF, seed treatment and weed management. (8 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Climate, soil, BBF layout, sowing window, seed rate 65–75 kg, 45 × 5 cm, thiram + carbendazim then Bradyrhizobium, 30:60:30 + S, pendimethalin, imazethapyr, girdle beetle, YMV, rust, harvest.

  4. Discuss the management of pink bollworm in Bt cotton and the importance of refuge, detopping and timely crop termination. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Resistance to Bt; rosette flowers; boll damage; avoid pre-monsoon sowing; short-duration hybrids; pheromone traps; ETL; refuge rows; detopping; stubble destruction; terminate by December.

  5. Compare soybean, chickpea and cotton with respect to season, climate, soil, sowing, nutrient needs, flowering behaviour, maturity indices and yield. (12 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Kharif/rabi/kharif; temperatures; black soils; spacing and seed rates; NPK; self vs often cross-pollinated; cleistogamy; maturity signs; yields.

removing the cotton terminal bud at 80–90 DAS

non-Bt rows that delay Bt resistance
Parawilt
sudden wilt of cotton after heavy water following drought
Girdle beetle
