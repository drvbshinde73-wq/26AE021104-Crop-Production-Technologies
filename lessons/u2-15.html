Same seed, same soil – different weather, different harvest

In March 2022 an early heat wave raised Indian temperatures 4–5 °C above normal during wheat grain filling. The crop looked healthy, but grains shrivelled and national production fell by several million tonnes. Weather is the one input a farmer cannot buy – but an agronomist who understands how light, temperature, rain, humidity and wind act on the crop can plan around it.

The main weather elements that affect crops are solar radiation (light), temperature, rainfall/soil moisture, relative humidity and wind. Together with CO2 they decide crop growth (increase in size and dry matter) and development (progress through phenological stages such as germination, flowering and maturity).

Growth vs development

Growth is quantitative – the plant gets taller and heavier. Development is qualitative – the plant moves from one stage to the next (vegetative → flowering → grain filling → maturity). Temperature and day length mainly control development; light, water and nutrients mainly control growth.

15.2 Solar radiation (light)

Only about 45–50 % of solar radiation is photosynthetically active radiation (PAR, 400–700 nm), and crops convert just 1–3 % of incident sunlight into dry matter. Light acts on crops through:

AspectEffect on crops
IntensityRate of photosynthesis rises with light up to the light-saturation point (lower for C3, higher for C4). Cloudy weather during rice grain filling lowers yield.
Quality (wavelength)Red and blue light drive photosynthesis; far-red affects stem elongation; UV damages tissues.
Duration (photoperiod)Controls flowering in short-day (rice, soybean) and long-day (wheat, chickpea) crops.
InterceptionDepends on leaf area index (LAI) and canopy architecture; an LAI of 3–5 intercepts about 95 % of light.

Why spacing is a light decision

Row direction and spacing decide how much light reaches lower leaves. North–south rows receive more uniform light during the day. In intercropping, the tall crop (tur, maize) is the light-winner, so the short crop (soybean, moong) must be shade-tolerant or sown at wider spacing.

15.3 Temperature

Every crop has three cardinal temperatures: a minimum below which growth stops, an optimum at which growth is fastest, and a maximum above which growth stops and tissues are injured.

Growing degree days (GDD) measure heat accumulation: GDD = Σ [(Tmax + Tmin)/2 − Tbase]. A wheat crop needs about 1,500–1,700 GDD (base 5 °C) from sowing to maturity. Warmer seasons accumulate GDD faster, so the crop matures earlier with less time to fill grain.

Watch out

For wheat in central and peninsular India, every day of sowing delay after mid-November costs about 25–30 kg grain/ha because the crop is pushed into the hot weather of March.

15.4 Rainfall and soil moisture

Rainfall is the main water source for about 50 % of India's net sown area. What matters is not only the total but its distribution, intensity and timing.

  • Effective rainfall – the part of rain stored in the root zone and used by the crop (not lost as runoff or deep percolation).

  • Dry spells of 2–3 weeks at flowering or pod-filling cause heavy yield loss in soybean, tur and cotton.

  • Excess rain / waterlogging deprives roots of oxygen (soybean, cotton and pulses are sensitive; rice tolerates).

  • Critical stages – crown-root initiation of wheat, panicle initiation and flowering of rice, tasselling–silking of maize, flowering and pod formation of pulses.

15.5 Relative humidity and wind

ElementFavourable effectsHarmful effects
Relative humidity (RH)Moderate RH reduces transpiration and water stressHigh RH (> 80 %) favours fungal diseases (blast, rust, mildew) and pests; very low RH increases water loss and causes flower drop
WindGentle wind supplies CO2, aids pollination (maize), dries crops after rainStrong wind causes lodging (sugarcane, wheat), shattering, soil erosion, high evapotranspiration; hot dry winds (loo) scorch crops

In the field

Farmers plant shelter belts of tall trees (e.g. Casuarina, subabul) on the windward side of fields. A shelter belt protects a distance of about 10–20 times its height downwind, reducing evaporation and lodging.

Floods / waterloggingDrainage, raised beds, submergence-tolerant rice (Swarna-Sub1)
Heat waveLight frequent irrigation, timely sowing, heat-tolerant varieties
Hailstorm / unseasonal rainCrop insurance (PMFBY), timely harvest, weather-based agro-advisories
Agro-advisories: The India Meteorological Department issues district-level Agromet Advisory Bulletins twice a week through Gramin Krishi Mausam Sewa, the Meghdoot app and SMS – farmers can time sowing, spraying and irrigation according to forecasts.

Facts & amazing information

1

Plants use only about 1–3 % of the sunlight falling on them to make dry matter – sugarcane, among the best, reaches about 2–3 %.

2

Rice pollen becomes sterile if temperature exceeds about 35 °C for just one hour at anthesis.

3

The Indian monsoon delivers roughly 75 % of the country's annual rainfall in only four months.

4

A 1 °C rise in temperature during the growing season can lower wheat yield in India by about 4–6 %.

5

Maize pollen is carried by wind; one tassel releases about 2–5 million pollen grains.

6

A shelter belt 10 m high can protect crops from wind for up to 200 m downwind.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Explain the effect of temperature on crop growth and development. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Cardinal temperatures, GDD, phenology, cold and heat stress effects, examples (wheat terminal heat, rice sterility), management.

  2. Describe the role of solar radiation in crop production. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Intensity, quality, duration; PAR; light saturation in C3/C4; LAI and interception; photoperiodism; spacing and row direction.

  3. Critically analyse how the distribution of rainfall, rather than its total amount, determines crop productivity in rainfed agriculture of Maharashtra. Suggest agronomic adaptations for each rainfall pattern. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Onset, dry spells, intensity, cessation; critical stages; effective rainfall; adaptations – sowing window, BBF/ridge-furrow, mulching, protective irrigation, contingency crops, intercropping.

  4. Using the concept of growing degree days, explain why late-sown wheat in peninsular India matures early and yields less. Calculate the GDD accumulated in 10 days with mean temperature of 24 °C (base 5 °C) and interpret. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: GDD 19/day → 190 in 10 days; faster accumulation shortens grain-fill period; terminal heat; yield components affected; management – timely sowing, varieties.

  5. Evaluate the agronomic strategies to minimise the impact of weather extremes – delayed monsoon, mid-season drought, heat wave and floods – on kharif crops, with emphasis on contingency crop planning. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Contingency plans, varietal choice, sowing windows, moisture conservation, protective irrigation, drainage, Sub1 rice, crop insurance, agromet advisories.

  6. Discuss the combined effect of relative humidity and temperature on pest and disease outbreaks in crops. How can weather-based forecasting reduce pesticide use? (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Disease triangle; high RH + moderate temp – blast, rust, late blight, mildews; pests – aphids, whitefly; forecasting models; need-based spraying; IMD/agromet advisories.
