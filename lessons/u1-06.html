Giving soils a name and an address

Doctors classify diseases, botanists classify plants – and soil scientists classify soils. Without names, a farmer in Akola and a scientist in the USA could not compare notes. When you hear that a field is a "Vertisol", you instantly know it is clayey, cracks in summer, swells in rain and holds a lot of water. One word carries a whole package of information. That is the power of classification.

6.1 Why classify soils?

Soils are extremely diverse, like the many types of people or plants. We classify them to:

  • give each soil a common name and identity;

  • predict its physical, chemical and biological behaviour;

  • select suitable crops, irrigation, drainage and management;

  • support decisions in agriculture, forestry, engineering and conservation;

  • give researchers, surveyors and land managers a common scientific language.

Explained simply

Classification is like a library catalogue. Lakhs of soils exist, but by grouping them by their properties we can find similar soils quickly, transfer research results from one place to another, make soil maps, and recommend the right crop, fertilizer or irrigation method for each group.

6.2 History of soil classification

Year Scientist / system Idea
1880s V. V. Dokuchaev (Russia) – “Father of Soil Science” Soil is a natural body shaped by climate and vegetation. Started genetic classification.
1920s C. F. Marbut (USA) – “Father of American soil classification” Great soil groups based on soil characteristics (colour, texture, structure).
1938 Baldwin, Kellogg and Thorp Zonal, Intrazonal and Azonal soils (genetic).
1960 USDA – 7th Approximation Based on measurable soil properties, not guessed origin.
1975 / 1999 Soil Taxonomy (USDA, 1st and 2nd edition) Modern hierarchical system – the one used today.
India Dr. J. W. Leather (England) “Father of Soil Science and Agricultural Chemistry in India”.

From old ideas to modern taxonomy

Early systems grouped soils by what formed them – climate and vegetation (Dokuchaev's zonal, intrazonal and azonal soils). The modern USDA Soil Taxonomy (1975, revised many times) groups soils by what can actually be measured in the profile – diagnostic horizons, temperature and moisture. This makes it objective: two scientists examining the same pit should reach the same name.

Genetic classification: zonal, intrazonal, azonal

Class Main controlling factor Features Examples
Zonal Climate and vegetation Well-developed profile; reflect the climatic belt; parent material matters little Laterite, Podzol, Chernozem
Intrazonal Local conditions (topography, parent material) Found within a zone but with strong local influence: excess water, salts, CaCO₃ Saline, sodic, peat soils
Azonal Time (too young) Poorly developed profile, no horizon differentiation Alluvial soils, sand dunes

Limitation: this system was based on assumed origin (genesis), so many soil series did not fit.

6.3 Soil Taxonomy (USDA)

Soil Taxonomy is a hierarchical system based on measurable properties seen in the field and lab, mainly diagnostic horizons. A pedon (about 1–10 m², up to ~2 m deep) is the smallest 3-D unit sampled for study; a group of similar pedons forms a polypedon/soil series.

Reading a soil name like a postal address

Soil Taxonomy has six levels: Order → Suborder → Great group → Subgroup → Family → Series, just like Country → State → District → Taluka → Village → House. All order names end in "-sol" (Latin solum = soil), and each has a short formative element that appears in every lower name. For example, in "Ustert" the -ert tells you it is a Vertisol and Ust- tells you it has a dry (ustic) moisture regime.

Common mistake

Do not confuse Alfisols (high base saturation, moderate weathering) with Ultisols (low base saturation, more weathered). Both have a clay-rich B horizon; base saturation decides which.

Taxonomy names are like a word built from small parts. Example: Typic Argiustoll:

  • Typic = subgroup (the central, typical example)

  • Argi = argillic (clay) horizon → great group

  • ust = ustic (dry-but-summer-rain) moisture regime → suborder

  • oll = Mollisol → order

The 12 soil orders

Memory trick: A-VAGAMI-HOUSE = Aridisols, Vertisols, Alfisols, Gelisols, Andisols, Mollisols, Inceptisols, Histosols, Oxisols, Ultisols, Spodosols, Entisols.

Order Word root Key features Typical environment
Aridisols L. aridus = dry Dry; accumulation of salts, gypsum, CaCO₃ in subsoil; very low OM; salty horizons Deserts and arid regions
Vertisols L. verto = turn High-shrink/swell clay (smectite); deep cracks when dry; self-mixing (churned); slickensides; parent basalt or limestone; surface loose; heavy to plough (large clods) Black cotton soil of Deccan, Maharashtra
Alfisols Pedalfer (Al + Fe) Argillic horizon; base saturation > 35 %; A–E–B development; moderately productive; A horizon < 25 cm Forests, sub-humid
Gelisols L. gelare = freeze Permafrost within 2 m (100 cm for some); glacic layer ≥ 30 cm; frost churning Cold polar/alpine
Andisols Jpn. ando = black soil Volcanic ash soils; young (5000–7000 yrs); very low bulk density (< water); very high P-fixation; high OC (7–12 %) and CEC; hold much water Volcanic regions
Mollisols L. mollis = soft Thick dark mollic epipedon; base saturation > 50 %; high OM; very fertile Grasslands, prairies
Inceptisols L. inceptum = beginning Young “toddler” soils; cambic horizon; weak development; low in N, P, K; all areas except deserts Widespread in India
Histosols Gk. histos = tissue Organic soils (peat, muck) with 20–30 %+ OM; developed in water Bogs, marshes
Oxisols Fr. oxide Oxic horizon; intensely weathered; yellowish-red; high clay and Fe/Al oxides; acidic; low productivity Humid tropics
Ultisols L. ultimus = last “Ultimately weathered”; strongly leached; argillic horizon; base saturation < 35 %; acidic; dark humus-rich surface Humid tropics/subtropics
Spodosols Gk. spodos = wood ash Spodic horizon; ashy grey sandy surface; humus translocated; acidic; low base saturation Cool, wet conifer forest
Entisols Recent “Baby” soils; little/no horizon development (only A and C); poor in N, P, OM; rich in K and lime; resistant minerals Floodplains, sand dunes
Vertisols and Inceptisols dominate Maharashtra; Entisols are the most abundant order in the world’s newly deposited soils; Alfisols and Ultisols differ by base saturation (>35 % vs <35 %); Mollisols have >50 % base saturation.

Soil orders of India

In India the main orders are Inceptisols (largest), Entisols, Alfisols, Vertisols, Aridisols and smaller areas of Ultisols, Mollisols and others. Traditionally, ICAR also recognises the major soil groups: alluvial, black (regur), red, laterite, desert (arid), mountain/forest, saline-alkali and peaty soils.

6.4 Soils of Maharashtra

Maharashtra has many soil types. More than 80 % of the state is covered by basalt (Deccan Trap lava), so a huge part of the state has black soil formed from it. Five types are recognised: black, laterite, red, alluvial and clay soils (plus coarse/yellow-brown soils in hilly parts).

Soil Origin Main features Region Crops
Black soil (regur / lava soil) Weathering of basalt (igneous). Black colour attributed to titaniferous magnetite and organic matter High moisture retention (clay + lime); drains slowly – over-irrigation makes it swampy/waterlogged; high lime; cracks when dry; mostly Vertisols and Inceptisols More than ¾ of the state; Godavari and Bhima–Krishna basins; thickest in Tapi basin; all Marathwada districts + Yavatmal, Akola, Washim, Amravati Cotton (best), sorghum, red gram, pearl millet, wheat, sugarcane

Black soil = >¾ of Maharashtra; best crop cotton; formed from basalt.

Laterite = cashew + mango; Red = teak; Alluvial = rice, sugarcane, wheat.

Black soils have high CEC (40–60 cmol(+)/kg) because of smectite (montmorillonite) clay; laterite/red soils have low CEC (5–15).

Soils of Maharashtra in one picture

Black soils (Vertisols and Inceptisols) from basalt dominate the Deccan plateau – Marathwada, Vidarbha and western Maharashtra; ideal for cotton, soybean, jowar, tur, sugarcane and wheat, but sticky when wet and hard when dry. Laterite and lateritic soils in the Konkan (Ratnagiri, Sindhudurg) – acidic, well-drained, good for mango, cashew, coconut and rice. Red and yellow soils in eastern Vidarbha (Bhandara, Gadchiroli, Chandrapur) – suited to rice. Coastal alluvial and saline soils along the coast, and alluvial soils along river banks such as the Tapi and Godavari.

Why black soils crack

Black soils are rich in montmorillonite (smectite) clay, which swells like a sponge when wet and shrinks when dry. In summer, wide deep cracks open – deep enough to swallow a pen. Surface soil falls into the cracks and the soil slowly turns itself over: the name Vertisol comes from Latin verto = to turn.

Try it yourself

Soil detective: ask a farmer in your village which soil type he has and what crops grow best. Then check its colour, stickiness when wet and cracks when dry. Can you guess whether it is a Vertisol, Inceptisol, Alfisol or lateritic soil?

Facts & amazing information

1

USDA Soil Taxonomy recognises 12 soil orders – every soil on Earth fits into one of them.

2

Vertisols get their name from Latin verto = to turn, because they literally turn themselves over through cracking.

3

Black soils (regur) cover roughly one-sixth of India's land area, mostly on the Deccan plateau.

4

Deep cracks in Vertisols can be over 1 cm wide and reach 50 cm or more in summer.

5

Laterite is soft when freshly cut but hardens permanently in air – Konkan houses and forts are built of it.

6

India's National Bureau of Soil Survey and Land Use Planning (NBSS&LUP) is headquartered at Nagpur, Maharashtra.

Long-answer questions for ESE

Each question shows marks, Bloom's Taxonomy Level (BTL) and the course outcome it assesses.

  1. Trace the development of soil classification from Dokuchaev to USDA Soil Taxonomy and evaluate the advantages of a quantitative system. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO1)

    Answer points: Genetic classification; Marbut; 1938 system; 7th Approximation; Soil Taxonomy 1975/1999; diagnostic horizons; advantages.

  2. Explain the hierarchical levels of USDA Soil Taxonomy and the role of diagnostic horizons and formative elements in naming a soil. (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO1)

    Answer points: Order to series; epipedons and subsurface horizons; formative elements; example – Typic Haplustert.

  3. Describe the twelve soil orders with their formative elements and identify the orders dominant in India. (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO1)

    Answer points: Twelve orders; key features; India – Inceptisols, Entisols, Alfisols, Vertisols, Aridisols, Mollisols, Ultisols, Oxisols.

  4. Analyse the distribution, properties and management of the major soils of Maharashtra. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Black, red, laterite, alluvial, coastal saline; properties; crops; management needs.

  5. Compare Vertisols and Alfisols with respect to clay mineralogy, physical behaviour and crop management. (5 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Smectite vs kaolinite/illite; cracking; drainage; tillage; crops.
