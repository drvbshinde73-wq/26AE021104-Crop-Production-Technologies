Why does water vanish in one field and stand in another?

After the same rain, water disappears within minutes in a sandy field near the river, but stands for days in a black-soil field. A bullock-drawn plough moves easily on some days and gets stuck on others. Roots go deep in one soil and stay shallow in another. All these everyday observations are explained by the physical properties of soil – the subject of this lecture, and the most important lecture for an agricultural engineer.

5.1 Why physical properties matter

Physical properties tell us how the soil behaves – how it holds water and air, how roots push through it, how easily it can be ploughed. They depend on: the amount, size, shape and arrangement of particles; mineral composition; organic matter; pore space; and water content. In turn they control: plant support and root penetration, drainage and aeration, retention of moisture and nutrients, and chemical and biological behaviour.

The important physical properties are: texture, structure, surface area, density (bulk and particle), porosity, colour, temperature, consistency, compaction, crusting and plasticity.

5.2 Soil texture

Soil texture is the relative proportion (percentage) of sand, silt and clay in the fine-earth fraction (particles smaller than 2 mm). It is a permanent, natural property – ploughing cannot change it. It is measured by mechanical analysis (hydrometer or pipette method).

Separate Size (USDA) Feel Behaviour
Sand 2.0 – 0.05 mm Gritty, non-sticky Large pores, fast drainage, low water holding, low CEC, low fertility
Silt 0.05 – 0.002 mm Smooth like flour/talc Medium water holding and movement; erodes easily; can crust
Clay < 0.002 mm Sticky and plastic when wet Tiny pores, high water holding, high CEC, poor drainage, risk of waterlogging; swells and shrinks

(International system: sand 2–0.02 mm, silt 0.02–0.002 mm, clay < 0.002 mm.)

Loam is “just right”: a balance of sand (drainage), silt (smoothness) and clay (nutrient and water holding). Ideal texture for most crops is loam to clay loam.

Explained simply – balls, marbles and powder

Imagine filling three jars: one with cricket balls (sand), one with marbles (silt) and one with talcum powder (clay). Water pours straight through the balls (large pores, fast drainage, little holding). The powder holds water but lets it through very slowly. Loam – a good mix of all three – gives the best balance, which is why farmers love loamy soils.

Texture is called a permanent property because you cannot change the sand-silt-clay proportion of a whole field by ploughing; you can only manage it.

Feel method in the field

Wet a little soil and rub it between thumb and finger. Gritty = sand; smooth and floury = silt; sticky and forms a long ribbon = clay. With practice, you can estimate the textural class within a minute without any laboratory.

5.3 Soil structure

Soil structure is the arrangement of primary particles (sand, silt, clay) into larger clumps called aggregates or peds. Texture tells you what the particles are; structure tells you how they are put together. Structure can be changed by management (tillage, manure).

Binding agents that glue particles into peds: organic matter and fungal hyphae, microbial gums, Fe and Al oxides, CaCO₃, silica, clay itself, and flocculating cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe³⁺, Al³⁺). Wetting–drying, freezing–thawing and roots also help.

Type of structure Shape Where found Effect on water
Platy Flat plates, horizontal layers Compacted surface soils, E horizon Blocks downward flow – water spreads sideways
Prismatic / columnar Vertical pillars Subsoil (B) of clayey and sodic soils Water funnelled down vertical cracks
Blocky (angular / sub-angular) Cube-like blocks Subsoil (B) Moderate, zig-zag infiltration
Granular / crumb Small round grains Surface (A) horizon rich in organic matter Rapid, even infiltration – the ideal structure

Structure is further described by Type (shape), Class (size: very fine to very coarse) and Grade (distinctness: structureless, weak, moderate, strong).

Term Meaning
Aggregates Clumps of soil particles of various size
Granules Small (0.5–5 mm), round aggregates
Crumbs Small, porous, rounded aggregates (< 1 cm) – ideal
Peds Natural soil aggregates formed by soil processes
Clods Large hard lumps formed by tillage when soil is too wet/dry or compacted

  • Factors affecting structure: climate, organic matter, tillage, plant roots, soil organisms (earthworms), fertilizers, wetting and drying.

  • Importance: affects porosity, aeration, water movement, root growth, temperature, mechanical stability, erosion resistance, and fertility.

Texture is the bricks, structure is the building

Texture tells you the size of the particles; structure tells you how they are arranged and glued together into lumps called aggregates (peds). The glue is clay, humus, microbial gums and fungal threads. Granular/crumb structure, like a heap of small cookie crumbs, is the best for crops – lots of pores between crumbs for air and water. Unlike texture, structure can be improved (organic matter, green manure, proper tillage) or destroyed (ploughing when too wet, heavy machines, puddling).

5.4 Surface area

Specific surface area = total surface area of particles in a unit mass of soil (m²/g). Cut a cube into smaller cubes and the total surface grows enormously (see table). Surface is where water films, nutrient ions and chemical reactions happen, so finer particles = more surface = more reactivity.

Particle diameter (cm) Number of particles in 1 cm³ Total surface area (cm²)
1.0 1 3.14
0.1 1,000 31.4
0.001 1 billion 3,141.6
0.0001 10 trillion 31,416

  • Approx. specific surface: sand < 1 m²/g; silt ~1–10; kaolinite ~10–20; illite ~80–100; montmorillonite 600–800 m²/g; humus ~800+ m²/g.

  • Ideal range for productive soils ≈ 20–200 m²/g.

  • Higher surface area → more water retention, more nutrient adsorption, more swelling, more pesticide adsorption.

Why clay is so active

Cut a 1 cm cube into tiny cubes of 1 µm: the total surface becomes 10,000 times larger! Clay particles are so tiny and plate-shaped that one gram of some clays has a surface area of hundreds of square metres – more than a tennis court. All the chemical action – holding nutrients and water – happens on surfaces, so a little clay has a big effect.

5.5 Soil density

Density = mass per unit volume.

Particle density (ρp) Bulk density (ρb)
Meaning Density of the solid particles only (no pores) Density of the whole soil including pores
Formula Mass of solids ÷ Volume of solids Oven-dry mass of soil ÷ Total volume (solids + pores)
Normal value 2.65 g/cc (Mg m⁻³); range 2.60–2.75 1.1–1.6 g/cc; ideal 1.1–1.4
Depends on Mineral composition. More OM lowers it. Almost constant. Texture, structure, compaction, organic matter. Changes with management.
Sand / clay Coarse sand 2.655, fine sand 2.659, silt 2.798, clay 2.837 Sandy 1.6, loam 1.4, silt loam 1.3, clay 1.1
Why is bulk density of clay lower than sand? Clay has lots of tiny pores (more total pore space), so the same volume holds less solid mass. Sand packs tightly with fewer pores. A dense (high bulk-density) soil = compacted = poor for roots.

Bulk density vs particle density

Particle density is the density of the solid grains alone (like weighing only the stones in a jar) – about 2.65 g/cm³ for most mineral soils, because quartz and feldspar are about that dense. Bulk density includes the pores too (the stones plus the air between them) – so it is always lower, usually 1.1–1.6 g/cm³.

A high bulk density (> 1.6) means the soil is compacted – roots struggle to push through, and water cannot enter easily. Tractors and over-tillage raise it; organic matter lowers it.

5.6 Porosity

Pore space = the part of soil volume not filled with solid particles; it is occupied by air and water. Roots grow and microbes live here.

Porosity (%) = [1 − (Bulk density ÷ Particle density)] × 100

Bulk density = 1.3 g/cc, particle density = 2.65 g/cc.

Porosity = [1 − (1.3 ÷ 2.65)] × 100 = [1 − 0.49] × 100 = 51 %.

So about 51 % of the volume is pore space (air + water).

  • Macropores (non-capillary, large): drain water quickly by gravity; hold air. Dominant in sandy and well-structured soils.

  • Micropores (capillary, small): hold water tightly against gravity. Dominant in clays.

  • Ideal porosity ≈ 45–60 %. Sandy ≈ 40 %, clay ≈ 58 % (but clay pores are small).

  • Factors: texture, structure, organic matter, macro-organisms, depth, cropping.

Worked example

A soil has BD = 1.33 g/cm³ and PD = 2.65 g/cm³. Porosity = (1 − 1.33/2.65) × 100 = (1 − 0.50) × 100 = 50 %. Half the soil volume is pores.

But the size of pores matters too: macropores (large, in sandy soils) drain water and let air in; micropores (small, in clay) hold water. Interestingly, clay soils have more total porosity than sandy soils, but most of it is in tiny pores – so they drain slowly.

5.7 Soil colour

Colour is a quick clue to what is happening in the soil. Soil scientists use the Munsell colour chart, which describes colour in three parts:

  • Hue – the basic spectral colour (e.g., 10YR, 5YR; Y = yellow, R = red).

  • Value – lightness or darkness (0 = black, 10 = white).

  • Chroma – purity or strength of colour (0 = grey, 8 = vivid).

Colour Usually indicates
Black / dark brown High organic matter (humus) – or, in black soils, Ti-magnetite and clay
Reddish brown / red Iron oxides (hematite), well-drained oxidised conditions
Yellowish brown Hydrated iron oxides (limonite), or drier conditions
Bluish / greenish grey (gley) Waterlogging; Fe reduced – lack of oxygen
Whitish / light grey Leaching of Fe/OM, or presence of salts/lime/quartz

Origin of colour: lithochromic (inherited from parent rock – e.g., reddish from iron-rich basalt, light grey from quartz-rich granite) and pedochromic (developed by soil processes – humus darkening, gleying, leaching).

Colour is the soil's report card

Dark brown or black often means plenty of humus (or, in Vertisols, special clay). Red means well-aerated soil with oxidised iron. Yellow means hydrated iron in moister conditions. Grey or blue-green with rusty spots (mottles) means poor drainage – the soil is waterlogged part of the year. Scientists compare colours with the Munsell colour chart (hue, value, chroma).

5.8 Soil temperature

Soil temperature controls seed germination, root growth, microbial activity, nutrient availability and crop establishment. Average annual soil temperature in India is about 17–24 °C; ideal range for most crops is 20–30 °C. Soil temperature falls with depth and daily fluctuations are small at depth.

  • Dark soils absorb more heat and warm faster; light soils reflect and warm slowly.

  • Organic matter and moisture: wet soils warm slowly because water has high specific heat.

  • Slope aspect: south-facing slopes (northern hemisphere) are warmer.

  • Soil porosity: loose porous soils warm and cool faster; vegetation or mulch reduces fluctuations.

5.9 Soil consistency

Consistency is the resistance of soil to mechanical stress (crushing, moulding) at different moisture levels – it depends on cohesion (attraction between similar particles) and adhesion (attraction to other surfaces).

Moisture state Terms used What you feel
Wet Non-sticky → sticky; non-plastic → plastic High stickiness and plasticity
Moist Loose, very friable, friable, firm, very firm, extremely firm Friable (easily crumbled) is ideal; firm = needs moderate pressure
Dry Loose, soft, slightly hard, hard, very hard, extremely hard Hard = breaks only with difficulty; extremely hard = cannot be broken by hand

Controlled by texture, structure, moisture content, organic matter, and the type of clay.

The right time to plough

Every farmer knows there is a "vaapsa" (proper moisture) condition when soil ploughs beautifully into crumbs. Too wet and it smears and sticks to the plough; too dry and it breaks into hard clods. This is consistency in action – plough in the friable range.

5.10 Soil compaction

Compaction = compression plus increase in bulk density; soil particles are pressed together, pore space (especially macropores) decreases. Caused by heavy machinery, animal trampling, repeated tillage in wet soil, and low organic matter. A plough pan is a hard layer just below ploughing depth.

  • Effects: bulk density ↑, pore space ↓, aeration ↓, infiltration ↓ (more runoff), root growth restricted, poor crop yield.

  • Ideal bulk density: < 1.5 Mg m⁻³ (loamy). Remedies: add organic matter, avoid working wet soil, deep ploughing/subsoiling, controlled traffic, cover crops.

5.11 Soil crusting

Raindrops hit bare soil, break the aggregates, and the tiny dispersed particles fill the pores. When the sun dries the surface rapidly, a thin, hard, dense crust forms. Effects: reduces infiltration, increases runoff, restricts seedling emergence, reduces gas exchange. Prevention: mulching, adding organic matter/gypsum, maintaining vegetation cover, light surface tillage (rotary hoeing).

5.12 Plasticity and Atterberg limits

Plasticity is the ability of a wet soil to be moulded into a shape under pressure and to keep that shape after the pressure is removed, without cracking or changing volume (like modelling clay). Only soil with enough clay shows plasticity.

Limit Meaning
Shrinkage limit (SL) Moisture below which soil does not shrink further; soil is dry and hard
Lower plastic limit (LPL) Lowest moisture at which soil can be rolled into a 3 mm thread without breaking
Upper plastic limit (UPL) / liquid limit Moisture at which soil starts to flow like a liquid

Plasticity index (Ip) = UPL − LPL. Higher Ip means a wider moisture range over which soil is plastic.

Soil Plasticity index Behaviour
Gravel, sand 0 Non-plastic
Silt 10–15 Low plasticity
Clay (alluvial) 15–100 Medium to high
Clay (black soil) 100–250 Very high

  • Plasticity increases with clay %, with smectite > kaolinite, and with organic matter.

  • Agricultural meaning: soil is best ploughed in the friable range (between SL and LPL) – too wet = sticky/puddled, too dry = hard clods.

Explained simply – adding water to flour

Add water to dry clay soil little by little, like making dough. At first it is a hard powder (solid), then crumbly (semi-solid), then you can roll it into threads without breaking (plastic), and finally it flows like batter (liquid). The water contents at which these changes happen are the shrinkage limit, plastic limit and liquid limit – the Atterberg limits. Plasticity index = LL − PL; a high value means a very clayey, sticky soil, important for designing farm roads, canals and earthen bunds.

Try it yourself

Measure bulk density at home: push an empty tin can (known volume, e.g., 250 cm³) into undisturbed soil, dig it out with the soil intact, dry the soil in the sun or oven and weigh it. Bulk density = dry weight (g) ÷ volume (cm³). Compare a footpath soil with garden soil – which is more compacted?

Facts & amazing information

1

One gram of smectite clay can have a surface area of 600–800 m² – more than a tennis court.

2

A loam soil can store roughly 150–200 mm of available water in a metre of depth – a 'water tank' for crops between rains.

3

Bulk density above about 1.6 g/cm³ in clayey soils starts to restrict root growth.

4

Soil texture cannot be changed by ploughing in a whole field, but soil structure can be built or destroyed in a single season.

5

The Atterberg limits used in soil mechanics and civil engineering were developed by a Swedish chemist, Albert Atterberg, in 1911.

6

Dark soils warm up faster in sunlight; mulches can lower summer soil temperature by several degrees.
