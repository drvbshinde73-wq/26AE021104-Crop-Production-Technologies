Two crops, one field – more than double the benefit

In Vidarbha a farmer sows six rows of soybean followed by two rows of tur. Soybean is harvested in October; tur keeps growing on residual moisture until January. On the same hectare he harvests two crops, spreads his risk, gets nitrogen from both legumes and keeps the land covered for seven months. This lesson is about planning which crops to grow, in what arrangement and in what sequence.

16.1 Important terms

Cropping system

  1. In simple wordsWhich crops a farmer grows, in what order and how, on his land through the year.
  2. Scientific definitionThe cropping pattern of a farm and its interaction with farm resources, other farm enterprises and available technology that determine their make-up.
  3. Standard definition · key factCropping systems include sole cropping, sequence cropping, intercropping, mixed cropping and relay cropping.
  4. One-line (exam)Cropping system = cropping pattern + its management.

Cropping pattern

  1. In simple wordsThe share of land under different crops in an area at a given time.
  2. Scientific definitionThe yearly sequence and spatial arrangement of crops, and of crops and fallow, on a given area.
  3. Standard definition · key factIt is usually expressed as the percentage of area under each crop in a region.
  4. One-line (exam)Cropping pattern = proportion and sequence of crops on land.

Intercropping

  1. In simple wordsGrowing two or more crops together in the same field in definite rows.
  2. Scientific definitionGrowing two or more crops simultaneously on the same field in a definite row arrangement (e.g. 2 : 1, 4 : 2), so that crop intensification occurs in both time and space.
  3. Standard definition · key factIntercropping aims at greater productivity per unit area and risk reduction; its efficiency is measured by LER.
  4. One-line (exam)Intercropping = two or more crops together in definite rows.

Mixed cropping

  1. In simple wordsMixing seeds of two or more crops and sowing them together without fixed rows.
  2. Scientific definitionGrowing two or more crops simultaneously on the same piece of land without any definite row arrangement, mainly as insurance against crop failure under rainfed conditions.
  3. Standard definition · key factExample: mixtures of jowar, tur, moong and cowpea in dryland farming.
  4. One-line (exam)Mixed cropping = crops mixed, no definite rows.

Explained simply

A cropping pattern tells you what is grown (40 % soybean, 30 % cotton…). A cropping system also tells you how and when those crops are arranged in space and time, and how they use the farm's land, water, labour and money.

Example: 100 ha of land with 150 ha cropped in the year gives a cropping intensity of 150 %. Maharashtra’s cropping intensity is only about 127 %, because most land is rainfed.

Watch out

Mixed cropping has no row arrangement – seeds are mixed and sown together. Intercropping always has a definite row ratio. In the exam, always mention the row proportion when you give an intercropping example.

Monocropping (sole cropping)

One crop is grown alone on a field. Monocropping repeats the same crop on the same land year after year. It suits places where one crop fits the climate, soil, machines and market.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Simple to manage; easy to mechanise; specialised skill; easy to marketPest and disease build-up; soil nutrients run down; high market risk; more fertiliser needed

Sequential cropping

Two or more crops are grown one after another on the same land in a year; the next crop is sown after the earlier one is harvested. Examples: rice–wheat, maize–wheat, rice–rice. Two crops a year is double cropping, three is triple cropping, four is quadruple cropping.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Higher cropping intensity; better use of land and labour; more income per yearNeeds timely harvest and sowing; heavy nutrient removal; needs irrigation

Mixed, intercropping and strip patterns

Examples: sorghum + pigeon pea, maize + cowpea, groundnut + pigeon pea.

Relay cropping

  • Second crop sown in the standing first crop.
  • Example: lathyrus (utera / paira) in rice.
  • Uses leftover soil moisture.
  • First crop may be damaged at sowing.

Why relay? It gives the second crop an early start when the gap after harvest is too short for land preparation.

Ratoon cropping

  • A new crop regrows from the stubble after harvest.
  • Examples: sugarcane, sorghum (also banana, rice in some areas).
  • Saves seed, planting and land preparation.
  • Yield falls after a few ratoons.

Good ratoon care: cut the stubble close to the ground, fill gaps, apply fertiliser and irrigate soon after harvest.

Multi-tier (multistoried) cropping

  • Crops of different heights and root depths grow together in layers.
  • Sunlight and soil layers are used efficiently.
  • Common in coconut and arecanut gardens (e.g. coconut – banana – black pepper – ginger/pineapple).
  • Short, shade-tolerant crops suit the lower tiers.

16.3 Advantages of intercropping and its measurement

Land equivalent ratio (LER)

  1. In simple wordsA number that tells how much more land you would need with sole crops to get the same yield as an intercrop.
  2. Scientific definitionThe relative land area under sole crops required to produce the same yields as obtained from intercropping at the same management level: LER = (Yab/Yaa) + (Yba/Ybb).
  3. Standard definition · key factLER > 1 shows intercropping advantage.
  4. One-line (exam)LER = Σ (intercrop yield ÷ sole crop yield).

  • Higher total yield per unit area and better use of light, water and nutrients (complementarity).

  • Insurance against risk – if one crop fails due to drought or pests, the other gives some return.

  • Nitrogen economy – legume component fixes N and benefits the cereal.

  • Weed suppression by quick canopy cover; less soil erosion.

  • Pest reduction – the companion crop acts as barrier or trap crop.

  • Better income distribution – harvests at different times.

The advantage of intercropping is measured by the Land Equivalent Ratio (LER):

LER = (Yield of crop A in intercrop ÷ yield of A in sole crop) + (Yield of crop B in intercrop ÷ yield of B in sole crop). LER > 1 means intercropping is advantageous: an LER of 1.35 means sole crops would need 35 % more land to produce the same yields.

Worked example

Sole soybean = 2,000 kg/ha; sole tur = 1,500 kg/ha. In a 4:2 intercrop, soybean gives 1,600 kg/ha and tur 750 kg/ha.
LER = 1600/2000 + 750/1500 = 0.80 + 0.50 = 1.30 → a 30 % land advantage.

16.4 Crop rotation

Crop rotation

  1. In simple wordsChanging the crops grown on a field in a planned order year after year.
  2. Scientific definitionThe process of growing different crops in recurrent succession on the same land over a definite period, with the object of getting maximum profit with least investment without impairing soil fertility.
  3. Standard definition · key factGood rotations include legumes, alternate deep- and shallow-rooted and exhaustive and restorative crops.
  4. One-line (exam)Crop rotation = planned recurrent sequence of crops.

Crop rotation is the growing of different crops on the same piece of land in a planned, regular sequence over a period of time, so as to get maximum profit with least damage to soil fertility.

PrincipleReason / example
Deep-rooted crop after shallow-rootedUses nutrients and water from different soil layers (cotton after wheat)
Legume after cereal (exhaustive crop)Legume restores N (chickpea after rice; soybean before wheat)
Exhaustive crop followed by restorative cropSugarcane, potato are exhaustive; pulses and green manure are restorative
Crops of different familiesBreaks the life cycle of pests, diseases and weeds (e.g. Orobanche in mustard, wilt in tur)
Include a fodder or green-manure cropImproves soil organic matter and supports livestock
Fit farmer resources and marketLabour peaks, irrigation, equipment and prices must suit the rotation

  • Advantages: maintains soil fertility and structure, reduces pests, diseases and weeds, spreads labour, improves input efficiency, stabilises income.

  • Disadvantages of mono-cropping avoided: nutrient mining, build-up of soil pathogens (e.g. sugarcane nematodes), resistant weeds and declining yields (“soil sickness”).

Crop rotation – as taught in class

Growing different crops in a planned, repeating order on the same field: (1) cereal, e.g. wheat → (2) legume, e.g. chickpea → (3) root crop, e.g. potato → (4) fodder or green manure, e.g. berseem, sesbania. Historic example: the Norfolk four-course rotation of wheat, turnips, barley and clover (England, 18th century).

Principles: (1) follow a cereal with a legume; (2) follow shallow-rooted crops with deep-rooted crops; (3) alternate heavy feeders with light feeders; (4) do not repeat crops of the same family.

The role of legumes

  • Legume roots host Rhizobium bacteria in nodules.
  • The bacteria turn nitrogen gas from the air into forms plants can use (biological N fixation).
  • Roots and residues leave nitrogen in the soil for the next crop.
  • A legume in the rotation can cut the nitrogen fertiliser the next crop needs.

16.5 Important cropping systems in Maharashtra and India

Cropping intensity

  1. In simple wordsHow many times the same land is cropped in a year.
  2. Scientific definitionThe ratio of gross cropped area to net sown area, expressed as a percentage: Cropping intensity = (gross cropped area ÷ net sown area) × 100.
  3. Standard definition · key factIndia’s cropping intensity is about 155 %; 200 % means two crops a year on all land.
  4. One-line (exam)Cropping intensity = GCA ÷ NSA × 100.
Region / situationCropping system

Marathwada (semi-arid)Soybean–chickpea; bajra + tur; kharif fallow – rabi jowar

Konkan (high rainfall, laterite)Rice – fallow; rice – cowpea/groundnut (residual moisture); rice – vegetables
Dryland scarcity zone (Solapur, Ahilyanagar)Kharif fallow – rabi jowar; bajra + tur; sunflower in rabi

Integrated farming systems

Small farmers in Maharashtra combine crops with dairy, goats, poultry, fish ponds and horticulture. Residues of one enterprise become inputs for another: crop residue → fodder → dung → compost/biogas → field. Such integrated farming systems raise income and reduce risk on farms smaller than 2 ha.

Try it yourself

In your own village, find one intercropped field. Count the row ratio, ask the farmer the yields of both crops and what he would harvest from sole crops. Calculate the LER. Is intercropping really giving him more?

Facts & amazing information

1

India's cropping intensity rose from about 111 % in 1950–51 to about 155 % today.

2

The rice–wheat system of the Indo-Gangetic plains feeds over 400 million people in South Asia.

3

Tur sown with soybean grows slowly for the first 60 days, so the two crops hardly compete until soybean is harvested – a perfect temporal complementarity.

4

Sugarcane monoculture for many years can raise soil nematode and termite populations and lower soil organic carbon.

5

Ancient Indian farmers mixed up to 12 crops (‘baranaja’) in Uttarakhand to reduce risk – a living example of mixed cropping.

6

Crop rotation with legumes can save 30–60 kg N/ha for the following cereal crop.

7

The Norfolk four-course rotation (wheat – turnip – barley – clover) of 18th-century England ended the practice of leaving one-third of the land fallow every year.

8

Maharashtra’s cropping intensity is only about 127 %, compared with about 190 % in Punjab – because most of its land depends on rain.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Explain different types of cropping systems with examples. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Sole, mixed, intercropping, strip, relay, sequential, ratoon, alley; definitions and examples.

  2. Define catch, cover, trap, smother, border and companion crops with one example each, and list the factors that decide the choice of a cropping system. (5 marks · BTL-1 · CO2)

    Answer points: Fodder cowpea; cowpea; marigold in tomato; sweet potato; border rows; onion with carrot; climate, soil, water, market, resources, farm family.

  3. What is crop rotation? State its principles and advantages. (7 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Definition; principles (deep/shallow, legume/cereal, exhaustive/restorative, families, fodder, resources); advantages.

  4. A farmer obtains 1,700 kg/ha soybean and 800 kg/ha tur in a 4:2 intercrop, against sole yields of 2,100 kg/ha and 1,450 kg/ha. Calculate the LER, interpret it, and critically discuss the limitations of LER as an index of intercropping advantage. (8 marks · BTL-3 Apply · CO2)

    Answer points: LER = 0.81 + 0.55 = 1.36; 36 % land advantage; limitations – ignores duration (ATER), prices, inputs, risk; complementary indices.

  5. Analyse the biological mechanisms – spatial, temporal and nutritional complementarity – that make cereal–legume intercropping more productive than sole cropping. Illustrate with Indian examples. (8 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Canopy architecture, rooting depth, duration differences, N transfer, competitive ratio; soybean + tur, sorghum + tur, maize + cowpea; facilitation vs competition.

  6. Classify cropping systems. Explain monocropping, sequential, relay, ratoon and multi-tier cropping with advantages, limitations and examples. (8 marks · BTL-4 · CO2)

    Answer points: Tree: monocropping and multiple cropping (mixed, inter, sequential, relay, ratoon, multi-tier); rice–wheat; lathyrus in rice; sugarcane ratoon and ratoon care; coconut–banana–pepper–ginger.

  7. Discuss the concept of an integrated farming system and evaluate its role in improving income and resilience of small and marginal farmers in India. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Components, resource recycling, employment, risk reduction, nutrition, examples (crop + dairy + poultry + fish), constraints.

  8. “The rice–wheat system has become a victim of its own success.” Critically evaluate the sustainability issues of this system and suggest diversification options. (12 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Groundwater decline, soil degradation, nutrient imbalance, residue burning, Phalaris minor, yield plateau; options – DSR, zero-till, pulses/maize diversification, Happy Seeder, conservation agriculture.

  9. Design a sustainable three-year crop rotation for an irrigated 3-ha farm on medium-deep black soil in western Maharashtra including sugarcane. Justify each crop in the sequence using rotation principles. (12 marks · BTL-6 Create · CO2)

    Answer points: Sugarcane (adsali) – ratoon – soybean – wheat/chickpea – green manure (sunhemp) – sugarcane; legume, family break, exhaustive–restorative balance, labour, market.
