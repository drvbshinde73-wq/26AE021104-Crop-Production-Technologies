The science that feeds 1.4 billion people

India harvested about 330 million tonnes of food grains in recent years – roughly five times what it produced in 1950, from nearly the same land. No new continent was found and no extra rain fell. The difference came from agronomy: better seeds sown at the right time, at the right spacing, with the right water and nutrients. This lesson explains what agronomy is, where it came from, and why every agricultural engineer must think like an agronomist.

11.1 Meaning and definition of agronomy

The word agronomy comes from two Greek words: agros = field and nomos = to manage. Literally, agronomy is the art and science of managing the field.

Definition. Agronomy is the branch of agricultural science that deals with the principles and practices of field-crop production and soil management to obtain the maximum economic yield per unit area, per unit time and per unit input, while keeping the soil productive for future generations.

Three ideas are packed into this definition:

  • Principles – the scientific reasons behind a practice (why a crop needs a certain spacing, temperature or nutrient).

  • Practices – the field operations themselves: tillage, sowing, irrigation, weeding, fertilizer application and harvesting.

  • Sustainability – production today must not degrade the soil, water or environment tomorrow.

Other useful definitions:

  • Agronomy is the science of manipulating the crop–soil–climate complex so as to get the maximum yield.

  • Agronomy is a synthesis of several disciplines – soil science, plant physiology, agricultural meteorology, plant breeding, entomology, pathology and agricultural engineering – applied to field crops.

Explained simply

Think of a crop as a factory. Soil science studies the land the factory stands on, plant breeding designs the machines (varieties), meteorology forecasts the power supply (sunlight and rain). The agronomist is the factory manager who decides when to switch on, how much raw material to use and how to keep production high without wearing the machinery out.

Indian agriculture is at least 10,000 years old. Ancient texts describe agronomic ideas that are still taught today.

In the field

Many traditional sayings are agronomy in disguise. Marathi farmers say “Rohini nakshatra–t perni, Mrug–at ugavan” – sow around the Rohini/Mrig nakshatra (late May – June) so that seedlings emerge with the first monsoon showers. Modern agro-advisories say the same: sow kharif crops after at least 75–100 mm of cumulative rain.

Where engineering meets agronomy

Every machine an agricultural engineer designs answers an agronomic need: a seed drill must place seed at the depth and spacing the agronomist specifies; a drip system must deliver the water the crop needs at each growth stage; a harvester must cut at physiological maturity with minimum grain loss. Good machines start with good agronomy.

11.4 Importance of agronomy in India

  • Food and nutrition security – feeding the world's most populous country from only about 2.4 % of the world's land area.

  • Livelihood – about 45 % of India's workforce depends on agriculture.

  • Economy – agriculture and allied sectors contribute roughly 16–18 % of India's GDP and supply raw material to sugar, textile, oil and food-processing industries.

  • Resource-use efficiency – with shrinking land and water, more output must come from each hectare, each drop and each kilogram of fertilizer.

  • Sustainability – correcting the side effects of intensive farming: declining soil organic carbon, falling water tables, salinity and residue burning.

  • Climate resilience – drought-, flood- and heat-tolerant practices protect farmers from erratic monsoons.

11.5 Role of an agronomist

  • Identify the best crop and variety for a given soil, climate and market.

  • Fix the time and method of sowing, seed rate and plant geometry.

  • Plan nutrient, water and weed management for each growth stage.

  • Design cropping systems and rotations that keep soil healthy.

  • Develop contingency plans for delayed monsoon, dry spells or floods.

  • Test new technologies in farmers' fields (front-line demonstrations) and transfer them through extension.

  • Advise policy – minimum support price, crop insurance, crop diversification.

Watch out

Agronomy ≠ agriculture. Agriculture is the whole enterprise (crops, livestock, fisheries, forestry, engineering, economics). Agronomy is the part that deals with field crops and soil management. Horticulture deals with fruits, vegetables, flowers, spices and plantation crops – that is Unit III.

Try it yourself

Ask a farmer in your village three questions: When did you sow your last soybean or jowar crop? How much seed did you use per acre? What did you harvest? Convert the figures to per-hectare values (1 ha = 2.47 acres) and compare them with the university recommendation. The gap you find is the yield gap that agronomy tries to close.

Facts & amazing information

1

India is the world's largest producer of milk, pulses and jute, and the second-largest producer of rice, wheat, sugarcane, cotton, fruits and vegetables.

2

The semi-dwarf wheat variety Sonalika and the rice variety IR 8 (“miracle rice”) launched the Green Revolution in the late 1960s.

3

Dr Norman Borlaug, whose wheat varieties transformed Indian farming, received the Nobel Peace Prize in 1970 – the only agricultural scientist to do so for crop research.

4

Kautilya's Arthashastra (c. 300 BC) described a rain-gauge (varshamana) nearly 2,000 years before rain gauges appeared in Europe.

5

India's average yield of rice is about 2.8 t/ha, while China's is about 7 t/ha – a reminder of how much yield gap agronomy can still close.

6

The term ‘agronomy’ was first widely used in France in the 18th century; India's first Agronomy department was set up at the Imperial Agricultural Research Institute, Pusa.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Write the scope of agronomy in present-day Indian agriculture. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Crop production, soil fertility, water, weed management, cropping systems, dryland farming, climate-smart and precision agriculture, organic farming.

  2. Explain the role of an agronomist. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Choice of crop/variety, sowing time and method, nutrient–water–weed plans, cropping systems, contingency plans, testing and transfer of technology, policy advice.

  3. Trace the historical development of agronomy in India from the Vedic period to the Green Revolution and beyond. Critically evaluate how each phase changed the objectives of agronomy. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Ancient texts (Rig Veda, Arthashastra, Krishi Parashara, Brihat Samhita, Vrikshayurveda); colonial institutions (Pusa 1905, ICAR 1929); SAUs; Green Revolution – objective shifted to yield maximisation; present – sustainability, resource-use efficiency, climate resilience.

  4. “Agronomy is the mother of all agricultural sciences.” Justify this statement by explaining the interrelationship of agronomy with other disciplines. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Synthesis role; soil science, physiology, breeding, meteorology, protection, engineering, economics and extension contributions; examples; agronomist integrates them in field-level decisions.

  5. Critically analyse why India's crop yields remain well below world averages despite the Green Revolution. Suggest agronomic strategies to bridge the yield gap. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Small holdings, rainfed area, poor seed replacement, imbalanced fertilization, low water-use efficiency, late sowing, weeds, residue burning, extension gaps; strategies – timely sowing, quality seed, INM, micro-irrigation, mechanisation, precision tools, crop diversification.

  6. Examine the statement that ‘future gains in food production must come from resource-use efficiency rather than from area expansion’. Discuss with reference to land, water and nutrients. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Fixed net sown area; per-capita land decline; groundwater depletion; low N-use efficiency (30–40 %); options – drip/sprinkler, fertigation, site-specific nutrient management, conservation agriculture, cropping intensity.

  7. Discuss how the objectives of agronomy have shifted from ‘maximum production’ to ‘sustainable and climate-resilient production’. Support your answer with Indian examples. (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Green Revolution side effects (Punjab groundwater, Indo-Gangetic soil fatigue, stubble burning); new goals – conservation agriculture, SRI, DSR, natural farming, crop diversification, climate-resilient varieties, contingency planning.

science and practice of field-crop production and soil management
Yield gap
difference between potential (research-station) yield and farmers' actual yield
Green Revolution
rapid rise in wheat and rice yields after 1966 from HYVs, fertilizer and irrigation
Sustainable agriculture
farming that meets present needs without harming future productivity
Precision agriculture
applying inputs at the right place, rate and time using GPS, sensors and data
