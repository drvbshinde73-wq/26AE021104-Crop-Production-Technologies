Why every farmer is an agronomist

The word agronomy comes from the Greek agros (field) and nomos (to manage). Every decision a farmer takes – which crop, which variety, when to sow, how much seed, fertilizer and water, how to control weeds and when to harvest – is an agronomic decision. Agronomy is the science that turns soil, seed, water and sunlight into food, fibre and fuel for 1.4 billion Indians.

11.1 Meaning and definitions of agronomy

Agronomy is the branch of agricultural science that deals with the principles and practices of field-crop production and soil management to obtain the highest economic yield while maintaining the productivity of the land.

AuthorityDefinition
LyonAgronomy is the science of crop production and soil management.
Hughes and HensonIt is the branch of agriculture that deals with the theory and practice of field-crop production and soil management.
Martin and LeonardAgronomy is that branch of agriculture dealing with field-crop production and soil management.
Modern (sustainability) viewAn integrated science of soil, water, weather, crop, nutrient and pest management for profitable and sustainable production.

Explained simply

Soil science tells us what the soil is, plant breeding gives the seed, meteorology predicts the weather – and agronomy decides how to grow that seed in that soil under that weather for the best harvest.

11.2 Agronomy – a science, an art and a business

AspectMeaningExample
ScienceBased on systematic knowledge, experiments and principlesFertilizer-response trials, irrigation scheduling by IW/CPE ratio
ArtSkill and judgement gained by experienceJudging the right soil moisture (‘vapsa’) for sowing; reading crop colour
BusinessProduction for profit – cost, return and marketChoosing soybean or cotton by expected price and cost of cultivation

Key point

The aim of agronomy is maximum economic yield (MEY) – not the biological maximum – at the lowest cost per unit of produce, without degrading soil and water.

11.3 History of agronomy in India

PeriodDevelopments
Indus–Saraswati civilisation (3300–1300 BC)Wheat, barley, peas, sesame and the world's earliest cotton; furrowed field at Kalibangan (Rajasthan); granaries and bunds
Vedic period (1500–500 BC)Ox-drawn plough (sira), manuring with cow dung, rice and barley; Rigveda and Atharvaveda describe sowing, harvesting and pest control
Kautilya's Arthashastra (≈ 300 BC)Rain-gauge (varshaman), crop seasons by rainfall, seed treatment, irrigation tax; Sitadhyaksha – superintendent of agriculture
Classical texts (400–1100 AD)Krishi-Parashara – weather forecasting, seed storage, ploughing; Vrikshayurveda of Surapala – plant nutrition and protection
Medieval periodPersian wheel, canals (Firoz Shah Tughlaq); new crops – maize, chilli, potato, groundnut, tobacco
British periodRoyal Commission on Agriculture (1926–28); Imperial Agricultural Research Institute, Pusa (1905) → IARI New Delhi (1936); Imperial Council of Agricultural Research (1929) → ICAR
1947–1965Grow More Food campaign, first agricultural university at Pantnagar (1960), IADP (1961), AICRP projects
Green Revolution (1966 onwards)Semi-dwarf HYV wheat (Kalyan Sona, Sonalika) and rice (IR-8, Jaya), fertilizer and irrigation; Dr M. S. Swaminathan, Dr N. E. Borlaug, C. Subramaniam
1970–2000Dryland agronomy (CRIDA 1985), cropping-systems research, watershed development, IPM
2000–todayConservation agriculture, Bt cotton (2002), Soil Health Card (2015), precision and digital agronomy, natural farming

11.5 Importance of agronomy in India

  • Food and nutrition security – India feeds about 18 % of the world's population from 2.4 % of its land; foodgrain output rose from about 51 million t (1950–51) to over 330 million t.
  • Livelihood – agriculture employs about 46 % of India's workforce and contributes about 18 % of gross value added.
  • Raw material for industry – cotton for textiles, sugarcane for sugar and ethanol, oilseeds for edible oil.
  • Resource-use efficiency – more crop per drop of water, per kilogram of fertilizer and per rupee spent.
  • Sustainability – soil and water conservation, residue management and reduced pollution.
  • Climate resilience – contingency crop plans, drought- and heat-tolerant varieties, adjusted sowing dates.

11.6 Relation of agronomy with other sciences

Remember

An agronomist is like the captain of a cricket team: every player (science) has a special skill, but the captain decides how to combine them to win – a high, profitable and sustainable yield.

11.7 Basic principles of agronomy

  1. Planning and selection of crops and varieties according to agro-climate and market.
  2. Proper tillage and seedbed preparation for good germination and root growth.
  3. Timely sowing with the right seed rate, spacing and seed treatment.
  4. Balanced and integrated nutrient management based on soil testing.
  5. Efficient water management with drainage where needed.
  6. Timely weed, pest and disease management (IPM).
  7. Suitable cropping systems and crop rotation to sustain soil fertility.
  8. Harvest at physiological maturity and proper post-harvest handling.
  9. Conservation of natural resources for future generations.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Define agronomy and critically examine why it is described simultaneously as a science, an art and a business, citing field examples from Indian agriculture. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Definitions (Lyon, Hughes & Henson); science – principles and experiments; art – skill, judgement of moisture; business – cost, MEY, market; examples; conclusion on MEY and sustainability.

  2. Trace the evolution of agronomy in India from the Indus civilisation to the era of precision and conservation agriculture, and analyse the turning points that changed crop productivity. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Indus, Vedic, Arthashastra, Krishi-Parashara; medieval crops; Pusa, ICAR; Green Revolution – HYV, fertilizer, irrigation; dryland research; CA, Bt cotton, SHC; analysis of productivity jumps.

  3. Discuss the scope of agronomy in the context of rainfed farming, resource conservation and climate change in India. (10 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Crops and varieties, establishment, soil, nutrient, water, weed management, cropping systems; 51 % rainfed area; moisture conservation; CA; climate-resilient practices; new frontiers.

  4. “Agronomy is the mother branch of agriculture.” Justify this statement by explaining its relationship with allied sciences, with a neat diagram. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Integration of soil science, meteorology, breeding, physiology, entomology, pathology, engineering, economics, statistics; diagram; examples of each link.

  5. Explain the basic principles of agronomy and apply them to plan the cultivation of a kharif crop on a medium-black soil of Maharashtra. (10 marks · BTL-3 Apply · CO2)

    Answer points: Nine principles; application to soybean or cotton – variety, seedbed, sowing at 75–100 mm rain, seed treatment, fertilizer, BBF/ridge, weed control, IPM, harvest.
