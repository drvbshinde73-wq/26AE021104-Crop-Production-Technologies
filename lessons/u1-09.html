One number that controls everything

A farmer applies the same fertilizer on two fields. One field gives a bumper crop; the other shows yellow leaves and poor growth. The soil test reveals the reason: pH 6.8 in the first field and 8.9 in the second. Soil reaction (pH) is like the master switch of the soil – it decides which nutrients are available, which are locked up, which microbes are active and even which crops can grow at all.

9.1 What is soil reaction?

Soil reaction is the degree of acidity or alkalinity of a soil. It is measured as the pH of a soil–water suspension. It is considered the single most important chemical property of soil because it controls nutrient supply, toxicity and microbial activity.

Acidic Neutral Alkaline
pH Below 7 About 7 Above 7
H⁺ vs OH⁻ H⁺ > OH⁻ H⁺ = OH⁻ OH⁻ > H⁺
Where Humid, high-rainfall regions — Arid and semi-arid regions
Why Rain leaches Ca, Mg, K, Na; H⁺ takes over exchange sites Balanced Bases (Ca, Mg, Na) accumulate; Na₂CO₃ hydrolyses giving OH⁻, pH may reach 9–10

Explained simply

Soil reaction simply means how acidic or alkaline the soil is. It depends on the balance between hydrogen ions (H⁺, which make soil acidic) and hydroxyl ions (OH⁻, which make it alkaline). When they are equal, the soil is neutral (pH 7).

9.2 pH is a logarithmic scale

pH = −log₁₀ [H⁺] (Sørensen), where [H⁺] is in mol/L (or g/L). At neutrality [H⁺] = 10⁻⁷, so pH = 7. Water: Kw = [H⁺][OH⁻] = 10⁻¹⁴, so pH + pOH = 14.

  • Each pH unit = 10-fold change in H⁺. pH 6 has 10 × more H⁺ than pH 7; pH 5 has 100 ×; pH 4 has 1000 ×.

  • Example: [H⁺] = 4.2 × 10⁻⁶ → pH = 6 − log 4.2 = 6 − 0.62 = 5.38.

One step = ten times

The pH scale is logarithmic: each one-unit drop means 10 times more acidity. So a soil of pH 5 is 10 times more acidic than pH 6, and 100 times more acidic than pH 7. This is why changing pH by even one unit needs a lot of lime and is a big deal for crops.

Like the Richter scale for earthquakes, a small change in the number means a big change in the real strength.

Common mistake

pH 4 is not "twice as acidic" as pH 8, and pH 6 is not "slightly" more acidic than pH 7 – it is ten times more. Always think in powers of ten.

9.3 Sources of soil acidity

  • Natural processes: decomposition of organic matter forms organic acids; root and microbial respiration produce CO₂ → carbonic acid; nitrification of ammonium releases H⁺: NH₄⁺ + 2O₂ → NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + H₂O.

  • Acidic parent material: granite, sandstone, shale – low in bases; highly weathered tropical soils.

  • Acid rain/atmospheric deposition: SO₂ and NOₓ form sulphuric and nitric acids.

  • Leaching of basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na) by heavy rain – leaves H⁺ and Al³⁺ behind.

  • Acid-forming fertilizers: urea, ammonium sulphate, ammonium nitrate: (NH₂)₂CO + 4O₂ → 2NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + CO₂ + H₂O (ultimately).

  • Crop removal of bases; continuous cropping without returning OM.

How soils become acidic

Heavy rain is the main culprit: it washes out basic cations (Ca, Mg, K, Na) year after year, and H⁺ and Al³⁺ take their place. Other sources: CO₂ from roots and microbes forming carbonic acid, organic acids from decaying matter, acid-forming fertilizers (ammonium sulphate, urea through nitrification) and acid rain. That is why the high-rainfall Konkan has acid soils while the dry Deccan does not.

9.4 Sources of soil alkalinity

  • Parent material: limestone, dolomite, calcite, Ca–Mg-rich minerals release bases and carbonates.

  • Accumulation of carbonates/bicarbonates: CaCO₃, MgCO₃, Na₂CO₃, NaHCO₃ build up when leaching is limited (arid and semi-arid).

  • Poor-quality irrigation water high in Na⁺, CO₃²⁻, HCO₃⁻.

  • Evaporation and capillary rise bring alkaline groundwater and salts to the surface.

  • Excess sodium inputs: sodium nitrate, industrial effluents, fly ash.

  • Inadequate leaching/poor drainage and waterlogging.

How soils become alkaline

In low-rainfall areas the bases are not washed away; instead, water evaporates and leaves calcium carbonate, sodium carbonate and other salts behind. Irrigation with water rich in bicarbonate or sodium, and poor drainage, make the problem worse – common in canal-irrigated parts of western Maharashtra.

9.5 Three kinds of acidity

Kind What it is How big
Active H⁺ ions in the soil solution – what roots and microbes feel; measured as soil pH Tiny – at pH 4 only about 2 kg CaCO₃/ha needed to neutralise (furrow slice)
Exchangeable H⁺ and Al³⁺ adsorbed on colloids; released by an unbuffered salt such as KCl About 100 × the active acidity
Residual H and Al tightly bound to organic matter and clay (can be released only slowly or by lime) Largest: 1,000 × (sandy) to 100,000 × (clayey) the active acidity; lime need 4–8 t/ha

The three are in equilibrium: when lime neutralises active acidity, exchangeable and residual acidity release more H⁺ to replace it. That is why large amounts of lime are needed.

Active acidity is the tip of an iceberg; most of the acidity is hidden below the “water” (on and inside colloids).

9.6 Buffering capacity

Buffering capacity is the soil’s ability to resist a change in pH when acid or base is added. Add the same amount of HCl to pure water (pH 7 → ~4) and to a soil suspension (pH 7 → ~6.5–6.8): the soil changes much less because colloids absorb H⁺ by exchange. Buffering is higher with more clay, more clay colloids/organic matter and higher CEC. Sandy, low-clay soils are weakly buffered and change pH quickly. Importance: stabilises pH, moderates sudden changes in nutrient availability and protects roots from pH stress.

The shock absorber of soil

Buffering capacity is the soil's resistance to change in pH – like a shock absorber on a bike that smooths out bumps. When you add acid or lime, the exchange sites on clay and humus release or absorb H⁺ and keep the pH from swinging wildly. Clayey, humus-rich soils are highly buffered (need more lime to change pH); sandy soils are poorly buffered (pH changes quickly). This protects plants and microbes from sudden changes.

9.8 Significance of soil pH

  • Overall: most nutrients are most available at pH 6.0–7.5 (the “sweet spot”).

  • Nitrogen: maximum availability at pH 6–8, because that is the best range for microbes that mineralise N and fix it symbiotically.

  • Phosphorus: maximum at pH 6.5–7.5. Below 6.5, Fe and Al in solution form insoluble Fe/Al phosphates; above ~7.5–8, Ca phosphates precipitate. High P availability again at pH > 8.5 as soluble sodium phosphates.

  • Iron and manganese: availability increases with acidity (more soluble) – can become toxic below pH 5.5; become deficient at high pH.

  • Zn, Cu, B: most available in slightly acidic to neutral soils; deficiency in alkaline/calcareous soils. Molybdenum: opposite – more available at higher pH.

  • Ca, Mg, K: low in strongly acid soils (leached); adequate above pH 6.

  • Aluminium: toxic below pH 5.0–5.5; roots are damaged.

  • Microorganisms: most work best at pH 6.0–7.5; nitrifying bacteria are inhibited below ~5.5 and above ~9; fungi tolerate acid soils.

  • Earthworms are inhibited by high acidity.

Acidic soils: apply lime (CaCO₃, Ca(OH)₂, dolomite). Alkaline soils: apply gypsum, elemental sulphur, organic matter; grow tolerant crops.

Ideal pH for most crops: 6.3–6.8 (range 6.0–7.5).

Using pH to make decisions

  • pH below 5.5: aluminium and manganese toxicity, P fixed by Fe and Al – apply lime; grow tolerant crops such as rice, tea, potato, pineapple.
  • pH 6.5–7.5: the sweet spot – most nutrients available, most crops happy.
  • pH above 8.5: sodium problems, poor structure, deficiency of Zn, Fe, Mn, B and P – apply gypsum and organic manures; grow tolerant crops such as barley, cotton, sugar beet.

Plants are chemists that work with only 18 ingredients

A sugarcane plant can grow 3 metres tall and produce sweet juice, fibre and leaves – all from sunlight, air, water and just a handful of chemical elements taken from the soil. Scientists have found that plants need exactly 18 elements to complete their life. Remove any one, and the plant cannot complete its life cycle, however much of the others you supply. This part of the lecture is about those 18 essential nutrients.

9.9 Introduction

Plants need chemical elements to build their bodies and run their processes. They take them mostly from the soil (and air and water) as ions. Only a tiny fraction (< 0.2 %) of soil mineral nutrients is dissolved in soil water; about 98 % is locked in organic matter, humus, insoluble compounds and minerals (slow reserve), and about 2 % is adsorbed on colloids. These pools stay in dynamic equilibrium, so as roots remove nutrients from the solution, colloids and reserves refill it.

Liebig's barrel

Imagine a wooden barrel made of staves of different heights, each stave being one nutrient. Water can only fill up to the shortest stave. In the same way, crop yield is limited by the nutrient in shortest supply – Liebig's Law of the Minimum. Adding more nitrogen will not help if zinc is the short stave.

9.10 Criteria of essentiality (Arnon and Stout, 1939; Arnon 1954)

  • The plant cannot complete its life cycle (grow and reproduce normally) without the element.

  • The element is specific – no other element can replace it.

  • The element has a direct role in plant metabolism (not just correcting some bad soil condition).

Because this is strict, Nicholas proposed “functional or metabolic nutrients”. Elements like Na, V, Si, Se, Al, I that help some plants are called beneficial elements (their essentiality is not proven for all plants; they help at very low concentrations).

Three questions to test an element

Arnon and Stout asked three simple questions: (1) Can the plant complete its life without it? If not – (2) Can any other element do its job? If not – (3) Is it directly involved in the plant's own metabolism? If yes to all, the element is essential. Sodium helps some plants but fails these tests for most crops, so it is called beneficial, not essential.

9.11 Classification of essential nutrients

18 essential elements – C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni and Co. Nine are macronutrients (C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S) and nine are micronutrients (Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni, Co). Nickel (Brown et al., 1987) and cobalt (needed for biological N₂ fixation in legume nodules) are the most recent additions to the list.

Group Nutrients Mainly from Typical plant concentration
Structural (non-mineral) C, H, O Air (CO₂) and water ~ 90 % of dry matter (45 % C, 45 % O, 6 % H)
Primary macronutrients N, P, K Soil N 1.5 %, P 0.2 %, K 1.0 %
Secondary macronutrients Ca, Mg, S Soil Ca 0.5 %, Mg 0.2 %, S 0.1 %
Micronutrients (trace) Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni, Co Soil Fe 100, Mn 20, Zn 20, B 20, Cu 6, Cl 100, Ni 0.1, Mo 0.1, Co < 0.1 mg/kg

  • Metals: K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu (Mo, Ni, Co). Non-metals: N, P, S, B, Cl.

  • Absorbed as cations: NH₄⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺, Ni²⁺, Co²⁺. As anions: NO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻, SO₄²⁻, BO₃³⁻ / H₃BO₃, MoO₄²⁻, Cl⁻.

Macro vs micro does not mean important vs unimportant

Macronutrients are needed in large amounts (kilograms per hectare); micronutrients in tiny amounts (grams per hectare). But both are equally essential. A crop short of a few grams of zinc can fail just as badly as one short of nitrogen. Think of salt in cooking: you need only a pinch, but without it the meal is ruined.

Where the 18 come from

C, H and O come free from air and water (about 95 % of the dry weight of a plant!). The other 15 come from the soil – and these are the ones farmers manage with fertilizers and manures.

Why does mobility in the plant matter? Mobile nutrients move from old leaves to new growth, so deficiency shows on OLD (lower) leaves (N, P, K, Mg). Immobile nutrients stay put, so deficiency shows on YOUNG (upper) leaves and growing tips (Ca, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B).

Explained simply – three pockets of money

Nutrients in soil are like a person's money: cash in hand (soil solution – immediately available), savings account (exchangeable ions on clay and humus – available soon) and fixed deposit / property (minerals and organic matter – released slowly over months or years). Crops spend the cash first; the soil keeps refilling it from the savings and deposits.

9.13 How nutrients reach the root

  • Mass flow: nutrient ions move along with water that the plant is drawing in (important for NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺).

  • Diffusion: ions move from high to low concentration towards the root surface that has depleted them (key for P and K).

  • Root interception / contact exchange: roots physically grow into contact with colloids and directly exchange ions (small contribution).

Three delivery routes

Root interception – the root grows to the nutrient (like walking to the shop). Mass flow – nutrients come dissolved in the water the plant is drinking (like a delivery van on a river). Diffusion – nutrients move slowly from a high to a low concentration near the root (like the smell of food spreading in a room). Nitrate moves easily by mass flow; phosphorus and potassium depend mostly on diffusion, which is why P fertilizer should be placed near the root zone.

Hidden hunger: mild deficiency may show no visible symptoms yet still cut yields noticeably. It is detected only by plant tissue analysis or yield decline.

Reading the leaves in the field

  • Yellowing of older lower leaves starting from the tip = nitrogen deficiency (common in maize and rice).
  • Purple or reddish older leaves = phosphorus deficiency (often seen in young maize in cold weather).
  • Scorched, brown leaf margins on older leaves = potassium deficiency.
  • Yellow young leaves with green veins = iron deficiency (common on calcareous black soils, e.g., in sugarcane and groundnut).
  • Small leaves, short internodes, "khaira" of rice = zinc deficiency.

Remember the mobility rule from the diagram: mobile nutrients show symptoms on old leaves; immobile ones on young leaves.

Nutrient toxicity

Toxicities are most frequent for Mn and B. Excess N gives very dark green, excessive vegetative growth and delayed maturity; excess P induces Zn/Fe deficiency; excess K induces Mg/Ca deficiency; excess Fe/Mn in acid or waterlogged soils gives brown spots; excess B/Cl from irrigation water gives leaf-tip and margin burn.

  • Prevention: (1) liming reduces toxicity of most nutrients (except Mo); (2) follow recommended fertilizer rates and use waste materials (sewage sludge, fly ash) carefully; (3) use tolerant crop species; (4) provide drainage (Fe and Mn become toxic in waterlogged soils); (5) monitor groundwater for B and Cl; dilute if high; (6) add organic matter to bind toxic elements; (7) plough in dry soil and leach with rainwater.

Black gold of the soil

Ask an experienced farmer which field is best and he will point to the one with dark, crumbly, sweet-smelling soil full of earthworms. That darkness and smell come from soil organic matter. Though it is often less than 1–2 % of the soil weight in India, it does more for soil health than anything else. Farmers rightly call FYM and compost the "black gold" of farming.

9.15 What is SOM?

Soil organic matter is the partly decayed plant and animal material in the soil, together with new compounds formed by soil microorganisms. It is typically 1–5 % of the weight of a well-drained topsoil (varies with climate, soil type and land use). It is dynamic: fresh residue (days–weeks) → partly decomposed (months–years) → stable organic matter/humus (years–centuries).

Explained simply

Soil organic matter is everything in the soil that was once alive or is still alive – roots, leaves, dead insects, manure, microbes – at all stages of decay. Over time, microbes turn fresh residues into a dark, stable, spongy substance called humus, which can last for hundreds of years.

9.16 Sources

  • Plant: crop residues (stems, leaves, roots, stubble), root exudates, plant litter from forest/grassland.

  • Animal: dung, urine, bedding, decomposing animal remains, microbial biomass.

  • Other: soil organisms (earthworms, insects, fungi, bacteria) and organic amendments (compost, green manures, biochar).

9.17 Factors affecting the amount of SOM

Factor Effect
Climate Temperature controls decomposition rate (warm = faster loss); rainfall affects moisture and leaching. Cool, moist climates build more OM.
Soil moisture Adequate moisture favours microbes; very dry or waterlogged conditions slow decomposition (peat forms in waterlogged soils)
Vegetation and land use Type of vegetation, amount and quality of residues; natural ecosystems (forest, grassland) have higher OM
Soil properties Clay protects OM (clay > silt > sand); structure, aeration, drainage, pH
Nutrient availability Adequate N, P, K, S support microbial growth
Management Residue addition or removal, tillage intensity (more tillage = more OM loss), manure/compost, crop rotation, cover crops, grazing

Why Indian soils are low in organic matter

High temperatures make microbes work fast, so organic matter is "burnt" quickly. Crop residues are often removed for fodder or burnt in the field, and intensive tillage exposes organic matter to air. That is why most Indian soils have less than 0.5–0.75 % organic carbon. Adding FYM, compost, green manure and crop residues, and reducing tillage, can slowly raise it.

Explained simply – a food chain underground

Decomposition is like a community kitchen. Earthworms, insects and mites first chop residues into small pieces. Bacteria and fungi then digest them, releasing CO₂, water and energy, and converting proteins to ammonium and then nitrate (ammonification and nitrification). Easily digestible parts (sugars, proteins) are eaten first; tough parts like lignin are left and slowly become humus.

Decomposition of water-soluble compounds

  • Carbohydrates: C₆H₁₂O₆ + 6O₂ → 6CO₂ + 6H₂O + energy (aerobic).

  • Nitrogen compounds: proteins → (aminization) amino acids → (ammonification) NH₄⁺ → (nitrification, aerobic) NO₂⁻ → NO₃⁻.

  • Nutrients are either mineralised (released in inorganic forms: NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻, SO₄²⁻) or immobilised (taken up by microbes) depending on microbial demand and C:N ratio.

Decomposition of insoluble compounds

Cellulose, hemicellulose, starch and proteins are broken down by extracellular enzymes (hydrolysis) into simple sugars and amino acids that microbes absorb. Lignin is slowly and partially broken into aromatic units that form humus.

Other products

  • Gases: CO₂, H₂O vapour, CH₄ (methane, only under anaerobic conditions).

  • Low-molecular-weight organic compounds: acetic, formic, citric, oxalic acids; alcohols (methanol, ethanol), aldehydes, acetone; phenolics.

  • Humified compounds: complex, aromatic, resistant, persist for long.

  • Inorganic nutrients: NH₄⁺, NO₂⁻, NO₃⁻; H₂PO₄⁻, HPO₄²⁻; SO₄²⁻.

C:N ratio rule: residues with narrow C:N (< 20–25:1, e.g., legumes) decompose fast and release N. Residues with wide C:N (> 30:1, e.g., straw) make microbes borrow soil N, so N is temporarily immobilised; farmers add extra N when incorporating straw.

Factors affecting decomposition

  • Temperature (low = slow; optimum 25–35 °C = fast).

  • Moisture (too dry or waterlogged = slow; optimum = fast).

  • Aeration (oxygen needed for fast aerobic decomposition).

  • Quality of material: low C:N decomposes faster; high lignin, waxes, tannins decompose slowly.

  • Soil properties: texture, structure, pH (optimum 6–7.5), clay minerals.

  • Nutrient availability (N, P, K, S).

  • Type and activity of microorganisms.

  • Management: residue retention, tillage, crop rotation, organic amendments.

The C:N ratio – a balanced diet for microbes

Microbes need about one part nitrogen for every 20–25 parts carbon to build their bodies. If you add straw with C:N 80:1, the microbes do not get enough N from the straw, so they take N from the soil – and the crop suffers temporary N deficiency (immobilisation). A legume residue with C:N around 15–20 has plenty of N, so it decomposes fast and releases N to the crop (mineralisation).

Practical tip

When incorporating rice or wheat straw, add some extra nitrogen (urea or FYM) or a decomposer culture, and allow time before sowing the next crop. This is also why well-rotted compost (C:N ~ 15–20) is better than fresh straw.

9.20 What decides the amount of SOM?

SOM level = Inputs − Losses + Stabilization. Inputs: crop residues, manures and composts, roots and exudates, green manures. Losses: microbial decomposition (CO₂), leaching of dissolved organic compounds, erosion and runoff. Stabilisation: physical protection inside aggregates, chemical bonding to clay (organo-mineral complexes), and formation of stable humic substances.

One material, many jobs

Organic matter is like a multi-purpose tool: it acts as glue for structure, a sponge for water, a storehouse for N, P and S, a magnet for cations (high CEC), a buffer against pH changes and food for billions of microbes. Increasing organic carbon by even a small amount improves almost every soil property at the same time.

9.22 Humus

Humus is dark-coloured, stable organic material formed during decomposition. It is a complex, heterogeneous mixture of high molecular-weight compounds with aromatic and aliphatic structures and functional groups (carboxyl, phenolic). It is colloidal (large surface area, highly reactive) and is more resistant to further decomposition than fresh residues.

Fraction Molecular weight Colour Solubility
Humic acid High Dark brown to black Soluble in alkali, insoluble in acid
Fulvic acid Lower Light brown to yellow Soluble in both acid and alkali
Humin Highest Black Insoluble in acid and alkali; strongly bound to minerals

Properties: very high CEC, high water holding capacity, chelates micronutrients (keeps them available), buffers pH, colloidal and highly reactive. Importance: improves structure, retains nutrients, increases water holding, reduces erosion, long-term fertility and carbon storage.

Humus vs organic matter

All humus is organic matter, but not all organic matter is humus. Humus is the final, well-decomposed, dark, stable part in which you can no longer recognise leaves or roots. It is colloidal, has a very high CEC (150–300 cmol/kg or more) and can hold several times its own weight of water.

Try it yourself

Buffering experiment: put 50 g sand in one cup and 50 g clayey garden soil in another, add 50 ml water each and the same few drops of vinegar. Test pH (or use the red cabbage indicator) before and after. The sand's pH drops more – it is poorly buffered.

Leaf survey: walk through a crop field or garden and photograph leaves showing yellowing, purple colour or burnt edges. Note whether the symptom is on old or young leaves and use the mobility rule to guess the deficient nutrient. Ask your teacher to confirm.

Mini compost in a bottle: cut a plastic bottle, add layers of dry leaves (brown, high C) and kitchen vegetable waste (green, high N) with a little soil, keep moist and shake weekly. In 4–6 weeks you will see the residues darken and crumble – decomposition in action. Try a bottle with only dry leaves and compare the speed (C:N effect).

Facts & amazing information

1

The term pH was introduced by Danish chemist S.P.L. Sørensen in 1909.

2

Konkan lateritic soils commonly have pH 5–6, while black soils of the Deccan are often 7.5–8.5.

3

At very low pH (below about 5), aluminium becomes soluble and toxic, giving short, stubby roots.

4

Some crops prefer acidity: tea, pineapple and potato grow well at pH 5–5.5.

5

Clayey, humus-rich soils may need several times more lime than sandy soils to raise pH by one unit, because of high buffering.

6

Kari soils of Kerala can have pH below 3.5 when they dry out – among the most acidic agricultural soils in India.

7

Of the 18 essential elements, C, H and O come free from air and water and make up about 95 % of a plant's dry weight.

8

Molybdenum is needed in such tiny amounts that a few grams per hectare can correct a deficiency.

9

Nickel (1987, for the enzyme urease) and cobalt (for vitamin B₁₂ in legume root nodules) are the latest additions, taking the list of essential elements to 18.

10

Zinc deficiency causes 'khaira' disease of rice – one of the commonest micronutrient problems in Indian soils.

11

Liebig's Law of the Minimum (1840) says yield is limited by the scarcest nutrient – the shortest stave of the barrel.

12

Soil test data show that most Indian soils are low in nitrogen and many are deficient in zinc and sulphur.

13

Soils hold about three times more carbon than all the world's vegetation – a huge carbon bank.

14

Humus can hold water equal to several times its own weight, like a sponge.

15

Some humus in soil is hundreds to thousands of years old.

16

Most Indian soils have less than 0.5–0.75 % organic carbon because heat speeds up decomposition.

17

Burning crop residues destroys organic matter, kills soil microbes and pollutes the air – incorporating them builds soil carbon.

18

A good earthworm population can pass many tonnes of soil per hectare through their bodies each year, producing nutrient-rich castings.
