Reading soil like a book

If you cut a deep pit in a field and look at its wall, you will see bands of different colours – dark on top, lighter below, sometimes reddish or stony at the bottom. Each band is a page in the life story of that soil. Soil scientists call this vertical section a soil profile, and the bands are horizons. Learning to read them tells you how deep roots can go, how water moves and how fertile the soil is.

3.1 Definitions

Soil profile

  1. In simple wordsA vertical cut through the soil from the surface down to the rock, showing all its layers.
  2. Scientific definitionA vertical section of the soil through all its horizons and extending into the parent material, used to study soil morphology and classification.
  3. Standard definition · key factThe soil profile is the sequence of natural layers or horizons in a soil, exposed in a pit or cutting.
  4. One-line (exam)Soil profile = vertical section showing all horizons.

Soil horizon

  1. In simple wordsOne layer of the soil profile, different in colour or feel from the layers above and below it.
  2. Scientific definitionA layer of soil, approximately parallel to the land surface, with characteristic properties (colour, texture, structure, consistence, chemistry) produced by soil-forming processes.
  3. Standard definition · key factHorizons are designated as O, A, E, B, C and R (master horizons) with suffixes for special features.
  4. One-line (exam)Horizon = distinct layer of soil profile parallel to surface.

A soil profile is a vertical section of soil through all its layers, from the surface down to the parent rock. Each distinct layer is a horizon; horizons differ in colour, texture, structure and composition. To study a soil, we dig a pit and look at the wall.

Explained simply

A soil profile is like a slice of a layered cake seen from the side. Each layer (horizon) has its own colour, texture and structure. A pedon is the smallest 3-D block of soil (about 1 to 10 m²) that shows all the horizons – like one full piece of cake.

3.2 Master horizons

Horizon Nickname What you see Key facts
O Organic horizon Dark, soft surface layer of leaf litter and humus Leaf litter (undecomposed leaves, twigs, moss, lichens) on top; humus (decomposed) below. Some soils (peat) are only O layers. Intense biological activity. Typical thickness 2–5 cm.
A Topsoil / “biomantle” Top of the mineral soil; dark because of humus Most biologically productive layer; earthworms, fungi, bacteria live here; most roots; the layer farmers plough. Roughly 10–30 cm.
E Eluvial / leaching layer Light-coloured, mostly sand and silt, poor in clay Water leaches (washes out) clay, iron, aluminium and organic matter downward. Found mainly under forests; often absent in cultivated tropical soils.
B Subsoil / illuvial layer Red-brown or yellow-brown; firm, rich in clay Zone of accumulation of clay, Fe and Al oxides, carbonates, gypsum. Red-brown colour from iron oxides. Roots can reach here. Typically 30–100 cm.
C Regolith / parent material Large rocks or lumps of partially weathered bedrock Least affected by soil-forming processes; little or no organic matter; just weathered rock fragments.
R Bedrock Continuous solid rock mass Deepest horizon; colour of original rock (e.g., limestone, dolomite); may need blasting to dig.

Memory trick: "O A E B C R"

Remember the order from top to bottom as "Old Ants Eat Bread Crumbs Regularly".

  • O – Organic: leaves and litter (thick in forests, usually absent in cultivated fields).
  • A – topsoil: dark, rich in humus, most roots and life – the farmer's working layer.
  • E – Eluviated (washed-out): pale layer that has lost clay, iron and organic matter.
  • B – subsoil: where the washed-down clay, iron and lime accumulate.
  • C – partly weathered parent material.
  • R – hard bedRock.

In the field

When a tractor ploughs 20–25 cm deep, it mixes the A horizon – this mixed layer is called the Ap horizon (p = ploughed). Repeated ploughing at the same depth can create a hard plough pan just below it that blocks roots and water – one reason for deep ploughing or chisel ploughing once in a few years.

3.3 Eluvial and illuvial zones

  • Eluvial zone (O, A, E): materials are washed out and carried downward. (Latin e = out.)

  • Illuvial zone (B): materials are washed in and accumulate – clay, carbonates, iron, gypsum.

Imagine pouring water through a tea strainer: the water takes small particles (clay, iron) out of the top and some settle in the lower layer. The top becomes pale (E) and the lower layer becomes rich and clayey (B).

Explained simply

Eluvial = Exit: material leaves. Illuvial = In: material comes in. Rain water percolating downward picks up fine clay and dissolved substances from the upper layers (eluviation) and drops them lower down (illuviation), just as tea leaves stay in the strainer while the tea liquid passes through.

3.4 Solum, saprolite and regolith

Solum

  1. In simple wordsThe real, living part of the soil – the top layers where roots grow.
  2. Scientific definitionThe upper part of a soil profile in which soil-forming processes are active; it consists of the A and B horizons (true soil).
  3. Standard definition · key factSolum = A + B horizons, i.e. the genetic soil developed by pedogenic processes.
  4. One-line (exam)Solum = A + B horizons.

Regolith

  1. In simple wordsAll the loose broken material lying over hard rock.
  2. Scientific definitionThe unconsolidated mantle of weathered rock material and soil lying above the solid bedrock; it includes the solum and the C horizon.
  3. Standard definition · key factRegolith comprises soil plus weathered parent material over bedrock.
  4. One-line (exam)Regolith = A + B + C over bedrock.

  • Regolith = all the weathered material from the surface to solid bedrock.

  • Solum = the upper, most strongly weathered part of the profile (O, A, E, B) – “the true soil” where roots and biological activity are concentrated.

  • Saprolite = the least weathered part of the regolith lying just above bedrock (largely the C horizon, still showing the rock’s original structure).

O A E B C R – remember “Old Aunts Eat Biscuits, Cakes and Rolls” (any phrase you like).

Solum = A + B (plus O and E if present). C is not part of the solum.

Three words, one picture

Solum = the true soil (O + A + E + B) where most roots and biological activity are. Saprolite = the C horizon, rock that is rotten but still keeps the original rock structure. Regolith = everything loose above the hard rock (solum + saprolite). Look at the diagram above and point to each one.

Try it yourself

Make a soil monolith: in a road cutting or well-digging site, scrape a clean vertical face. Stick a strip of cello-tape or press a long strip of cardboard smeared with glue against the face, peel it off, and label the horizons by colour. Measure the depth of each horizon with a scale.

Facts & amazing information

1

Soil scientists can 'read' about 10,000 years of history in a single soil profile.

2

Grey or blue-green colours with rusty mottles in a horizon reveal that it is waterlogged for part of the year.

3

Most crop roots in India feed within the top 30 cm – the A horizon – but deep-rooted crops like cotton and pigeonpea reach the B horizon.

4

Repeated ploughing at the same depth can form a hard 'plough pan' that blocks roots and water.

5

A pedon is usually only 1 to 10 m² in area – the smallest soil unit that shows all the horizons.

6

The Munsell colour chart lets scientists anywhere in the world describe a soil colour with the same code.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Describe the procedure for studying a soil profile in the field and recording its morphological features. (5 marks · BTL-3 Apply · CO1)

    Answer points: Site, pit, face preparation, horizon marking, colour, texture, structure, consistence, roots, boundaries, sampling.

  2. Explain why most black soils of India do not show a distinct E horizon. (7 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Pedoturbation by shrink–swell; high base status; smectite; self-mulching.

  3. Describe the master horizons of a soil profile with a neat sketch and analyse the processes responsible for each horizon. (8 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: O, A, E, B, C, R; humification, eluviation, illuviation, weathering; sketch.

  4. Differentiate solum, regolith and saprolite and discuss their significance for root growth, groundwater recharge and engineering structures. (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO1)

    Answer points: Definitions; depths; roots, recharge, foundations, canals.

  5. Explain eluviation and illuviation and evaluate the field evidence used to identify them in a soil profile. (12 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO1)

    Answer points: Movement of clay, Fe, Al, OM; E and B horizons; clay skins, colour contrast; examples.
