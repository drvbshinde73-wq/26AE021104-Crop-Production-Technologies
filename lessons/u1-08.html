8.1 Overview

Chemical properties decide which nutrients are present, in what form, and whether plants can take them up. The major ones are: (1) soil pH, (2) electrical conductivity (EC), (3) ion exchange (CEC and AEC), (4) soil organic carbon, (5) nutrient availability. Others: base saturation, ESP, buffering capacity, carbonates, redox potential.

8.2 Soil pH

pH is the negative logarithm of hydrogen ion activity (concentration): pH = −log [H⁺]. It tells whether the soil is acidic or alkaline. It is one of the most important soil properties for plant growth. (Chapter 10 explains it fully.) Quick facts:

  • Measured in 1:2.5 soil : water suspension (or 0.01 M CaCl₂) with a pH meter after stirring about 30 minutes.

  • Common range in soils: 4 to 10. Ideal: 6.3–6.8 (acceptable 6.0–7.5).

  • Acid soils need lime; alkaline soils need tolerant crops/gypsum/sulphur.

8.3 Electrical conductivity (EC) – a measure of salinity

Pure water hardly conducts electricity; dissolved salts (ions) do. So EC measures the amount of soluble salts in the soil solution. EC is expressed in deciSiemens per metre (dS m⁻¹) at 25 °C. (1 dS/m = 1 mmho/cm.)

Measurement: prepare a soil–water suspension (commonly 1:2.5), shake about 30 minutes, let it settle, and measure EC of the clear liquid (supernatant) with an EC meter. For saline soil classification, EC of the saturated paste extract (ECe) is used.

ECe (dS m⁻¹, saturated extract) Salinity class Effect on crops
< 2 Non-saline (desirable) Negligible
2 – 4 Slightly saline Very sensitive crops affected
4 – 8 Moderately saline Many crops – yield reduced
8 – 16 Strongly saline Only tolerant crops
> 16 Very strongly saline Very few crops survive

Significance of EC: salinity indicator; guide for crop selection (salt-tolerant vs sensitive); judging irrigation water quality; monitoring soil health over time; planning leaching, drainage and reclamation; fertilizer management.

8.4 Ion exchange – the heart of soil chemistry

Soil particles carry electrical charges on their surfaces. Because of these charges, they hold ions (charged nutrients) like a magnet. The swapping of ions between the soil particle surface and the soil solution is called ion exchange. It is reversible and continuous.

  • Cations (+): Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺, NH₄⁺, H⁺, Al³⁺, Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺.

  • Anions (−): Cl⁻, NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻, BO₃³⁻ (borate), MoO₄²⁻.

  • Ions held on colloids = exchangeable ions. When roots take up ions from the soil solution, colloids release held ions to replace them (nutrient supply!).

  • Sites: clay, silt fraction and organic matter – the colloidal material is the most important. Charge is highest on edges, corners, furrows and cavities. Soil is amphoteric (can carry both + and − charges).

  • Cation exchange is far more common than anion exchange because most soil colloids are negatively charged.

A cation exchange example

Colloid–Ca²⁺ + 2K⁺ ⇌ Colloid–K₂ + Ca²⁺

  • Reversible – can run in either direction.

  • Equivalent charges – one Ca²⁺ (2 charges) is replaced by two K⁺ (2 charges); total charge stays balanced.

  • Mass action – a higher concentration of K⁺ in the solution (e.g., from fertilizer) pushes the exchange to the right.

Which cations stick hardest?

Order of holding: Al³⁺ > H⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > K⁺ ≈ NH₄⁺ > Na⁺. (K⁺ and NH₄⁺ are held about equally.) Reasons: (1) higher charge is held more strongly; (2) smaller hydrated size gets closer to the surface (H⁺ is tightly held; Na⁺ is highly hydrated so held weakly); (3) higher concentration – a large amount of a weak ion can still displace a strong one.

Cation exchange capacity (CEC)

CEC is the total amount of exchangeable cations a soil can hold per unit weight of soil. It is the soil’s nutrient storage capacity. Unit: cmol(+) kg⁻¹ (older unit: meq/100 g – same numerical value).

Key points to remember

Low-CEC soils (sandy, red, laterite): apply fertilizer in smaller, split doses to avoid losses.

Ideal CEC for productive soils: roughly 15–30 cmol(+) kg⁻¹.

Base saturation

Base saturation (%) = percentage of the CEC occupied by basic cations (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺).

Base saturation (%) = [(Ex. Ca + Mg + K + Na) ÷ CEC] × 100

Worked example

CEC = 20 cmol(+)/kg. Exchange sites hold: Ca 9, Mg 3, K 1, Na 1 and (H + Al) 6.

Exchangeable bases = 9 + 3 + 1 + 1 = 14.

Base saturation = 14 ÷ 20 × 100 = 70 %.

Acidic cations = 6 (30 %). Productive soils usually have 60–90 % base saturation; < 50 % suggests an acidic/degraded soil and the need to check lime requirement.

Anion exchange capacity (AEC)

Some sites on soil colloids carry positive charge, so they hold anions. AEC = total exchangeable anions held per unit mass of soil, expressed in cmol(+) kg⁻¹ (or meq/100 g). It is usually much lower than CEC (about 0.1–5 vs 10–40) but becomes important in acidic soils and in soils rich in kaolinite, Fe and Al oxides and organic matter.

  • Sources of positive charge: broken edges of clay (more in kaolinite and illite); protonated organic groups; hydroxylated surfaces of Fe and Al oxides; low pH.

  • Effect of pH: AEC increases as soil becomes more acidic (more H⁺ attaches to surfaces) and decreases at neutral to alkaline pH.

Phosphate (H₂PO₄⁻) Strongly adsorbed at all pH Low mobility; easily “fixed”
Sulphate (SO₄²⁻) Moderately adsorbed Low–moderate mobility
Chloride (Cl⁻) Very weakly adsorbed Highly mobile, leached
Nitrate (NO₃⁻) Not adsorbed Highly mobile; lost by leaching to groundwater

Why ion exchange matters

  • Nutrient store: holds K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺ for plants.

  • Less leaching: cations stick to colloids, not washed away.

  • pH buffering: resists sudden pH change.

  • Fertilizer planning: low CEC soils need split doses.

  • Reclaiming soils: gypsum supplies Ca²⁺ that replaces Na⁺ on colloids (sodic soils).

  • Natural filter: holds heavy metals (Pb²⁺, Cd²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺) and wastewater ions, giving cleaner water.

8.5 Soil organic carbon (SOC)

SOC is the carbon part of soil organic matter, produced by the decomposition of plant and animal residues and microbial biomass. Sources: crop residues, root biomass, farmyard manure, compost, green manure, cover crops, microbial biomass. Forms: labile (active, quickly turned over) and stable (humus) fractions; protected as free, occluded (inside aggregates) or mineral-associated.

Measurement: Walkley–Black wet oxidation (chromic acid) or dry combustion (CN analyser). Roles: improves structure and aggregation, increases water holding and CEC, supplies and retains nutrients, supports microbes, buffers pH, reduces heavy-metal toxicity, builds long-term fertility, and helps climate-change mitigation (carbon sequestration).

Ideal (Indian soil-test status): ≥ 0.75 % = high; > 1.0 % = strong target. (Classes: < 0.5 % low, 0.5–0.75 % medium, > 0.75 % high.)

8.6 Nutrient availability

Only the part of a nutrient that is in a form roots can absorb is “available”. In soil the nutrient exists in five pools, in dynamic equilibrium: soil solution (immediately available), exchangeable (on colloids; quickly available), fixed/bound (less available), organic (released gradually by mineralization) and mineral (inside minerals; released very slowly by weathering). Factors: pH and redox, organic matter, CEC/AEC, texture and clay, moisture and aeration, temperature, microbial activity, management (fertilization, liming, cropping).
