Where does the charge come from?

In the last lecture we saw that colloids are tiny, charged particles with a huge surface. Now we open them up: what kinds of colloids occur in soil – layer-silicate clays, oxides, allophane and humus – and why they carry a charge that lets them hold and exchange nutrient ions.

8.1 Types of soil colloids

There are four major types:

Group Class Examples
Inorganic colloids 1. Layer-silicate clays Kaolinite, illite, smectite (montmorillonite), vermiculite, chlorite
2. Iron and aluminium oxide clays (sesquioxides) Gibbsite Al(OH)₃, goethite, hematite Fe₂O₃
3. Allophane and other amorphous clays Allophane (volcanic ash soils)
Organic colloid 4. Humus Humic acid, fulvic acid, humin

Clay minerals as sandwiches

Layer-silicate clays are built from two kinds of sheets: silica (tetrahedral) and alumina (octahedral). Stack them like sandwiches:

  • 1:1 clay (kaolinite) – one slice of bread + one filling; layers held tightly by hydrogen bonds; does not swell; low CEC (3–15 cmol/kg). Common in red and laterite soils.
  • 2:1 expanding clay (montmorillonite/smectite) – filling between two slices; water and cations enter between layers, so it swells a lot; high CEC (80–120 cmol/kg). Dominant in black soils.
  • 2:1 non-expanding (illite) – layers locked by K⁺ ions; little swelling; medium CEC.

Humus has the highest CEC of all (150–300 cmol/kg or more) – which is why even a little organic matter matters so much.

1. Layer-silicate clays

These are crystalline clays built from two kinds of building blocks stacked in sheets, like pages of a book:

  • Silica tetrahedron: one silicon atom surrounded by four oxygen atoms (a four-sided pyramid). Joined in a tetrahedral sheet (T).

  • Alumina octahedron: one aluminium (or Mg) atom surrounded by six oxygens/hydroxyls (an eight-sided block). Joined in an octahedral sheet (O).

2. Iron and aluminium oxide clays (sesquioxides)

In warm humid climates with heavy rain and long weathering, silica and bases leach out and the less soluble Fe and Al oxides remain. These are called sesquioxides (Latin sesqui = one and a half, because they have 1.5 times as much oxygen as Fe or Al): Al(OH)₃ (gibbsite), Fe₂O₃ (hematite), FeOOH (goethite). They give soils the red/yellow colour, have low CEC, but carry positive charges → good anion exchange → fix phosphate.

3. Allophane and other amorphous minerals

Allophane is non-crystalline (amorphous), common in soils from volcanic ash (Andisols). It has high anion exchange and often also high CEC, high water holding, high phosphate fixation, and very low bulk density.

4. Humus (organic colloid)

Humus is amorphous, dark brown to black, nearly insoluble in water but soluble in dilute alkali (NaOH/KOH). It is the stable intermediate product left after considerable decomposition of plant and animal remains. Its negative charge comes from partly dissociated carboxyl (–COOH), phenolic (–OH) and hydroxyl groups. Humus has CEC of 150–300 cmol/kg – several times that of clay.

8.2 Origin (sources) of charge on soil colloids

Colloids are charged. The charge comes from three sources:

Where does the negative charge come from?

Imagine a classroom where each bench should seat a student with charge +4 (Si⁴⁺). If a student with only +3 (Al³⁺) sits there instead, the bench is short by one positive charge – the surface becomes negative. This swap during formation of the mineral is isomorphous substitution, and the charge is permanent.

Other charges come from broken edges of clay crystals and from pH-dependent groups (like –OH and –COOH in humus) that lose H⁺ as pH rises. That is why liming an acid soil actually increases its CEC.

(i) Isomorphous substitution – permanent charge

In the crystal lattice, one ion is replaced by another of similar size but lower charge, without breaking the crystal structure. Example: in the tetrahedral sheet Si⁴⁺ (0.42 Å) is replaced by Al³⁺ (0.51 Å); in the octahedral sheet Al³⁺ is replaced by Mg²⁺ (0.66 Å). Each substitution leaves one negative charge unbalanced, so the clay becomes negatively charged. This charge is permanent – it does not change with pH. It is the major source of charge in 2:1 and 2:1:1 clays (smectite, vermiculite, illite).

(ii) Broken edges (broken bonds)

At the edges of clay crystals the lattice is cut, leaving unsatisfied valencies (dangling bonds) that can accept or give H⁺. This charge is pH-dependent: positive at low pH (protonation), negative at high pH (deprotonation). It is an important source in 1:1 clays (kaolinite) because they have proportionally more edge surface, and exists in all clays.

(iii) pH-dependent charge

Surface functional groups (–OH, –COOH) on oxides, organic matter and clay edges gain or lose H⁺ as pH changes: at low pH they gain H⁺ → positive charge (OH₂⁺); at high pH they lose H⁺ → negative charge (O⁻). This is the main source of charge for humus, Fe/Al oxides and allophane. Most soils overall carry net negative charge, but some highly weathered, oxide- or allophane-dominated soils can have a net positive charge.

Facts & amazing information

1

Bentonite (a smectite clay) is used to seal canals and farm ponds because it swells and blocks water flow.

2

Liming an acid soil actually raises its CEC, because humus and oxide edges gain negative charge as pH rises.

3

The 'Tyndall effect' – light scattering by a colloidal suspension – is why clay water looks cloudy.
