The black rock under Maharashtra

About 66 million years ago, huge cracks opened in the Earth and lava poured out again and again, layer upon layer, over a vast area of western India. That lava cooled into the dark rock called basalt – the Deccan Trap. Today most of Maharashtra stands on it, and when basalt weathers it gives us the famous black cotton soil. So the story of our soils begins with rocks and minerals.

2.1 Introduction

All soil starts as rock. Rock is made of minerals; minerals are made of elements. Water, wind, temperature and living things slowly break rock into smaller and smaller pieces (weathering). That broken material is the parent material of soil. Then plants, animals and microbes add organic matter, and over time the pile of broken rock becomes soil with layers. So the chain is:

Elements➜Minerals➜Rocks➜Weathering➜Parent material➜Soil

Explained simply – the building chain

Think of a building: elements are bricks' raw clay, minerals are the bricks, and rocks are the walls. When the wall slowly breaks down (weathering), the broken pieces become the parent material, and living things then turn that material into soil.

2.2 What is a rock?

A rock is a hard mass of mineral matter made of one or more rock-forming minerals. Rocks form the solid crust of the Earth. The study of rocks is called petrology (Greek petra = rock, logos = science), and the study of how rocks originate is petrogenesis.

Inside the Earth the temperature is very high. In some places rocks of the upper mantle and lower crust partially melt to form a hot liquid called magma. Melting happens when (i) temperature rises, (ii) pressure falls (rising rock decompresses), or (iii) water/gases are added, which lowers the melting point. When magma reaches the surface it is called lava.

Rock vs mineral – the chocolate test

A mineral is like plain chocolate: one substance with a fixed recipe (chemical formula) and a regular crystal shape. A rock is like a fruit-and-nut bar: a mixture of several minerals stuck together. Granite, for example, is a "bar" of quartz, feldspar and mica.

How to remember the three families

Igneous = born of fire (Latin ignis): melted rock (magma or lava) cooled and hardened. Sedimentary = settled layers: bits of older rock carried by water or wind, settled, pressed and cemented. Metamorphic = changed form (Greek meta = change, morph = shape): existing rock changed by great heat and pressure without melting.

Cooling speed decides crystal size: lava that cools fast on the surface (basalt) has tiny crystals; magma that cools slowly deep underground (granite) has large, visible crystals.

Rocks of Maharashtra

Basalt (igneous) covers most of the state and gives black soils. In the Konkan, basalt weathered under heavy rain gives red-brown laterite, cut into blocks for houses. Parts of eastern Vidarbha have older granite, gneiss and sandstone, which give lighter red and yellow soils.

Common mistake

Students often say "sedimentary rocks form from lava" – no! Lava forms igneous rock. Sedimentary rocks form from sediments (sand, mud, shells) deposited mainly under water.

A. Igneous rocks (primary or massive rocks)

They are the first rocks formed on Earth when molten magma solidified. Based on where the magma cools, they are of two main kinds:

Extrusive (volcanic) Intrusive (plutonic)
Where cooled On or near the surface (lava) Deep inside the crust
Cooling speed Fast Very slow
Crystal size Fine-grained / glassy / bubbly (vesicular) Coarse-grained, large visible crystals
Examples Basalt, rhyolite, andesite, obsidian, pumice, scoria Granite, diorite, gabbro, pegmatite, syenite

  • Why large crystals in granite? Because magma cooled slowly underground, so atoms had time to arrange into big crystals. Lava cools quickly, so crystals stay tiny.

  • Vesicular rocks (pumice, scoria) have holes because gas/steam bubbles were trapped as lava cooled on the surface.

  • Dykes are intrusive rocks formed in vertical cracks; sills form in horizontal cracks. Plutonic = solidified at great depth; hypabyssal = at shallow depth.

  • Basalt is the most important rock for India’s Deccan plateau, and the parent of Maharashtra’s black soil.

B. Sedimentary rocks (secondary rocks)

When rocks weather, the broken pieces are carried by water, wind or ice and dropped (deposited) somewhere, often in layers. Over millions of years these layers are pressed and cemented together by materials such as silica (SiO₂), iron oxide (Fe₂O₃) or lime, forming sedimentary rocks. They are also called clastic rocks (made of fragments).

C. Metamorphic rocks

Meta = change, morph = form. When igneous or sedimentary rocks are buried deep or squeezed by mountain-building, the heat, pressure and hot fluids change their minerals or texture without fully melting them. Change by water/fluids is hydro-metamorphism; change by pressure is dynamo-metamorphism.

2.4 Minerals

A mineral is a naturally occurring, inorganic solid with a definite chemical composition and an orderly crystal structure. Rocks are collections of minerals (like a cake made of different ingredients). Minerals are broadly classified as metallic (ferrous, non-ferrous, precious), non-metallic (mica, salt, potash, sulphur, limestone) and energy minerals (coal, petroleum).

Primary minerals are those that came directly from cooled magma and have not been chemically changed (quartz, feldspar, mica, hornblende, augite, olivine). Secondary minerals are new minerals formed from the breakdown of primary ones (clay minerals like kaolinite, iron oxides like hematite, carbonates like calcite). Most nutrients are released from primary minerals when they weather.

Relative abundance of minerals in the crust (approx.): plagioclase feldspars 39 %, K-feldspars 12 %, quartz 12 %, pyroxenes 11 %, micas 5 %, amphiboles 5 %, clays 5 %, other silicates 3 %, non-silicates 8 %. This is why feldspars dominate and why silicon is so common in soil.

Silicate groups by structure: quartz and feldspar = tectosilicates; micas, kaolinite = phyllosilicates (sheet-like); amphibole = double-chain; pyroxene = single-chain; olivine = isolated tetrahedra.

Ferromagnesian minerals (olivine, pyroxene, hornblende, biotite) contain Fe and Mg; they weather faster than quartz.

Why some minerals survive and others vanish

Minerals that crystallised first from very hot magma (olivine, pyroxene) are the least comfortable at the Earth's cool, wet surface, so they weather fastest. Quartz crystallised last at the lowest temperature and is the most stable – that is why river sand and beach sand are mostly quartz: everything else has already weathered away.

Primary minerals come straight from the cooling magma; secondary minerals (clays, oxides) are "new" minerals made from the broken pieces during weathering. Clay is therefore the child of feldspar and mica.

Try it yourself

Rock walk: collect three stones near your college or home. Look with a hand lens: can you see separate crystals (likely igneous like granite), layers or sand grains (sedimentary), or bands and shiny flakes (metamorphic)? Scratch with an iron nail – quartz will not scratch, calcite will. Put a drop of dilute acid (lemon juice or vinegar) – fizzing means calcium carbonate (limestone).

Check your understanding

Think of your answer first, then tap a question to see it.

Q1. Why does basalt have fine crystals while granite has coarse crystals?

Basalt cools quickly from lava at the surface (no time to grow crystals); granite cools slowly from magma deep underground.

Q2. Name the most weathering-resistant common mineral.

Quartz (SiO₂).

Q3. Give one example each of primary and secondary minerals.

Primary: feldspar, mica, quartz. Secondary: kaolinite, montmorillonite, iron oxides, calcite.

Q4. Which rock is the parent of the black soils of Maharashtra?

Basalt of the Deccan Trap.
