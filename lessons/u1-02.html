Why old temples look worn out

Look at an old stone temple or a stone step outside a well. The sharp edges carved centuries ago are now rounded, the surface is pitted, and green plants are growing from cracks. No one hammered it – sun, rain, cold nights, chemicals in water and plant roots did this work quietly over hundreds of years. This slow breaking and changing of rock is called weathering, and it is the very first step in making soil.

2.1 What is weathering?

Weathering is the slow breakdown (disintegration) and chemical change (decomposition) of rocks at or near the Earth’s surface. Imagine a hard stone left outside for thousands of years: sun heats it, rain wets it, ice cracks it, roots enter it – eventually it becomes sand, silt, clay and dissolved salts. That is weathering. Weathering links the solid Earth with the atmosphere, water and living things.

Agents of weathering/erosion: wind, glaciers (ice), running water, sea waves and underground water, plus temperature, plants and animals. Note: weathering breaks rock in place; erosion carries the pieces away.

Weathering vs erosion

Weathering breaks or changes rock in place. Erosion carries the broken material away by water, wind or ice. Think of a biscuit: crumbling it in your hand is weathering; brushing the crumbs off the table is erosion.

2.2 Why weathering matters in agriculture

  • It forms the parent material of soil.

  • It releases minerals (K, Ca, Mg, P, micronutrients) that plants need.

  • It develops soil properties such as texture and mineralogy.

  • It decides nutrient availability.

  • It builds soil depth and profile (horizons).

  • It controls long-term soil productivity: Rock → weathered material → soil.

The bigger the surface, the faster things happen

Cut a potato into small cubes and it cooks much faster than a whole potato – because more surface is exposed. In the same way, when rock breaks into tiny particles, its surface area becomes enormous, chemical reactions speed up, nutrients such as K, Ca and Mg are released, and clay forms that can hold water and nutrients.

2.3 Three types of weathering

Type Main cause Result
Physical (mechanical) Temperature changes, freezing of water, wind/water abrasion Rock breaks into smaller pieces; no change in chemistry
Chemical Water, oxygen, CO₂, acids Minerals change into new minerals; some elements dissolve
Biological Roots, burrowing animals, microbes, humans Both breaking and chemical action

2.4 Physical (mechanical) weathering

Rocks are poor conductors of heat. In a desert the surface heats up in the day (expands) and cools at night (contracts). Different minerals in the rock expand by different amounts, so cracks form and pieces peel off (exfoliation). Water entering cracks freezes into ice, which expands by about 9 % and acts like a wedge, widening cracks (frost wedging). Repeated cycles shatter the rock.

  • Caused by change of temperature (expansion and contraction).

  • Caused by freezing action of water in cracks.

  • Also: abrasion by wind-blown sand, flowing water, ice movement.

  • No new minerals formed – only smaller pieces.

Explained simply

Physical weathering is like breaking a chapati into pieces: the pieces are smaller but still chapati. The rock breaks but its minerals do not change.

In the Deccan plateau the summer day can be above 40 °C and the night much cooler. Rock expands in the day heat and contracts at night; the outer shell heats more than the inside, so thin sheets peel off like onion skin (exfoliation). Rounded basalt boulders seen on hills around Pune and Satara are formed this way.

Water is a powerful hammer

Water expands by about 9 % when it freezes. In a crack, that expansion pushes with enormous force and splits the rock – like a glass bottle of water that cracks in a freezer.

2.5 Chemical weathering

This is the real “soil-making” weathering because it turns primary minerals into clays and releases nutrients. Water is the key ingredient. Warm and wet climates have the fastest chemical weathering. There are five main reactions:

Reaction Meaning in simple words Example / equation
Solution Soluble minerals simply dissolve in water, helped by small amounts of acid. Silica, K, Na, Mg, Ca, Cl, CO₃, SO₄ dissolve. Gives “honeycomb” rocks. Rock salt dissolves in water.
Hydrolysis Water splits into H⁺ and OH⁻. H⁺ attacks the mineral and replaces cations (K⁺, Na⁺, Ca²⁺…), which are released. Main process that breaks silicates. Feldspar in granite → clay (kaolinite) + K⁺ + silica
Hydration Water is added to a mineral and becomes part of its structure (the mineral swells and softens). Hematite (Fe₂O₃) → limonite (Fe₂O₃·nH₂O); anhydrite → gypsum
Oxidation Oxygen reacts with a mineral (especially Fe²⁺, sulphides) and electrons are lost. Rusty red/yellow colours. 4Fe²⁺ + 3O₂ → 2Fe₂³⁺O₃ (hematite/limonite)
Carbonation CO₂ dissolves in water to form carbonic acid (H₂CO₃), which attacks carbonates and silicates making soluble bicarbonates. Makes caves and karst. CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O → Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻

  • Where do the acids come from? CO₂ + H₂O → H₂CO₃ (carbonic acid, from air and soil CO₂); sulphuric acid from pyrite weathering or acid rain; humic and fulvic acids from decaying organic matter.

  • Oxidation is important for ferromagnesian minerals (olivine, pyroxene, hornblende). It can follow after hydrolysis frees the iron from the silicate.

  • Carbonation of limestone: the equation CaCO₃ + CO₂ + H₂O ⇌ Ca²⁺ + 2HCO₃⁻ runs to the right when CO₂ is high (cold water, soil air) and to the left when CO₂ escapes (cave stalactites form).

Remember the five: S-H-H-O-C = Solution, Hydrolysis, Hydration, Oxidation, Carbonation. Hydrolysis is the most important for silicates and clay formation.

Explained simply

Chemical weathering is like cooking: the ingredients change into something new. Water, oxygen and carbon dioxide react with minerals and form new minerals and dissolved salts.

  • Hydrolysis – water splits feldspar to form clay (the most important reaction for soil formation).
  • Oxidation – iron combines with oxygen and turns red-brown, exactly like an iron nail rusting. That is why many tropical soils are red.
  • Carbonation – CO₂ dissolved in rain forms a weak acid that dissolves limestone (this is how caves form).
  • Hydration – minerals take up water and swell, then crumble.
  • Solution – soluble minerals such as rock salt and gypsum simply dissolve.

Chemical weathering is fastest where it is hot and wet – which is why Konkan laterites are deeply weathered.

2.6 Biological weathering

  • Roots grow into cracks and widen them as they thicken (physical). Roots also release acids (CO₂, organic acids) that dissolve minerals (chemical).

  • Burrowing animals (rodents, ants, earthworms, termites) dig, widen cracks and bring fresh material to the surface, where it weathers faster.

  • Decaying organisms make humic acids; water coloured brown by decaying leaves is acidic and dissolves minerals.

  • Lichens and mosses are pioneers on bare rock – they secrete acids and trap dust, forming the first thin soil.

  • Humans: quarrying, mining and construction break rocks and expose fresh surfaces; removal of vegetation increases weathering and erosion; pollution causes acid rain (SO₂ and NOₓ make sulphuric and nitric acid) that dissolves rocks faster.

Factors controlling the rate of weathering Effect
Climate (temperature, rainfall) Warm + wet = fast chemical weathering; cold = mainly physical
Rock/mineral type Quartz is resistant; olivine, calcite are easily weathered
Particle size / surface area Smaller pieces weather faster
Vegetation Supplies acids, roots, organic matter
Time Longer time = more weathered

Physical weathering = disintegration; chemical weathering = decomposition.

Weathering stages end with soil: Rock → regolith (weathered material) → soil.

Highly weathered tropical soils (laterite, red) have lost bases and are rich in Fe and Al oxides.

Living hammers and chemists

Plant roots grow into cracks and widen them, like a tree root lifting a footpath. Lichens and mosses release acids that dissolve the rock surface. Earthworms, ants and termites mix particles, and burrowing animals bring fresh rock to the surface. Humans too – ploughing, mining and road cuts expose rock to weathering.

Same rock, different soils

Basalt in the dry plains of Marathwada gives deep black soil, while the same basalt in the very wet Konkan gives red laterite. Why? Because rock is only the raw material. Climate, living organisms, slope and time decide what kind of soil is finally made. In 1941 the scientist Hans Jenny wrote this idea as a short equation that every soil scientist still remembers.

2.7 From rock to soil

Soil formation is a slow, two-step story:

  • Step 1 – Weathering of rock produces loose parent material (regolith).

  • Step 2 – Soil development (pedogenesis): living organisms add organic matter, water moves materials up and down, and the parent material gradually organises itself into layers called horizons. A mature soil has a characteristic profile.

It can take hundreds to thousands of years to form just 1 cm of soil, which is why soil is called a non-renewable resource in practical terms.

Weathering vs soil formation

Weathering only produces a loose heap of broken rock (regolith). It becomes soil when plants grow in it, add organic matter, and water moves materials up and down to create layers (horizons). So: weathering makes the raw material; soil formation (pedogenesis) organises it into a living, layered body.

2.8 The five soil-forming factors (Jenny’s equation)

The Russian scientist Dokuchaev said soil is a function of climate, organisms and parent rock. Hans Jenny (1941) wrote it as an equation:

S = f (Cl, O, R, P, T) – Soil = function of Climate, Organisms (biota), Relief (topography), Parent material and Time.

Factor Type How it affects soil Example
Parent material (P) Passive Gives the starting minerals. Decides texture, nutrient reserve, colour, and mineralogy. Basalt → clayey black soil; granite/sandstone → sandy or acidic soil; limestone → calcareous soil
Climate (Cl) Active Temperature and rainfall control weathering, leaching, and organic matter decomposition. Most powerful factor. High rain → leached, acid, laterite; low rain → alkaline, calcareous, saline
Organisms / biota (O) Active Vegetation, microbes, earthworms, humans add OM, mix soil, cycle nutrients. Grassland → thick dark mollic A horizon; forest → acidic litter layer
Relief / topography (R) Passive Slope controls drainage, erosion and moisture. Hilltop: thin, eroded; valley: deep, wet, alluvial; depressions: waterlogged, peat
Time (T) Passive Soils need time to mature. Young alluvium: no horizons; old tropical soils: deeply weathered

Climate and living things are the “active” factors (they do the work). Parent material, relief and time are “passive” (they set the stage).

Same parent rock + different climate = different soils. Same climate + different parent rock = different soils.

Explained simply – the recipe analogy

Think of making curd. Parent material is the milk; climate is the room temperature; organisms are the curd culture (bacteria); relief is where you keep the pot (a warm shelf or a cold corner); time is how many hours you wait. Change any one factor and the result changes.

Climate and organisms are "active" – they supply energy and do the work. Parent material, relief and time are "passive" – they are acted upon.

Relief in a Maharashtra field

On a sloping field, the upper part has thin, stony, light soil (soil washes down), while the low-lying part has deep, dark, clayey soil that sometimes stays waterlogged. Same rock, same rain, different soil – only because of slope.

2.9 Soil-forming (pedogenic) processes

All the events happening inside a developing soil can be grouped into four fundamental processes (Simonson, 1959):

Process Meaning Examples
Additions Materials added to the soil Organic matter, dust, rain salts, fertilizer, sediments from flooding
Losses Materials removed Leaching of salts, erosion, crop removal, gases (CO₂, N₂)
Translocations Materials moved within the profile Eluviation (out of A/E) and illuviation (into B); capillary rise of salts
Transformations Materials changed in place Weathering of minerals, humification, formation of clay, oxidation/reduction

Specific pedogenic processes that create well-known soils:

Process What happens Typical soil / climate
Humification Plant and animal remains decompose into dark humus Grassland – Mollisols, Chernozems
Eluviation / illuviation Clay, Fe, Al and OM washed out of upper layers (eluviation) and deposited lower (illuviation) Alfisols, Ultisols (argillic B horizon)
Podzolization Strong acid leaching removes Fe, Al and humus from E horizon, leaving a pale ashy layer; deposited in B Cool, wet conifer forest – Spodosols
Laterization (desilication) Heavy rain removes silica and bases; Fe and Al oxides remain; red hard soil Hot, humid tropics – Laterite, Oxisols
Calcification Ca carbonate accumulates in the lower layers Semi-arid to arid – calcareous, Mollisols/Aridisols
Salinization Soluble salts accumulate at surface by evaporation and capillary rise Arid with poor drainage – saline soils
Solonization / alkalization Na⁺ builds up on clay; soil disperses, pH > 8.5 Sodic (alkali) soils
Solodization (dealkalization) Leaching of Na, soil becomes acidic in upper layers Degraded alkali soils
Gleization Waterlogging causes reduction of Fe; grey-bluish colour Poorly drained, paddy and swamp soils
Pedoturbation (churning) Shrink–swell mixes the soil Vertisols (black cotton soil)

Four verbs to remember

Everything that happens in a soil can be described by four verbs: ADD (leaves, manure, dust), LOSE (leaching, erosion, harvest), MOVE (clay and salts travel up or down – translocation) and CHANGE (minerals become clay, residue becomes humus – transformation). Specific processes such as podzolization or laterization are just special combinations of these four.

Common mistake

Laterization removes silica and keeps iron and aluminium; podzolization removes iron, aluminium and humus from the upper layer and keeps silica. They are almost opposites – do not mix them up in the exam.

2.10 Soil development

A soil first shows only an A over C (young, Entisols), then a weakly developed B (Inceptisols), then a clear B with clay accumulation (Alfisols, Ultisols), and finally a deeply weathered soil with oxides (Oxisols). The older and warmer-wetter the environment, the more strongly weathered the soil is.

Soils have an age, like people

A young soil (Entisol – e.g., fresh river alluvium) has hardly any layers. A mature soil has well-developed A and B horizons. A very old soil (Oxisol) has lost most nutrients and is mostly oxides. Soil age depends on how much work has been done, not just years – a soil can stay "young" for a long time on a steep slope where erosion keeps removing the top.

Try it yourself

Rusting = oxidation: keep one iron nail in a dry bottle and one in a moist bottle with some soil for a week. The moist one turns red-brown – that is the same oxidation that colours red soils.
Carbonation: drop a piece of chalk (calcium carbonate) into vinegar and watch it fizz and dissolve.

Compare two spots: dig a small pit (30 cm) on a slope and another in a low-lying spot of the same field. Note the depth of dark topsoil, colour, stones and moisture. Which factor of Jenny's equation explains the difference?

Facts & amazing information

1

Water expands by about 9 % when it freezes – enough force to split solid granite.

2

The rounded boulders of Hampi were once a single solid granite mass, shaped over millions of years by weathering.

3

Chemical weathering roughly doubles for every 10 °C rise in temperature – which is why the hot, wet Konkan has deeply weathered laterites.

4

Lichens – a partnership of fungus and alga – release acids that can etch rock surfaces.

5

Cutting a 1 cm cube into 1 µm cubes multiplies its surface area 10,000 times – more surface means faster weathering.

6

The red colour of many Indian soils is literally rust: oxidised iron (haematite).

7

Hans Jenny's 1941 equation S = f(cl, o, r, p, t) is still the foundation of soil science more than 80 years later.

8

Basalt gives black soils on the dry Deccan plateau but red laterite in the wet Konkan – same rock, different climate.

9

The Kaas plateau near Satara has bare basalt with soil only a couple of centimetres thick – soil formation in its very early stage.

10

The word 'laterite' comes from Latin later = brick; it was first described by Francis Buchanan in Kerala in 1807.

11

Earthworms can bring several tonnes of soil to the surface per hectare every year – Darwin studied this for 40 years.

12

A soil on a steep slope can stay 'young' for ever because erosion keeps removing the top.
