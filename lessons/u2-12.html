Sorting 100 crops into sensible boxes

India grows well over a hundred field crops. A student cannot learn each one separately – so agronomists classify them. Once you know that chickpea is a rabi pulse, a C3 legume and a long-day plant, you can already predict its season, its need for nitrogen, its response to temperature and even its place in a crop rotation. Classification is a short-cut to understanding.

12.1 Why classify field crops?

  • To study a very large number of crops systematically.

  • To group crops with similar requirements of climate, soil, water and management.

  • To plan cropping systems and rotations (e.g. follow a cereal with a legume).

  • To decide sowing season, spacing and input needs quickly.

  • To simplify research, extension, statistics and marketing.

Explained simply

A library does not stack books randomly; it arranges them by subject, author and language. Crops are classified in the same way – by season, use, life span, botanical family, photoperiod, CO2 fixation and special purpose. One crop can sit in several boxes at once: maize is a kharif crop, a cereal, an annual, a C4 plant and a day-neutral plant.

12.2 Classification based on ontogeny (life span)

Ontogeny is the complete life history of a plant – from germination through vegetative growth and flowering to seed formation and death. Crops are grouped by how long this cycle takes.

Explained simply

Annuals “live fast” – they put everything into seed in one season. Biennials store food in a root, bulb or head in year one and spend it on flowering in year two. Perennials build a permanent framework of roots or wood and flower year after year.

12.3 Agronomic (economic use) classification

This is the most widely used classification in agronomy. Crops are grouped by the economic product for which they are grown.

1. Cereals

Cultivated grasses of family Poaceae grown for their edible starchy grain (caryopsis). The word comes from Ceres, the Roman goddess of grain.

Points to remember

  • The word cereal is derived from Ceres, the Roman goddess of grain.
  • Cereals are grasses grown for their edible starchy grains.
  • Cereal grains contain about 60–70 % starch and 7–12 % protein.
  • The germ (embryo) is a good source of the fat-soluble vitamin E; whole grains also supply minerals such as selenium, calcium and zinc, and B-vitamins.

2. Millets (Shree Anna / nutri-cereals)

Millets are small-seeded cereals of the grass family that are hardy, drought-tolerant, short in duration and grown on marginal soils with little input. Nutritionally they are richer than rice and wheat in fibre, minerals (Ca, Fe, Zn) and B-vitamins, and most are gluten-free. India classifies them as major millets (sorghum, pearl millet, finger millet) and minor (small) millets (foxtail, little, kodo, proso, barnyard and browntop). Buckwheat, amaranth and quinoa are pseudo-millets – grain-like seeds from non-grass families.

Updated names: Recent taxonomy (Plants of the World Online, Kew) places pearl millet in Cenchrus americanus (formerly Pennisetum glaucum) and browntop millet in Urochloa ramosa (formerly Brachiaria ramosa). Both names are accepted in exams; give the old name in brackets.

Kharif pulses: tur, moong, urad, cowpea, moth bean, horse gram. Rabi pulses: chickpea, lentil, pea, lathyrus, rajma.

  • Rich in protein (20–25 %) – the main protein source of a vegetarian diet.
  • Economically important in the cropping system: they fix atmospheric N and improve soil fertility for the next crop.
  • Their haulm (stems and leaves left after threshing) is used as green manure or fodder.
  • Green pods are used as vegetables (pea, cowpea, field bean).
  • Horse gram and grass pea (Lathyrus sativus) are hardy pulses that grow well in dry and poor soils.

History of botanical classification

The oldest classification of plants was introduced about 370 B.C. by Theophrastus (“father of botany”), who grouped plants as trees, shrubs, under-shrubs and herbs. Carl von Linné (1707–1778), known as Carolus Linnaeus, modernised it with the binomial system of naming (Species Plantarum, 1753).

Thallophyta

  • Lower forms of plants; do not have roots, stem or leaves.
  • Include algae, bacteria and fungi.
  • N-fixing bacteria increase soil fertility; smut and rust diseases are caused by fungi.

Bryophyta

  • Small green plants, higher in scale than Thallophyta; grow in wet places and are widely distributed.
  • None of them is cultivated for human use – no direct economic importance.
  • Include mosses, liverworts and hornworts.

Pteridophyta

  • Green plants with vascular tissues, roots, stems and leaves.
  • Do not have flowers and seeds; reproduce by spores.
  • No agronomic value except ornamental (and Azolla as a biofertilizer).
  • Include Psilophytes, club mosses, horsetails and ferns.

Spermatophyta

  • Most highly developed forms of plant life; produce seeds and bear true fruits (angiosperms).
  • Divided into two subdivisions: (i) Gymnosperms and (ii) Angiosperms – (a) monocotyledons, (b) dicotyledons.
  • Gymnosperms: the seed has an embryo with a food supply but is not enclosed in a fruit (naked seeds); high economic value (timber, resin, paper pulp); e.g. cone-bearing trees such as pine, fir and cedar.
  • Angiosperms: highly specialised plants reproducing by seeds enclosed in an ovary (fruit); include all major cultivated crops of economic importance as food, feed and fibre – e.g. wheat, cotton, tobacco.

Maharashtra example

On deep black soils of Solapur and Ahilyanagar, land is left fallow in kharif and rabi jowar (e.g. Maldandi 35-1) is grown on stored moisture. In Vidarbha soybean or cotton is grown in kharif and chickpea follows in rabi. Sugarcane spans seasons – adsali (July–August), pre-seasonal (October–November) and suru (January–February) planting.

Season – as taught in class

  • Kharif crops: June–July to September–October; e.g. rice, maize, castor, groundnut.
  • Rabi crops: October–November to January–February; e.g. wheat, mustard, barley, oats, potato, Bengal gram, berseem, cabbage and cauliflower.
  • Summer (zaid) crops: February–March to May–June; e.g. black gram, green gram, sesame, cowpea; also kharif rice, kharif maize, rabi maize and summer pulses under irrigation.

Explained simply

A crop's “home climate” is fixed by its origin: rice and sugarcane come from the hot humid tropics, wheat and barley from the cool Fertile Crescent, apple from temperate Central Asia. India grows tropical, sub-tropical and (in the Himalaya) temperate crops – but no polar agriculture, because no part of the country has a polar climate except the highest glaciated peaks.

12.7 Classification based on photoperiod (day length)

Photoperiodism is the response of plants to the relative length of day and night, mainly for flowering (Garner and Allard, 1920).

GroupFlowers whenExamples
Short-day plants (long-night)Day length is shorter than a critical valueRice, soybean, sorghum, cotton, tobacco, sugarcane, maize (most tropical types)
Long-day plantsDay length is longer than a critical valueWheat, barley, oats, chickpea, mustard, lentil, potato
Day-neutral plantsFlowering not controlled by day lengthSunflower, tomato, cucumber, many modern hybrids

Why it matters

A short-day soybean sown too late in the season (shorter days) flowers too early on a small plant and yields poorly. That is why sowing date is a photoperiod decision, not only a rainfall decision.

Facts & amazing information

1

The United Nations declared 2023 the International Year of Millets on India's proposal; millets are promoted as ‘Shree Anna’.

2

Triticale is the first man-made cereal – a cross between wheat (Triticum) and rye (Secale).

3

Azolla, a tiny water fern (pteridophyte), can double its weight in 3–5 days and adds 20–40 kg N/ha to rice fields through its partner Anabaena.

4

Sphagnum moss (a bryophyte) can hold up to 20 times its dry weight of water – that is why peat moss is used in nursery media.

5

Pulses add about 30–150 kg N/ha to the soil through symbiotic nitrogen fixation with Rhizobium.

6

Sugarcane is one of the most efficient C4 plants – it can produce over 100 t of cane per hectare in a year.

7

Poaceae, the grass family, provides more than half of all calories eaten by humans – rice, wheat, maize and sugarcane are all grasses.

8

Kharif and rabi are Arabic words meaning autumn and spring – they refer to the harvest time, not the sowing time.

Long-answer questions for ESE

  1. Explain the classification of crops based on photoperiod with examples. (5 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Short-day, long-day, day-neutral; definition, critical day length, examples, practical significance in sowing date.

  2. Describe the uses of important oilseed crops and the by-products of sugarcane. (5 marks · BTL-2 · CO2)

    Answer points: Groundnut, sesame, castor, mustard, safflower/sunflower, niger, linseed uses; juice, molasses, bagasse, pressmud, trash.

  3. Classify field crops on the basis of ontogeny (life span) and explain each group with suitable examples. (7 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Definition of ontogeny; annuals, biennials, perennials with examples; herbaceous/woody perennials; ratoon crops.

  4. Give the agronomic classification of crops. Distinguish between major and minor millets with botanical names and local names. (7 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Cereals, millets, pulses, oilseeds, sugar, starch, fibre, forage, spices, beverages, medicinal, green manures; jowar, bajra, ragi; kangni, kutki, kodo, proso, barnyard, browntop; pseudo-cereals.

  5. Classify crops by season (kharif, rabi, zaid) with at least five examples of each and explain zaid kharif and zaid rabi. (7 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Sowing–harvest months, weather, examples, sub-seasons.

  6. Classify crops on the basis of climate into tropical, sub-tropical, temperate and polar, and explain why India can grow crops of three of these groups. (8 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Climate definitions; examples; altitude and latitude range of India – tropical south, sub-tropical plains, temperate Himalaya; no polar agriculture.

  7. Discuss the special-purpose classification of crops. Design a cropping plan for a 2-ha rainfed farm in Marathwada that uses catch, cover, trap and contingency crops. (8 marks · BTL-3 Apply · CO2)

    Answer points: Definitions; plan – soybean + tur intercrop, marigold trap crop, horse gram cover, contingency crops (sunflower, bajra) for late monsoon; justification.

  8. Explain the botanical classification of the plant kingdom into Thallophyta, Bryophyta, Pteridophyta and Spermatophyta, giving the agricultural importance of each division. (8 marks · BTL-2 Understand · CO2)

    Answer points: Features of each; algae (BGA), fungi (mushrooms, Trichoderma, pathogens), lichens; mosses (Sphagnum); ferns (Azolla); gymnosperms vs angiosperms; crop examples.

  9. Classify the plant kingdom into four divisions and describe the features and examples of each, with their agricultural significance. (8 marks · BTL-2 · CO2)

    Answer points: Theophrastus 370 BC, Linnaeus; Thallophyta (algae, bacteria, fungi – N-fixers, smut, rust); Bryophyta (mosses, liverworts, hornworts); Pteridophyta (ferns, club moss, horsetail, Azolla); Spermatophyta – gymnosperms (naked seed, pine) and angiosperms (seed in ovary; monocots, dicots).

  10. “A single crop can belong to several classes simultaneously.” Illustrate this statement using maize, soybean and chickpea, and explain how each class guides a different management decision. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Season, use, family, photoperiod, CO2 pathway, root system; decisions – sowing date, nutrient (N) management, rotation, water, spacing.

  11. Evaluate the role of millets as ‘climate-smart crops’ for Indian dryland agriculture. Classify millets and compare their agronomic and nutritional advantages with those of rice and wheat. (10 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Major and minor millets; C4, drought tolerance, short duration, low input; nutrient density (Fe, Ca, fibre); limitations – yield, processing, market; policy (Shree Anna).

  12. Differentiate between gymnosperms and angiosperms, and between monocots and dicots, with crop examples. Why is this distinction important for weed control and crop management? (10 marks · BTL-4 Analyse · CO2)

    Answer points: Tables of differences; selective herbicides (2,4-D kills dicot weeds in cereals), root systems and rotation, nutrient uptake, tillage.

  13. Critically compare C3, C4 and CAM pathways of CO2 fixation. Explain why C4 crops dominate the hot semi-arid regions of India, and predict how rising temperature and CO2 may change this advantage. (12 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Biochemistry, enzymes, anatomy, photorespiration, temperature optima, WUE; C4 advantage at high temperature and light; elevated CO2 favours C3 (reduced photorespiration) but heat favours C4; implications.

  14. Critically evaluate the various systems of crop classification (ontogenic, agronomic, botanical, seasonal and climatic). Which system is most useful for an agricultural engineer planning farm machinery and irrigation, and why? (12 marks · BTL-5 Evaluate · CO2)

    Answer points: Basis, merits and limits of each; overlap; engineer needs – season and life span (machinery calendar, irrigation scheduling), agronomic use (processing), botanical (root system, plant geometry); justified choice.

complete life history of a plant from germination to death

life cycle of one season / two years / more than two years

small-seeded hardy cereal; major – jowar, bajra, ragi

legume grown for edible seed (dal)

non-grass plant with grain-like seeds, e.g. buckwheat, amaranth
