The sky is the farmer's biggest partner

No farmer can control the sky – but a good agronomist plans around it. Temperature, rainfall, light, humidity and wind decide when a seed germinates, how fast a crop grows, when it flowers and how much grain it fills.

4.1 Weather and climate

Weather is the state of the atmosphere at a place for a short time (hours–days). Climate is the average weather over a long period (30 years or more). Crop planning is based on climate; daily operations are based on weather.

4.2 Temperature

  • Every crop has cardinal temperatures – minimum, optimum and maximum.
  • Wheat: optimum ≈ 20–25 °C for growth; temperatures above 30 °C at grain filling cause terminal heat stress and shrivelled grain.
  • Rice: 20–35 °C; below 20 °C at flowering causes spikelet sterility.
  • Maize and sugarcane (C4) tolerate higher temperatures (30–35 °C).
  • Growing degree days (GDD) = Σ [(Tmax + Tmin)/2 − Tbase] – used to predict crop stages.
  • Frost (≤ 0 °C) kills tissues of chickpea, mustard and potato in north India.

4.3 Rainfall and humidity

  • India receives about 75 % of its rain from the south-west monsoon (June–September).
  • Too little rain → drought, wilting, poor grain set; too much → waterlogging, nutrient loss, diseases.
  • Dry spells at critical stages (flowering, grain filling) cause the largest losses.
  • High relative humidity favours diseases such as rice blast, late blight and grape downy mildew.

4.4 Light – intensity, quality and duration

  • Intensity drives photosynthesis; cloudy weather lowers rice and cotton yield.
  • Quality – red and blue light are used most in photosynthesis.
  • Duration (photoperiod) controls flowering: short-day crops (rice, soybean, jute) flower as days shorten; long-day crops (wheat, barley, oats) flower as days lengthen.

4.5 Wind and other parameters

  • Moderate wind helps pollination (maize) and CO2 supply; strong wind causes lodging (wheat, sugarcane, banana).
  • Hot dry winds (‘loo’) increase evapotranspiration and flower drop.
  • Hail damages leaves, flowers and fruits (grapes, onion in Maharashtra).
  • Climate change: higher temperature, erratic rain and extreme events are reducing yields – climate-resilient varieties and contingency plans are needed.

Explained simply

A crop is like a student preparing for an exam – temperature sets the speed, water is the food, light is the energy, and a shock at exam time (heat or drought at flowering) costs the most marks.

Facts & amazing information

A 1 °C rise in temperature during grain filling can reduce wheat yield by about 4–6 %.

Possible ESE questions

Short-answer questions

  1. Distinguish weather and climate. (2 marks)

    Answer points: Weather – short-term state of atmosphere; climate – long-term (≥30 years) average weather.

  2. What are growing degree days? (2 marks)

    Answer points: Cumulative heat units above a base temperature, used to predict crop phenology.

  3. Give one example each of short-day and long-day crops. (2 marks)

    Answer points: Short-day – rice/soybean; long-day – wheat/barley.

Long-answer questions

  1. Explain the effect of temperature on crop growth and development. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Cardinal temperatures; examples; heat stress, cold injury, frost; GDD; vernalisation; effect on phenology and yield.

  2. Discuss the role of light and photoperiod in crop production. (5 marks)

    Answer points: Intensity, quality, duration; photoperiodic classes with examples; practical implications for sowing dates.

  3. Describe the effect of weather parameters – rainfall, humidity and wind – on crops, and suggest management practices. (10 marks)

    Answer points: Drought, waterlogging, disease, lodging, hail; contingency planning, moisture conservation, drainage, windbreaks, insurance.
