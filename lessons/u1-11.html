Plants are chemists that work with only 17 ingredients

A sugarcane plant can grow 3 metres tall and produce sweet juice, fibre and leaves – all from sunlight, air, water and just a handful of chemical elements taken from the soil. Scientists have found that plants need exactly 17 elements to complete their life. Remove any one, and the plant cannot complete its life cycle, however much of the others you supply. This lesson is about those 17 essential nutrients.

11.1 Introduction

Plants need chemical elements to build their bodies and run their processes. They take them mostly from the soil (and air and water) as ions. Only a tiny fraction (< 0.2 %) of soil mineral nutrients is dissolved in soil water; about 98 % is locked in organic matter, humus, insoluble compounds and minerals (slow reserve), and about 2 % is adsorbed on colloids. These pools stay in dynamic equilibrium, so as roots remove nutrients from the solution, colloids and reserves refill it.

Liebig's barrel

Imagine a wooden barrel made of staves of different heights, each stave being one nutrient. Water can only fill up to the shortest stave. In the same way, crop yield is limited by the nutrient in shortest supply – Liebig's Law of the Minimum. Adding more nitrogen will not help if zinc is the short stave.

11.2 Criteria of essentiality (Arnon and Stout, 1939; Arnon 1954)

  • The plant cannot complete its life cycle (grow and reproduce normally) without the element.

  • The element is specific – no other element can replace it.

  • The element has a direct role in plant metabolism (not just correcting some bad soil condition).

Because this is strict, Nicholas proposed “functional or metabolic nutrients”. Elements like Na, Co, V, Si, Se, Al, I that help some plants are called beneficial elements (their essentiality is not proven for all plants; they help at very low concentrations).

Three questions to test an element

Arnon and Stout asked three simple questions: (1) Can the plant complete its life without it? If not – (2) Can any other element do its job? If not – (3) Is it directly involved in the plant's own metabolism? If yes to all, the element is essential. Sodium helps some plants but fails these tests for most crops, so it is called beneficial, not essential.

11.3 Classification of essential nutrients

17 essential elements (classically) – C, H, O, N, P, K, Ca, Mg, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni. (Co is needed for N-fixing legumes; some lists therefore say 18.)

Group Nutrients Mainly from Typical plant concentration
Structural (non-mineral) C, H, O Air (CO₂) and water ~ 90 % of dry matter (45 % C, 45 % O, 6 % H)
Primary macronutrients N, P, K Soil N 1.5 %, P 0.2 %, K 1.0 %
Secondary macronutrients Ca, Mg, S Soil Ca 0.5 %, Mg 0.2 %, S 0.1 %
Micronutrients (trace) Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl, Ni, Co Soil Fe 100, Mn 20, Zn 20, B 20, Cu 6, Cl 100, Mo 0.1 mg/kg

  • Metals: K, Ca, Mg, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu (Mo, Ni). Non-metals: N, P, S, B, Cl.

  • Absorbed as cations: NH₄⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Fe²⁺, Mn²⁺, Zn²⁺, Cu²⁺. As anions: NO₃⁻, H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻, SO₄²⁻, BO₃³⁻ / H₃BO₃, MoO₄²⁻, Cl⁻.

Macro vs micro does not mean important vs unimportant

Macronutrients are needed in large amounts (kilograms per hectare); micronutrients in tiny amounts (grams per hectare). But both are equally essential. A crop short of a few grams of zinc can fail just as badly as one short of nitrogen. Think of salt in cooking: you need only a pinch, but without it the meal is ruined.

Where the 17 come from

C, H and O come free from air and water (about 95 % of the dry weight of a plant!). The other 14 come from the soil – and these are the ones farmers manage with fertilizers and manures.

Mobility of nutrients

Mobile Less mobile Immobile
In soil NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, borate, Cl⁻, Mn NH₄⁺, K⁺, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, Cu²⁺ H₂PO₄⁻/HPO₄²⁻, Zn²⁺
In plant (phloem) N, P, K, Mg (highly mobile) Zn (moderate); S, Fe, Mn, Cu, Mo, Cl (less) Ca, B (immobile)
Why does mobility in the plant matter? Mobile nutrients move from old leaves to new growth, so deficiency shows on OLD (lower) leaves (N, P, K, Mg). Immobile nutrients stay put, so deficiency shows on YOUNG (upper) leaves and growing tips (Ca, S, Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B).

11.4 Forms of nutrients in soil

Nutrient Forms in soil
Nitrogen Organic N (~97 %) and mineral N (NH₄⁺, NO₃⁻)
Phosphorus Solution P, Ca-, Fe-, Al-phosphates, occluded P, organic P (25–90 %), mineral P
Potassium Water-soluble, exchangeable, fixed (non-exchangeable) and mineral K (90–98 % of total)

Micronutrients Water-soluble, exchangeable, adsorbed, chelated/complexed, held in secondary clay, in insoluble oxides, in primary minerals

Explained simply – three pockets of money

Nutrients in soil are like a person's money: cash in hand (soil solution – immediately available), savings account (exchangeable ions on clay and humus – available soon) and fixed deposit / property (minerals and organic matter – released slowly over months or years). Crops spend the cash first; the soil keeps refilling it from the savings and deposits.

11.5 How nutrients reach the root

  • Mass flow: nutrient ions move along with water that the plant is drawing in (important for NO₃⁻, SO₄²⁻, Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺).

  • Diffusion: ions move from high to low concentration towards the root surface that has depleted them (key for P and K).

  • Root interception / contact exchange: roots physically grow into contact with colloids and directly exchange ions (small contribution).

Three delivery routes

Root interception – the root grows to the nutrient (like walking to the shop). Mass flow – nutrients come dissolved in the water the plant is drinking (like a delivery van on a river). Diffusion – nutrients move slowly from a high to a low concentration near the root (like the smell of food spreading in a room). Nitrate moves easily by mass flow; phosphorus and potassium depend mostly on diffusion, which is why P fertilizer should be placed near the root zone.

Hidden hunger: mild deficiency may show no visible symptoms yet still cut yields noticeably. It is detected only by plant tissue analysis or yield decline.

Reading the leaves in the field

  • Yellowing of older lower leaves starting from the tip = nitrogen deficiency (common in maize and rice).
  • Purple or reddish older leaves = phosphorus deficiency (often seen in young maize in cold weather).
  • Scorched, brown leaf margins on older leaves = potassium deficiency.
  • Yellow young leaves with green veins = iron deficiency (common on calcareous black soils, e.g., in sugarcane and groundnut).
  • Small leaves, short internodes, "khaira" of rice = zinc deficiency.

Remember the mobility rule from the diagram: mobile nutrients show symptoms on old leaves; immobile ones on young leaves.

Correcting deficiencies

Nutrient Corrective measure
N (Nitrogen) Soil application of nitrogen fertilizers (urea, etc.); foliar spray of urea (1–2%).
P (Phosphorus) Soil application of diammonium phosphate (DAP) or single superphosphate (SSP).
K (Potassium) Soil application of muriate of potash (MOP).
Ca (Calcium) Application of lime or calcium carbonate in acidic soils; gypsum where appropriate, especially in sodic soils or where calcium and sulphur are required.
Mg (Magnesium) Application of magnesium sulphate to the soil or as a foliar spray.
S (Sulphur) Application of elemental sulphur, gypsum or sulphate-containing fertilizers.
Fe (Iron) Application of ferrous sulphate to the soil or as a foliar spray; iron chelates may be used in calcareous soils.
Mn (Manganese) Application of manganese sulphate to the soil or as a foliar spray, as recommended.
Zn (Zinc) Application of zinc sulphate to the soil (commonly around 25 kg/ha, depending on the crop and recommendation) or foliar spray (e.g., 0.5%).
B (Boron) Application of borax or boric acid at the recommended dose; avoid excess application because boron has a narrow range between deficiency and toxicity.
Cu (Copper) Application of copper sulphate at the recommended dose through soil or foliar application, as appropriate.
Mo (Molybdenum) Application of sodium molybdate or ammonium molybdate at the recommended dose; seed treatment may also be used in suitable crops.
Cl (Chlorine) Chloride-containing fertilizers, such as potassium chloride (MOP), where deficiency is confirmed; deficiency is rare under field conditions.
Ni (Nickel) Application of a suitable nickel source, such as nickel sulphate, in very small, recommended quantities when deficiency is confirmed.
Co (Cobalt) Application of cobalt sulphate or another suitable cobalt source at the recommended dose, particularly for legumes requiring effective biological nitrogen fixation.

Also: add organic manure as per recommendation. Fertilizer or foliar application both work.

Nutrient toxicity

Toxicities are most frequent for Mn and B. Excess N gives very dark green, excessive vegetative growth and delayed maturity; excess P induces Zn/Fe deficiency; excess K induces Mg/Ca deficiency; excess Fe/Mn in acid or waterlogged soils gives brown spots; excess B/Cl from irrigation water gives leaf-tip and margin burn.

  • Prevention: (1) liming reduces toxicity of most nutrients (except Mo); (2) follow recommended fertilizer rates and use waste materials (sewage sludge, fly ash) carefully; (3) use tolerant crop species; (4) provide drainage (Fe and Mn become toxic in waterlogged soils); (5) monitor groundwater for B and Cl; dilute if high; (6) add organic matter to bind toxic elements; (7) plough in dry soil and leach with rainwater.

Try it yourself

Leaf survey: walk through a crop field or garden and photograph leaves showing yellowing, purple colour or burnt edges. Note whether the symptom is on old or young leaves and use the mobility rule to guess the deficient nutrient. Ask your teacher to confirm.

Check your understanding

Think of your answer first, then tap a question to see it.

Q1. How many elements are essential for plants? Name the micronutrients.

17; Fe, Mn, Zn, Cu, B, Mo, Cl and Ni.

Q2. State Liebig's Law of the Minimum.

Growth is controlled by the nutrient present in the least relative amount.

Q3. Why does N deficiency appear on older leaves first?

N is mobile in the plant and is moved from old leaves to young growing parts.

Q4. By which mechanism do P and K mainly reach roots?

Diffusion.

Q5. Is sodium essential for most crops?

No – it is beneficial for some plants but does not satisfy the criteria of essentiality for most crops.
