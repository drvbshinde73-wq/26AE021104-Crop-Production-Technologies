The cheapest three decisions in farming

If seed is sown too deep, too thick or untreated, no amount of fertilizer later can rescue the crop. Method of sowing, seed rate and seed treatment cost little but decide the plant stand – and the plant stand decides the yield.

14.1 Methods of sowing

Seed and sowing

TermMeaning
SeedPlants propagate by seed (sexual) or by vegetative parts (asexual). A seed is a ripe, fertilised ovule – the unit of reproduction of flowering plants. Two types: monocotyledon (one cotyledon, e.g. maize) and dicotyledon (two cotyledons, e.g. bean, gram).
SowingPlacing seeds in the soil so that they germinate well in the field. Good sowing gets the seed rate, depth and spacing right.

A perfect sowing gets three things right

  • Seed rate – the correct amount of seed per unit area.
  • Depth – seed placed at the correct depth in the soil.
  • Spacing – correct row-to-row and plant-to-plant distance.

Why depth matters: too shallow and the seed is blown away by wind or eaten by birds and animals; too deep and it does not germinate.

Sowing is placing seed in the soil at the proper depth and spacing so that it germinates and establishes a healthy plant stand. The method affects plant population, uniformity, weed control and the possibility of mechanisation.

MethodHow it is doneAdvantagesLimitations / crops

DibblingSeed placed in holes made with a dibbler or stick at fixed spacingLow seed rate, uniform standLabour-intensive; cotton, groundnut, castor, maize, vegetables
Drilling / line sowingSeed dropped continuously in rows by seed drill or seed-cum-fertilizer drillUniform depth, inter-cultivation possible, fertilizer placementNeeds implement; wheat, soybean, chickpea, jowar

Depth of sowing: as a rule of thumb, sow seed at a depth of 3–4 times its diameter – small seeds (sesame, mustard) 2–3 cm, medium seeds (wheat, jowar) 4–5 cm, large seeds (groundnut, maize, chickpea) 5–7 cm. Seeds sown too deep exhaust their food reserve before emerging; seeds sown too shallow dry out.

Watch out

A high seed rate does not guarantee a high plant stand. Broadcasting usually needs 25–30 % more seed than drilling, yet gives a less uniform stand, because some seeds fall on the surface, some too deep and some clump together.

Methods of sowing in detail

Methods 1 to 6 sow seed directly in the field; transplanting moves nursery seedlings to the field.

Method 1: Broadcasting

Scattering seeds over the field, also called random sowing, by hand or by machine; the seeds are covered by planking or shallow ploughing. Best for small seeds. Examples: mustard, sesame, linseed, fodder oats, fenugreek.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easy and cheap; saves time; no skilled labour; suits small seedsUneven spacing; seeds left uncovered; poor germination and lodging; higher seed rate

Method 2: Dibbling

Seeds are placed in holes made at a fixed depth and spacing with a dibbler (a conical tool), then covered with soil. Mostly used for vegetables because it is slow. Examples: okra, pumpkin, maize, groundnut, castor, cotton.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Uniform plant stand; better germination; less seed neededVery time-consuming; costly; not for small seeds

Method 3: Drilling

Seeds drop in a continuous flow into furrows made by a seed drill and are covered at once. Drills can be bullock-drawn or tractor-drawn, and seed-cum-fertiliser drills place fertiliser too. Examples: wheat, chickpea, soybean, cotton, sunflower.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Saves a lot of time; controlled seed rate; rapid germination; seed and fertiliser togetherMachine is costly; needs technical skill

Method 4: Sowing behind the plough

The conventional village method: a person drops seed into the furrow made by the plough, so sowing depth follows the plough depth. Examples: maize, gram, pea, wheat, barley.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Easy and low cost; proper spacing and seed rate possibleSlow; needs more labour

Method 5: Hill dropping and Method 6: Check-row planting

  • Hill dropping: seeds are dropped at fixed spots (“hills”), not in a continuous stream. Plant-to-plant spacing in a row stays constant; in a drill, spacing varies.
  • Check-row planting: row-to-row and plant-to-plant distances are equal; rows run in two perpendicular directions, using a check-row planter, so inter-cultivation is possible in both directions.
  • Examples: okra, maize, cowpea, marigold, red gram, groundnut, castor.

Method 7: Transplanting

Seedlings are raised in a nursery, then planted in the prepared main field. Common for vegetables, flowers and rice; a transplanter can be used.

AdvantagesDisadvantages
Optimum plant population; shorter main-field duration; crop intensificationNursery raising is expensive; laborious and costly

Germination

Germination is the process by which a seed sprouts and grows into a seedling.

  1. Favourable conditions – moist, warm soil at the right depth.
  2. Water imbibition – the seed absorbs water through the micropyle, swells and splits open.
  3. Root and shoot form – the radicle grows down and the plumule grows up.
  4. Seedling is formed – cotyledons unfold, first leaves appear and photosynthesis begins.

Factors affecting germination

FactorEffect
SoilType, texture, structure and microbes all influence germination
MoistureToo little and the seed loses viability; too much rots the sprouts
TemperatureGermination rate falls above or below the optimum
LightRed light (660 nm) promotes germination; far-red (730 nm) inhibits it (phytochrome)
Seed fertilityOnly viable, healthy seed germinates well
Sowing methodDepth, spacing and soil cover decide how well seeds sprout

14.2 Seed rate and plant population

The seed rate is the quantity of seed sown per hectare to obtain the desired (optimum) plant population. It depends on spacing, test weight (1000-seed weight), germination percentage, purity and expected field losses.

Formula: Seed rate (kg/ha) = (10,000 m² ÷ area per plant m²) × test weight (g) × 100 × 100 ÷ (germination % × purity % × 1000 × 1000) × plants per hill.

Worked example

Soybean at 45 cm × 5 cm, 1 plant per hill, test weight 120 g, germination 80 %, purity 98 %.

Plants/ha = 10,000 ÷ (0.45 × 0.05) = 4,44,444. Seed weight for 4,44,444 seeds = 4,44,444 × 120 ÷ 1000 g = 53.3 kg. Correcting for germination and purity: 53.3 ÷ (0.80 × 0.98) ≈ 68 kg/ha. The recommended rate of 65–75 kg/ha matches this calculation.

14.3 Seed treatment

Seed treatment is the application of fungicides, insecticides, bio-agents, bio-fertilizers or nutrients to seed before sowing to protect it from soil- and seed-borne pests and diseases and to improve germination and early vigour. It is the cheapest plant-protection measure – a few rupees per kilogram of seed.

TypePurposeExample
FungicidalControl seed- and soil-borne fungi (wilt, root rot, smut)Carbendazim 2 g/kg; thiram 3 g/kg; carboxin + thiram 2–3 g/kg
Bio-agentEco-friendly control of soil-borne pathogensTrichoderma viride/harzianum 4–10 g/kg
InsecticidalProtect from termites, shoot fly, white grub, sucking pestsImidacloprid 48 FS or thiamethoxam 30 FS as per label
Bio-fertilizerIncrease N fixation or P availabilityRhizobium (legumes), Azotobacter / Azospirillum (cereals), PSB – 25 g/kg or 250 g per 10 kg
Hardening / primingFaster, uniform germination and drought toleranceSoaking in water or dilute salts then drying (e.g. jowar in 2 % KH2PO4)
PhysicalKill internal pathogensHot-water treatment of sugarcane setts (50 °C, 2 h); solar heat for wheat loose smut
Order of treatment – FIR: Fungicide first → Insecticide next → Rhizobium / bio-fertilizer last, just before sowing. Chemicals can kill the living bacteria, so bio-fertilizer goes last and treated seed is dried in shade, not in sunlight.

In the field

Smallholders treat seed in an earthen pot or a seed-treating drum: seed and chemical are rotated for 10 minutes so every seed is coated. For Rhizobium, a slurry of 10 % jaggery solution is used as a sticker. Treated seed must never be fed to animals or people.

Seed treatment – as taught in class

Seed treatment is applying chemicals, biological agents or physical processes to seed before sowing, to improve germination and to protect the seed and the young seedling. Objectives: improve germination and seedling vigour; protect against seed-borne and soil-borne diseases and early pests; add helpful microbes such as Rhizobium.

Type of seed treatmentMethods / agents
To improve germinationSeed priming, pre-soaking, hardening, pelleting and coating
To protect the plantChemical: fungicide, insecticide; biological: Trichoderma, Pseudomonas; physical: hot water
To add microbesRhizobium (legumes), Azotobacter, phosphate-solubilising bacteria (PSB)

Seed priming

Seed is soaked just enough to start the germination process, but not long enough for the root to emerge, then dried back.

  1. Soak – in water or a salt solution, e.g. 2–4 % CaCl₂.
  2. Hold – for a set time, often 12–24 h.
  3. Dry back – surface-dry the seed in shade.
  4. Sow – soon after drying, or store.

Types: hydropriming (water), osmopriming (salt solution), solid-matrix priming (moist vermiculite). Time and temperature matter – in an okra study, 24 h at 20 °C gave the best germination.

Other treatments that help germinationHow

ScarificationScratching or nicking a hard seed coat so that water can enter

Methods of application

MethodHow it is done
Dry dressingMix seed with powder in a seed-dresser drum, earthen pot or polythene bag; shake until every seed is coated. Most common on farms.
Wet treatmentSoak seed in a chemical or nutrient solution for a set time, then drain and dry in shade.
Slurry treatmentCoat seed with a thin paste of chemical and a little water, then dry in shade. Rice seedling roots are dipped in phosphate slurry.

Procedure

  1. Clean and grade – remove broken, shrivelled and diseased seed.
  2. Measure – weigh the seed and the exact dose of chemical or culture.
  3. Load container – use a seed-dresser drum, earthen pot or polythene bag.
  4. Mix until coated – shake or rotate until all seed is evenly coated.
  5. Dry in shade – spread wet-treated seed on a clean sheet in shade.
  6. Sow on time – sow at the advised time and label the treated seed.

Using bioagents and cultures

Treat with the bioagent first (e.g. Trichoderma viride, talc formulation, about 4 g/kg seed), then add the culture (Rhizobium or PSB, about 5 g/kg seed; follow the packet). Never mix fungicides with bioagents – fungicides kill the living bioagent, so the two are incompatible; bioagents are compatible with biofertilisers. Keep treated seed in shade – strong sun and heat kill the living organisms on the seed.

Try it yourself

Count 100 seeds of chickpea or soybean, place them between two layers of wet paper towel, roll it and keep it moist for 5–7 days. Count the healthy seedlings – that is your germination percentage. Now use it in the seed-rate formula to calculate how much seed you need for one hectare.
