How to use these answers

Three questions on the history of Indian agriculture, agronomy and the present status of field crops (Unit II). Answers include an animated timeline, tables, bar charts and photographs.

Q1. Write in detail the history of Indian agriculture.

Indian agriculture is one of the oldest continuous farming traditions in the world. Its history is best reconstructed from archaeobotanical evidence (charred grains, phytoliths, impressions), ancient texts and, for the modern period, official statistics.

7000 BCMehrgarh

Earliest farming in South Asia – wheat, barley, cattle (Baluchistan).

2500 BCIndus–Saraswati civilisation

Wheat, barley, cotton, sesame; ploughed field at Kalibangan; granaries at Harappa.

1500–500 BCVedic age

Plough (sira), manuring, irrigation wells, rice; Rigveda and Atharvaveda mention farming.

300 BCMauryan period

Kautilya's Arthashastra – crop seasons, seed treatment, irrigation tax, Sitadhyaksha (superintendent of agriculture).

400–1100 ADClassical texts

Krishi-Parashara and Vrikshayurveda (Surapala) – weather, seeds, plant protection, grafting.

1200–1700 ADMedieval period

Persian wheel, canals (Firoz Shah Tughlaq), new crops – maize, chilli, tobacco, potato, groundnut.

1757–1947British period

Cash crops (indigo, cotton, opium), famines, Pusa institute 1905, ICAR 1929, Royal Commission on Agriculture 1928.

1965–1980Green Revolution

HYV wheat and rice, fertilizer, irrigation – India becomes self-sufficient in food.

1970–2000Other revolutions

White (milk – Operation Flood), Blue (fish), Yellow (oilseeds), Golden (horticulture).

2000–todayModern agriculture

Bt cotton (2002), Soil Health Card (2015), PM-KISAN, drones, digital agriculture, natural farming.

Analysis: wheat output nearly doubled in the six years after high-yielding semi-dwarf varieties were introduced (12.3 Mt in 1964–65 to 26.4 Mt in 1971–72), the decisive phase of the Green Revolution. Between 1950–51 and 2020–21 foodgrain production grew about six times while net sown area grew only about 20 %, showing that growth came mainly from yield (varieties, fertilizer – from under 0.1 Mt to about 30 Mt of NPK – and irrigation).

G. Analysis of the present status

  • Maharashtra's actual rank among Indian states in 2023–24: 2nd in soybean, sugarcane, cotton, tur, gram and total pulses; 4th in total oilseeds; 6th in groundnut; 8th in wheat and maize; 9th in total foodgrains; and 13th in rice. In 2024–25 it rose to 4th in maize and 3rd in oilseeds.
  • Maharashtra ranks second in India in soybean (40 % of national output), sugarcane, cotton, tur, gram and total pulses (2023–24), making it the country's leading state for commercial and pulse crops of the black soils.
  • The state's foodgrain productivity (1,368 kg/ha in 2023–24) is little more than half of the national average (2,515 kg/ha); its cereal productivity is the lowest among the fourteen major states, mainly because of rainfed jowar, bajra and wheat on shallow and medium soils.
  • Sugarcane productivity (87 t/ha triennial average) is fourth among major states, while cotton productivity (326 kg/ha) is twelfth of thirteen – the main yield gap to close.
  • Crop geography is sharply regional: rice in east Vidarbha and Konkan (Gondia, Bhandara, Gadchiroli, Chandrapur); cotton in Yavatmal and Jalgaon; soybean and gram in Latur, Nanded, Dharashiv and Buldhana; sugarcane in Solapur, Kolhapur, Sangli and Pune; rabi jowar in Solapur, Beed and Dharashiv; maize in Nashik, Chh. Sambhajinagar and Jalgaon.
  • District yields in 2025–26 vary widely – soybean from about 270 kg/ha in Nanded and 430 kg/ha in Dharashiv to over 1,400 kg/ha in Buldhana and Beed – showing how strongly weather in a single season drives output in rainfed Marathwada.
  • Priorities: protective irrigation and micro-irrigation, better seed and timely sowing, integrated pest management in cotton, crop diversification towards pulses and millets, and stronger market and processing links.
