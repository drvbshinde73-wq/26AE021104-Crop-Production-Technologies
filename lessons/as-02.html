Q1. Define ion and give the classification of ions in detail.

An ion is an atom or group of atoms that carries an electrical charge because it has lost or gained one or more electrons. Loss of electrons gives a positive ion; gain gives a negative ion. The term was coined by Michael Faraday (1834) from the Greek ion = ‘going’, because ions move towards electrodes.

Lyotropic (replacing-power) series of cations on colloids: Al³⁺ > H⁺ > Ca²⁺ > Mg²⁺ > K⁺ = NH₄⁺ > Na⁺ – higher charge and smaller hydrated size are held more strongly. Anion adsorption: phosphate > sulphate > nitrate = chloride – which is why nitrate leaches easily but phosphate is ‘fixed’.

Explained simply

Plants ‘eat’ ions, not whole fertilizer granules. Urea becomes NH₄⁺ and then NO₃⁻; DAP gives NH₄⁺ and H₂PO₄⁻; MOP gives K⁺ and Cl⁻.

Q2. Describe the factors affecting soil reaction.

Soil reaction is the degree of acidity or alkalinity of soil, expressed as pH. It depends on the balance between acidic (H⁺, Al³⁺) and basic (Ca²⁺, Mg²⁺, K⁺, Na⁺) ions. The main factors are:

FactorHow it affects soil reaction
1. Parent materialAcidic rocks (granite, sandstone, rich in silica) give acid soils; basic rocks (basalt, limestone) give neutral to alkaline soils – Deccan basalt → black soils of pH 7.5–8.5
2. Rainfall and leachingHigh rainfall leaches Ca, Mg, K, Na and leaves H⁺ and Al³⁺ → acid soils (Konkan laterite, pH 4.5–6). Low rainfall – bases accumulate → alkaline/saline soils
3. Temperature / climateWarm humid climate speeds weathering and leaching (acidity); arid climate causes salt accumulation (alkalinity)
4. Organic matter decompositionProduces CO₂ (carbonic acid) and organic acids (humic, fulvic) – lowers pH; humus also buffers pH
5. VegetationConifer and tea litter make soil acid; grassland soils are near neutral; legumes acidify slightly by taking up more cations
6. FertilizersAmmoniacal fertilizers (ammonium sulphate, urea) acidify through nitrification: NH₄⁺ + 2O₂ → NO₃⁻ + 2H⁺ + H₂O; basic slag, CAN and sodium nitrate raise pH
7. Irrigation water qualityWater rich in Na⁺, CO₃²⁻ and HCO₃⁻ (high RSC) makes soil sodic/alkaline (pH > 8.5)
8. Topography and drainageLow-lying, poorly drained areas collect salts and become saline–alkaline; well-drained slopes leach and become acidic
9. Submergence / waterloggingFlooding brings pH of acid and alkaline soils towards neutral (6.5–7.0) through reduction reactions (rice fields)
10. Soil colloids and bufferingClayey, humus-rich soils with high CEC resist pH change; sandy soils change quickly
11. Exchangeable cationsHigh base saturation → neutral/alkaline; high H⁺ and Al³⁺ → acid; Na⁺ saturation (ESP > 15) → pH above 8.5
12. Biological activity & CO₂Root and microbial respiration raise CO₂ partial pressure, forming H₂CO₃ that lowers pH
13. Pollution and acid rainSO₂ and NOₓ form H₂SO₄ and HNO₃; oxidation of pyrite forms acid sulphate soils
14. AmendmentsLime (CaCO₃) raises pH of acid soils; gypsum, sulphur and pyrite lower pH of sodic soils
15. Season and soil depthpH is usually lower in the wet season and in the surface layer; subsoils of arid regions are more alkaline (CaCO₃ accumulation)

✍️ Exam-writing tip

Write each factor as a sub-heading with a one-line mechanism and an example. A neat flow – ‘rain → leaching of bases → H⁺ and Al³⁺ remain → acid soil’ – earns full marks.

Q3. Define soil colloid and give the sources of soil colloids with examples.

A colloid is a substance divided into particles of size about 1 nm to 1 µm (0.001–1 µm) dispersed in another medium. Soil colloids are the most active, finest fraction of the soil – the clay (< 2 µm, mainly < 1 µm) and humus particles – that carry an electric charge and a very large surface area. The term was introduced by Thomas Graham (1861) from Greek kolla = glue.

Properties: very small size, huge surface area (up to 800 m²/g), electrical charge (mostly negative), adsorption and cation exchange, Brownian movement, flocculation and dispersion, plasticity, cohesion, swelling and shrinkage, and the Tyndall effect.

Q4. Define soil pH and give the different classes of soil based on soil pH.

Soil pH is the negative logarithm (base 10) of the hydrogen-ion activity of the soil solution: pH = −log₁₀ [H⁺] = log (1/[H⁺]). The term was introduced by S. P. L. Sørensen (1909). Soil pH is measured in a 1 : 2.5 soil : water suspension with a glass-electrode pH meter. Because the scale is logarithmic, soil at pH 5 is 10 times more acidic than at pH 6 and 100 times more than at pH 7.

Practical (agronomic) grouping: acid soils (pH < 6.5) · neutral (6.5–7.5) · alkaline/calcareous (7.5–8.5) · saline (EC > 4 dS/m, pH < 8.5) · sodic (pH > 8.5, ESP > 15) · saline-sodic (EC > 4, ESP > 15).

Explained simply

Significance: pH controls nutrient availability (P is most available at 6.5–7.5), microbial activity, toxicity of Al and Mn in acid soils, and the choice of crops, fertilizers and amendments.

Q5. Give the classification of soil for pre-Dokuchaev and post-Dokuchaev periods.

Soil classification developed in two clear phases, separated by the work of the Russian scientist V. V. Dokuchaev (1846–1903), the ‘father of soil science (pedology)’, who in 1883 showed that soil is an independent natural body formed by climate, organisms, parent rock, relief and time.

(a) Pre-Dokuchaev classification (before 1883) – soil seen as weathered rock or a medium for plants
