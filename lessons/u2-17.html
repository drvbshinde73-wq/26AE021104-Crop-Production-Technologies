Three grasses that feed half of humanity

Rice, wheat and maize together provide about half of all the calories eaten on Earth. All three are grasses of the family Poaceae, yet they behave very differently: rice loves standing water, wheat needs cool nights, and maize races ahead in heat. A package of practices is the step-by-step recipe – from choosing the variety to harvesting – that turns these differences into high yields.

Why these four crops?

Rice and wheat together supply most of India’s food grain; maize is the fastest-growing cereal (feed and ethanol); sugarcane is the economic backbone of rural Maharashtra. Each crop below is described in the same order so that you can compare them: identity → importance → statistics → soil and climate → land preparation → sowing → varieties → nutrients → water → weeds → flowering → intercultural operations → pests → diseases → disorders → harvest → post-harvest.

Important terms and operations explained

Tillage

What it is: Mechanical working of soil with implements to make a good seedbed. Primary tillage is the first, deep opening of soil (mould-board, disc or chisel plough, 15–30 cm); secondary tillage is the lighter work that follows (harrowing, cultivating, planking, levelling).

Why it is done / why it matters: Loosens soil for root growth, improves infiltration and aeration, buries crop residues and weeds, kills soil-borne pests by exposing them to sun, and gives a fine, firm seedbed for good seed–soil contact.

How and when: Primary tillage after harvest of the previous crop or in summer (summer ploughing); 2–3 harrowings and planking just before sowing. Minimum/zero tillage sows directly into stubble to save time, fuel and soil moisture (e.g. wheat after rice with a happy seeder).

Puddling

What it is: Ploughing and churning the soil in 5–10 cm of standing water until it becomes a soft, uniform mud; the fine clay particles settle and form a compact, almost impervious layer (hard pan) below the plough layer.

Why it is done / why it matters: (1) Reduces percolation and seepage losses of water by 50–70 %; (2) kills and buries weeds; (3) makes the soil soft so seedlings can be transplanted easily; (4) creates a reduced (anaerobic) condition that increases availability of P, Fe and Mn; (5) levels the field so water stands uniformly.

How and when: Flood the field 2–3 days before transplanting; puddle with a bullock-drawn puddler, power tiller or tractor cage-wheel/rotavator in 2–3 passes, then level with a wooden plank. Transplant within 1–2 days. Drawbacks: it destroys soil structure and the hard pan hampers the next rabi crop (wheat) – hence DSR and SRI are promoted.

Nursery and dapog (mat) nursery

What it is: A small, well-prepared area where seedlings are raised before transplanting. In dapog, pre-germinated seeds are spread thickly on banana leaves or polythene over a thin soil/compost layer, so the seedlings form a mat without soil.

Why it is done / why it matters: Gives uniform, healthy seedlings, saves main-field time (crop occupies field 25–30 days less), allows better care against pests, and saves seed. Dapog mats suit mechanical (machine) transplanting.

How and when: Wet raised-bed nursery: 1/10 of main-field area, sown end of May–June, seedlings ready in 21–25 days. Dapog: mats ready in 12–14 days; one ha needs about 40 m² of dapog bed.

Transplanting

What it is: Uprooting seedlings from the nursery and planting them in the puddled main field at a fixed spacing.

Why it is done / why it matters: Ensures optimum plant population and uniform stand, gives the crop a head start over weeds, and shortens main-field duration so that more crops can be grown in a year.

How and when: Uproot 21–25-day seedlings (4–5 leaves), plant 2–3 per hill, 2–3 cm deep, in rows (20 × 15 cm) by hand, by a rope/marker or by a paddy transplanter; fill gaps within a week.

Tillering

What it is: Production of side shoots (tillers) from the buds at the lower, compressed nodes of the main stem in grasses (rice, wheat, sugarcane). Productive (effective) tillers bear panicles/ears; unproductive ones die or remain vegetative.

Why it is done / why it matters: Number of productive tillers per m² is a major yield component of cereals; sugarcane tillers become millable canes.

How and when: Encouraged by wider spacing, shallow transplanting, adequate N at the tillering stage and enough moisture. Maximum tillering in rice is at 35–45 days; in wheat at 40–45 days.

Panicle initiation, booting and heading

What it is: Panicle initiation (PI): the growing point changes from producing leaves to producing the young panicle (about 60 days before harvest in rice). Booting: the swollen flag-leaf sheath encloses the developing panicle. Heading/earing: the panicle or ear emerges.

Why it is done / why it matters: These are reproductive stages that decide the number of grains per panicle; stress (water, N, heat) at PI and flowering causes heavy yield loss.

How and when: Top-dress N at PI; keep standing water at PI and flowering in rice; protect against stem borer and blast at heading.

Crown-root initiation (CRI)

What it is: In wheat, permanent crown (adventitious) roots develop from the crown nodes about 18–21 days after sowing.

Why it is done / why it matters: Crown roots form the main root system that absorbs water and nutrients for the whole season; if soil is dry at CRI they do not develop, tillering falls and yield drops by 20–30 %.

How and when: First irrigation and half of N top dressing are applied exactly at CRI (18–21 DAS).

Tasselling, silking, protandry and ASI

What it is: Tasselling: emergence of the male inflorescence (tassel) at the top of the maize plant. Silking: emergence of silks (styles) from the tip of the ear. Protandry: pollen is shed 2–3 days before the silks of the same plant are receptive. ASI (anthesis–silking interval): days between pollen shedding and silking.

Why it is done / why it matters: Protandry makes maize mainly cross-pollinated by wind; a long ASI (caused by drought) means silks emerge after pollen is over, giving barren or poorly filled cobs.

How and when: Tasselling at 50–60 DAS, silking 2–3 days later. Ensure irrigation and N two weeks before to two weeks after silking; detassel female rows in hybrid seed plots.

Earthing up

What it is: Heaping soil from between the rows around the base of plants.

Why it is done / why it matters: Gives support against lodging (maize, sugarcane), covers top-dressed fertiliser, controls weeds, improves aeration and drainage, and encourages more roots.

How and when: Maize at knee-high stage (30–35 DAS) with a ridger or spade; sugarcane – partial earthing at 6–8 weeks and full earthing at 12–16 weeks.

Sett and bud chip

What it is: A sett is a piece of sugarcane stem with 1, 2 or 3 buds (eyes) used for planting; a bud chip is a single bud scooped out with a small piece of stem.

Why it is done / why it matters: Sugarcane is vegetatively propagated because true seed (fuzz) does not breed true; setts from healthy 9–10-month nursery cane germinate best. Bud chips save about 80 % seed cane.

How and when: Cut setts with a sharp knife avoiding damage to buds, treat them (fungicide + insecticide, 10 minutes), plant end-to-end in furrows; bud chips are raised in portrays for 30–35 days before transplanting.

Gap filling and thinning

What it is: Gap filling: planting in places where seeds or seedlings failed. Thinning: removing extra seedlings so that only the desired number remain per hill.

Why it is done / why it matters: Maintains the optimum plant population – a key factor for yield.

How and when: Gap filling within 7–10 days (rice, maize) or 30 days (sugarcane) with spare seedlings or pre-sprouted setts; thinning in maize 10–12 DAS to one plant per hill.

Detrashing and trash mulching

What it is: Detrashing: removing dry and lower green leaves of sugarcane. Trash mulching: spreading this trash between rows.

Why it is done / why it matters: Detrashing reduces scale insects, mealybugs and woolly aphid, improves air movement and makes canes clean for harvest. Trash mulch conserves moisture, suppresses weeds and adds organic matter (never burn trash).

How and when: Detrash at 5 and 7 months; spread trash in alternate rows of ratoons and adsali crop.

Propping

What it is: Tying the canes of two or three rows together with their own dry leaves (or supporting with bamboo).

Why it is done / why it matters: Prevents lodging of tall, heavy canes, which otherwise causes rotting, rat damage, root formation on nodes and loss of sugar.

How and when: Done at 6–8 months in tall adsali and pre-seasonal crops, after full earthing up.

Ratoon and stubble shaving

What it is: A ratoon is the crop that regrows from underground buds of the stubble after harvest of the plant crop. Stubble shaving is cutting the stubble uniformly at ground level.

Why it is done / why it matters: Ratooning saves seed cane, land preparation and planting cost and matures earlier; shaving ensures sprouting from healthy underground buds.

How and when: Immediately after harvest: shave stubble, do off-barring, fill gaps, apply 250 : 115 : 115 kg NPK and irrigate within 2–3 days; keep 1–2 ratoons only.

Lodging

What it is: Bending or breaking of stems so that the crop falls on the ground.

Why it is done / why it matters: Reduces photosynthesis, grain filling and quality, causes rotting and makes harvesting difficult.

How and when: Avoided by balanced N, dwarf/strong-stemmed varieties, earthing up, propping and by not irrigating in strong wind.

Brix

What it is: Percentage of total soluble solids (mainly sucrose) in cane juice, read with a hand refractometer.

Why it is done / why it matters: Simple field index of sugarcane maturity – harvesting at full maturity gives maximum sugar recovery.

How and when: Cane is mature when Brix is 18–20 % or more and the top-to-bottom Brix ratio is 0.95–1.0.

Dead heart and white ear

What it is: Dead heart: the central shoot dries when a borer larva cuts the growing point (vegetative stage). White ear: the whole panicle turns white and empty when the borer cuts the stem after heading.

Why it is done / why it matters: Typical symptoms used to diagnose stem borers in rice, maize, wheat and sugarcane; the dead heart pulls out easily.

How and when: Count dead hearts/white ears per m² to decide control (ETL: 5 % dead hearts or 1 egg mass/m² in rice).

Threshing, winnowing, parboiling and milling

What it is: Threshing separates grain from panicles/ears; winnowing removes chaff using wind; parboiling is soaking and steaming paddy before milling; milling removes husk (hulling) and bran (polishing).

Why it is done / why it matters: Clean, dry grain stores well and fetches a better price; parboiling improves milling recovery and retains B-vitamins.

How and when: Thresh by beating, power thresher or combine; dry grain to 12–14 % moisture; milling recovery of rice is about 65–68 %.

Straw, stover and haulm

What it is: Dry plant residue left after removing the economic produce – straw (rice, wheat), stover (maize, jowar), haulm (pulses, groundnut, soybean).

Why it is done / why it matters: Important dry fodder for cattle, raw material for compost, mulch, mushroom beds and paper; burning residue causes air pollution and nutrient loss.

How and when: Bale, chaff or incorporate residues; use a happy seeder to sow through residues.

Note on recommendations

Doses, varieties and dates below are general recommendations of the state agricultural universities of Maharashtra (MPKV Rahuri, Dr PDKV Akola, VNMKV Parbhani, Dr BSKKV Dapoli) and ICAR institutes. Always follow the latest package of practices and the pesticide label for your district.

Field preparation, tillage and layout

  • Summer ploughing after the previous crop, then 2–3 harrowings.
  • Puddling in 5–10 cm standing water with a puddler or rotavator (2–3 passes) followed by levelling with a plank.
  • Layout: field divided into small level plots (bunds 15–20 cm high) for uniform water; in Konkan, rab (burning of leaf litter on the nursery bed) was traditional – now replaced by raised beds and organic manure.

Nursery

  • Raised (wet) beds 1 m wide, 15 cm high, convenient length; nursery area about 1/10 of the main field (about 1,000 m² per ha).
  • Seed rate: fine grain 30–40 kg/ha, coarse 40–50 kg/ha; hybrids 15–20 kg/ha.
  • Seed treatment: salt-water test (17 % NaCl) to remove light seed; carbendazim 2 g/kg or Trichoderma 5–10 g/kg; Azospirillum + PSB.
  • Apply FYM 1–2 kg/m², 1 kg N + 0.5 kg P₂O₅ per 100 m²; sow end of May–June.
  • Dapog (mat) nursery for machine transplanting: seedlings raised on polythene in 12–14 days.

Irrigation and water management

  • Keep 2–5 cm standing water; a thin film (2 cm) at transplanting and tillering.
  • Critical stages: tillering, panicle initiation, booting, flowering (most critical) and grain filling.
  • Alternate wetting and drying (AWD) – re-irrigate when water falls 15 cm below soil surface (field water tube); saves 20–30 % water and reduces methane.
  • Drain the field 10–15 days before harvest.

Weed management

  • Critical period: first 30–40 days after transplanting.
  • Hand weeding at 20 and 40 DAT, or rotary/cono-weeder in line-planted rice (also aerates soil).
  • Pre-emergence pretilachlor 0.75 kg a.i./ha (3–5 DAT); post-emergence bispyribac-sodium 25 g a.i./ha (15–20 DAT).
  • Puddling and standing water themselves suppress many weeds; common weeds: Echinochloa spp., Cyperus spp., Monochoria.

Flowering and pollination

Panicle emerges 80–110 days after sowing. Florets open between 9 a.m. and 1 p.m.; anthesis of a panicle takes 5–7 days. Rice is self-pollinated (cross-pollination less than 1 %). Temperature above 35 °C or heavy rain at anthesis causes spikelet sterility. Hybrid rice seed is produced with a CMS (A) line, a maintainer (B) and a restorer (R) line, using supplementary pollination by rope-pulling.

Intercultural operations

  • Gap filling within 10 days.
  • Rotary weeding twice; top dressing of N at tillering and PI.
  • Keep bunds clean (they harbour pests); maintain water level.
  • Roguing of off-types in seed plots.

Post-harvest management

  • Dry grain to 12–14 % moisture before storage.
  • Parboiling – soaking and steaming paddy before milling; improves milling recovery and nutritive value.
  • Milling: hulling (removes husk – brown rice) and polishing (removes bran – white rice); milling recovery about 65–68 %.
  • Store in moisture-proof bins; protect from rice weevil and lesser grain borer.

SRI – System of Rice Intensification

Young 8–12-day seedlings, one per hill at 25 × 25 cm, alternate wetting and drying, and mechanical weeding with a cono-weeder. SRI saves 30–40 % water and up to 80 % seed and often raises yield because roots stay healthy and tillers multiply.

If only limited water is available: one irrigation – at CRI; two – CRI and flowering; three – CRI, jointing and milk. Avoid irrigation in strong wind (causes lodging).

Weed management

  • Critical period: 30–45 days after sowing.
  • Grassy weeds (Phalaris minor, wild oat): clodinafop-propargyl 60 g a.i./ha or sulfosulfuron 25 g/ha at 30–35 DAS.
  • Broad-leaf weeds (bathua, Melilotus): metsulfuron-methyl 4 g/ha or 2,4-D 0.5 kg a.i./ha at 30–35 DAS.
  • One hand weeding at 25–30 DAS.

Flowering and pollination

Ear emerges about 70–85 days after sowing; anthesis starts in the middle of the ear and proceeds up and down. Wheat is self-pollinated and often cleistogamous (pollination before the florets open); cross-pollination is under 1–2 %.

Intercultural operations

  • Hand weeding/hoeing at 25–30 DAS.
  • Top dressing of N at CRI irrigation.
  • Rogue off-types and loose-smut-infected ears in seed plots before they shed spores.

Irrigation

  • Critical stages: knee-high, tasselling and silking (most critical) and grain filling.
  • Water stress 2 weeks before to 2 weeks after silking can cut yield by 40–50 %.
  • Rabi maize needs 6–8 irrigations; avoid standing water (maize is sensitive to waterlogging).

Weed management

  • Critical period: 15–45 DAS.
  • Pre-emergence atrazine 1.0 kg a.i./ha (0.75 kg on light soils) within 2 days of sowing.
  • Post-emergence tembotrione 120 g a.i./ha or topramezone at 20–25 DAS.
  • Hoeing at 20–25 DAS followed by earthing up.

Flowering and pollination

Tasselling occurs 50–60 days after sowing; pollen shedding lasts 5–8 days. Silking starts 2–3 days later (protandry) – so pollination is mainly cross (about 95 %), by wind. Each silk leads to one kernel; poor pollination gives cobs with missing kernels (barren tips). The gap between anthesis and silking (ASI) increases under drought.

Intercultural operations

  • Thinning and gap filling 10–12 DAS (one plant per hill).
  • Hoeing and earthing up at 30–35 DAS (knee-high) – prevents lodging and covers top-dressed N.
  • Detasselling in seed production plots (female rows).

  • Deep ploughing (30–40 cm) with a mould-board plough, cross ploughing and 2–3 harrowings; FYM incorporated.
  • Open ridges and furrows 100–120 cm apart (heavy soils 120–150 cm); wide or paired-row planting (75–150 cm) allows mechanisation and intercropping.
  • For drip irrigation: paired rows with laterals in between.

Irrigation

  • Needs 2,000–3,000 mm water over the season.
  • Phases: germination (0–45 days), tillering (45–120 days) and grand growth (120–270 days) – most critical; ripening (withhold irrigation 3–4 weeks before harvest).
  • Drip irrigation and fertigation save 40–50 % water and raise yield; Maharashtra has made drip compulsory for sugarcane in many command areas.

Weed management

  • Critical period: first 90–120 days.
  • Pre-emergence atrazine 2 kg a.i./ha or metribuzin 1 kg/ha; 2,4-D 1 kg/ha for broad-leaf weeds and nut grass (Cyperus).
  • Intercrops (onion, groundnut, soybean, cowpea) and trash mulching in ratoons suppress weeds; hoeing 3–4 times.

Flowering (arrowing) and pollination

Sugarcane flowers (arrowing) under short days in October–December; the inflorescence is an open panicle (arrow). Flowering stops cane growth and reduces sucrose (pith becomes hollow), so it is undesirable in commercial crops but essential for breeding – crosses are made at the Sugarcane Breeding Institute, Coimbatore (hence “Co” varieties). Seed (fuzz) is used only in breeding; commercial propagation is vegetative.

Intercultural operations

  • Gap filling with pre-sprouted setts or nursery seedlings within 30 days.
  • Partial earthing up at 6–8 weeks and full earthing up at 12–16 weeks – supports canes and covers fertiliser.
  • Detrashing of dry lower leaves at 5 and 7 months – reduces pests, improves aeration.
  • Propping (tying canes) in tall, lodging-prone crops; removal of water shoots.
  • Intercropping in the first 3–4 months: onion, garlic, potato, soybean, groundnut, cowpea.
